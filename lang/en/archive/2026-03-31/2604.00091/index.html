Dark Matter Ignites a Gamma Glow Around the Sun express

Original: "A Solar Probe of Dark Matter Decay in the Galaxy"
· Maximilian Detering, Shyam Balaji
arXiv:2604.00091 · 2026-03-31 · CC BY 4.0 · 1 min
High Energy HEP Phenomenology
Decay of dark matter near the Sun produces gamma rays that telescopes can capture.
Abstract

Decays of dark matter in the Galaxy produce fast electrons, which, colliding with sunlight, turn it into gamma rays (inverse Compton scattering). Solar radiation amplifies this effect billions of times compared to the interstellar background, turning the Sun into a powerful light converter. Analysis of 15 years of Fermi telescope data on the solar halo has placed strict limits: the lifetime of dark matter must exceed 10^27 seconds for masses 10 GeV – 10 TeV in leptonic decay channels. This approach opens a new local search method, independent of galactic surveys and particle measurements.

Dark matter is an invisible substance that keeps galaxies from flying apart. If its particles occasionally decay near the Sun, they shoot out streams of fast particles—like invisible hammers striking light. Each impact turns a particle of sunlight into a gamma ray—a super-powerful flash that telescopes can spot.

The Sun acts like a giant lamp: in the thick of its light, collisions happen especially often, boosting the signal.

Analysis of 15 years of Fermi telescope observations found no such gamma glow. This means dark matter particles live at least 10²⁷ seconds—billions of times longer than the age of the universe. It might seem like a lack of result is a failure, but in reality it rules out entire classes of theories. If they lived just 10 times less, the Sun would shine brighter in gamma rays than all the stars in the sky, and an amateur telescope with a gamma camera could detect it. This 'solar' method searches for dark matter right under our noses, without peering into distant galaxies.

🎯 If dark matter decayed just a bit faster, the gamma glow around the Sun could be seen even with an amateur telescope—though you'd need a special camera.
