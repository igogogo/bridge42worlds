Mysterious Flashes in Andromeda: Not Black Holes, but Stars express

Original: "Eppur non si trovano Vol. 2: No Planetary-mass Primordial Black Holes toward the Andromeda Galaxy"
· Przemek Mróz, Andrzej Udalski
arXiv:2604.00111 · 2026-03-31 · CC BY 4.0 · 1 min
Cosmology Galaxies Instrumentation Stellar General Relativity
Mysterious flares in Andromeda, mistaken for signs of dark matter, turned out to be pulsating beacon stars.
Abstract

A reanalysis of observations of the Andromeda galaxy (M31) from the Subaru telescope with the Hyper Suprime-Cam, previously interpreted as the discovery of twelve candidate short-duration (less than a day) gravitational microlensing events by planetary primordial black holes (PBHs), was performed using an independent photometric pipeline based on difference image analysis. It was found that all twelve objects show asymmetric light curves and/or variability over several nights. Ten of them are classified as RR Lyrae stars, one as an eclipsing binary system, and one as an unconfirmed variable. No microlensing events, nor a significant population of planetary PBHs in the dark matter, were detected. The results underscore the critical importance of reliably ruling out variable stars when searching for microlensing with high-cadence observations.

For nearly a century, astronomers have been searching for dark matter — an invisible scaffold that keeps galaxies from flying apart. Fritz Zwicky first proposed it, and Vera Rubin proved its existence by showing that stars on the outskirts of galaxies move too fast. Stephen Hawking suggested that dark matter could be made of primordial black holes, born in the fire of the Big Bang.

Recent observations of the galaxy Andromeda with the Subaru telescope seemed to confirm the hypothesis: ultra-precise photometry (measuring brightness) of millions of stars revealed 12 brief flashes. They were explained by gravitational microlensing: a passing black hole, like a magnifying glass, momentarily boosted the light of a background star.

But a reanalysis of the data noticed that the flashes were asymmetrical and sometimes repeated. It turned out these weren't lenses, but beacon stars themselves, whose brightness changes rhythmically. Ten objects are RR Lyrae type: they pulsate with a period of less than a day, like a distant lighthouse. Another is an eclipsing pair, where two stars take turns blocking each other's light. The last remains a mystery. There are no black holes in dark matter. This case reminds us: even the most elegant theories need rigorous checking — it's easy to mistake the twinkling of a lighthouse for a signal from the abyss.

🎯 The pulsations of RR Lyrae stars are so stable that they are used as cosmic lighthouses to measure distances with a precision of a few percent.
