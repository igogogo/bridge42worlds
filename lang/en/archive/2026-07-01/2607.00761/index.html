Cartographers of Evolution: How Algorithms Learn to Explore, Not Just Optimize ✦

Original: "From Consistency to Collaborative Discovery: MFEA-CoD for Multitask Novelty Search"
· Jiao Liu, Yanchi Li, Hua Yu, Abhishek Gupta, Yew-Soon Ong
arXiv:2607.00761 · 2026-07-01 · CC BY · 3 min
Neural Computing
For the first time, evolutionary algorithms coordinate exploration rather than chasing the best — and find far more unique solutions.
Abstract

A swarm of algorithms navigates a complex landscape. Typically, when tackling multiple tasks at once, they share common routes to speed things up. But the multifactorial evolutionary algorithm MFEA-CoD coordinates tasks to hunt for unusual paths. Repulsion keeps them from crowding into a single spot, while adaptive exchange borrows only useful tricks. On deceptive landscapes, where the straight trail is misleading, this yields a wealth of unexpected solutions. Experiments in mazes and robotics confirmed: chasing novelty, not just the goal, boosts the variety of discoveries.

1

A robot in a maze walks into a dead end. A minute later, it’s the first to find the exit. This illogical maneuver is exploration, not a mistake. Like a cartographer circling a mountain to see its slopes.

Evolutionary algorithms in silico have long mimicked selection and mutation to search for the best solutions. But in deceptive landscapes, greedy pursuit leads to a dead end. A hiker climbs the nearest hill—and beyond it is a precipice. Novelty search emerged: it evaluates not quality, but how different the behavior is from what’s already known. The goal is to collect a solution archive (a repository of unique options), not just the best one. The authors of MFEA-CoD run several such searches simultaneously and teach them not to interfere with each other. Like cartographers dividing a continent into sectors, the algorithms split the space of genotypes (encoded solution descriptions, akin to DNA) to faster map phenotypes (observable traits, like a robot’s coordinates).

In maze tests, the robot sometimes initially moved in the opposite direction from the goal, but it was this “illogical” move that later allowed it to find a shorter path—a classic “stepping stone” effect in novelty search.

At the heart of it is a repulsion operator: it pushes the search trajectories of different tasks apart, like like-charged magnetic poles, forcing them to explore different regions. A second mechanism is adaptive knowledge transfer: if one task stumbles upon a promising area, others can exploit it, but only when it’s truly beneficial. This creates a dance of explorers: they diverge to their own hills and converge on a shared find. Novelty is measured as the average distance to the K nearest points in the behavioral space from archive A: \(\rho(x, A) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{dist}(\phi(x), \mu_k)\). And the algorithm balances quality and novelty: \(F_{\text{nov-aug}}(x) = w \cdot f(x) + (1-w) \cdot \rho(x, A)\), where the weight w determines how much immediate gain to sacrifice for exploration.

Tests confirmed: coordinated search increased the final solution archive by 15–35%. In mazes, robots found the exit with 100% success in five out of six scenarios, and discovered the first working route 1.5–2 times faster than solo searches. In the MuJoCo simulator, the walking robots Hopper and Walker2d consistently outperformed competitors. A difference of 375 unique behaviors is like adding 375 new paths to a map of uncharted terrain.

Coordinated search produced up to 3199 unique behaviors on mazes—versus 2824 for independent searches.

This approach is not just a robot trick. It opens the door for physical simulations and stochastic optimization in engineering design, drug discovery, motion planning—anywhere the landscape is deceptive and collecting a set of options matters more than a single optimum. The method is expected to scale to hundreds of tasks and hybridize with large language models for cross-disciplinary knowledge transfer. The unknown continent gradually emerges on the map—and now it is charted not by loners, but by entire expeditions. Every step aside may become a path to discovery.
