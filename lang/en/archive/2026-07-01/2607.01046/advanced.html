Monodromy in Vineyards: A Single Singularity Is the Culprit

Original: "The Singular Source of Vineyard Monodromy"
· Erin W. Chambers, Christopher Fillmore, Shankha Shubhra Mukherjee, Rohit Roy, Elizabeth Stephenson, Mathijs Wintraecken
arXiv:2607.01046 · 2026-07-01 · CC BY 4.0 · 3 min
cs.CG math.DG math.GT
Mathematicians have shown that local monodromy in periodic families of persistence diagrams is generated by just one type of singularity of the symmetry set—the intersection of birth and death lines.
Abstract

Vineyards—parameterised families of persistence diagrams—are used to follow the evolution of topological features. When the parameter traces a closed loop, monodromy can arise: diagram points permute, hindering tracking and interpretation. The work focuses on plane curves (1‑manifolds) using singularity theory. It is proved that for a sufficiently small loop, monodromy cannot occur without a singular point of the distance function. The central geometric object is the symmetry set—the locus of centres of spheres tangent to the manifold in more than one point. It classifies these singularities and exactly determines when monodromy appears. This characterisation opens the door to algorithmic criteria for detecting and exploiting (or avoiding) monodromy in applications.

1

Context

Two passengers board an empty subway car on the Circle Line and take seats. They ride a full loop and, to their surprise, end up in each other’s seats—without either having moved. That’s essentially what monodromy does in vineyards—families of diagrams born from persistent homology. When the parameter is closed off (a daily rhythm, a processing cycle), feature labels can get scrambled, skewing the results. This is especially critical in data analysis for biology, climatology, or materials science. Until now, though, it wasn’t clear which geometric properties of the original object cause this confusion.

Methods

The authors applied singularity theory—a powerful framework developed to classify critical points. They considered a 1D manifold (a closed smooth curve) in the plane and a family of squared distance functions to a point on the loop. Their key ‘microscope’ was the generalized symmetry set—the locus of centers of circles tangent to the curve in at least two points, or with second-order contact (osculation). This set and its singularities are fully described by the Bruce–Giblin–Gibson classification. For each of the eight singularity types, the mathematicians performed a small loop traversal and tracked the evolution of persistence diagrams over time, relying on information about the birth and death order of cycles.

Results

The main result: locally, monodromy is generated solely by a singularity of type A₂₁/A₂₁—the intersection of two smooth branches of the symmetry set, one responsible for the simultaneous birth of two connected components, the other for the simultaneous death of two cycles. Near such a point, the space splits into four sectors, and as you loop around it, four contact points on two concentric circles sequentially change order. If, on top of that, the elder rule kicks in when matching births and deaths, after the loop two points on the diagram swap places—an exchange occurs. Numerical simulations back this up: only these singularities produce intertwined vines; all others yield unlinked rings.

Implications

Now we know exactly where to look for the source of ‘scrambling’ in periodic data. For the first time, this result ties the fine geometry of the original shape (the singularities of its symmetry set) to the topology of the vineyard. It explains known examples and opens the door to algorithmic control: you can deliberately construct loops that encircle the right singularities to steer feature tracking—or avoid them to simplify the analysis.

Future development

The natural next step is to transfer the method to 3D surfaces and higher dimensions, where singularity classification is already partly in place. There’s also the challenge of formulating global monodromy criteria: as the paper shows, even with a local A₂₁/A₂₁ singularity, a large loop may not produce monodromy if the singularities interact ‘in opposite phase.’ Tackling this will call on nonlinear dynamics and possibly knot theory.

Impact

This work will impact any field that processes time series with a cyclic parameter using topological data analysis—from heart rate monitoring to climate cycle studies. Computational geometry tools can now be fine-tuned to automatically search for singularities.

Next steps

The immediate next step is developing efficient algorithms to compute the symmetry set and its singularities from discrete data. Meanwhile, theorists will search for global composition laws governing local monodromy contributions.

Key open problems

Monodromy in vineyards is a specific instance of a deep connection between the geometry of parameter spaces and the topology of invariants, known from complex algebraic geometry and catastrophe theory. This result could spur analogous studies for multi-dimensional persistence.

🎯 The name ‘vineyard’ comes from how point trajectories on the diagram resemble grapevines. Monodromy itself was first observed in physics while studying phase transitions in crystals.
