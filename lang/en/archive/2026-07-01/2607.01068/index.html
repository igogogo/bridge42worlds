The Sieve of Infinity: How Q-spaces Intertwine with Measurable Cardinals

Original: "New results about Q and $$\Delta$$-spaces"
· János Balázs Ivanyos, Ákos Székely
arXiv:2607.01068 · 2026-07-01 · CC BY · 3 min
math.GN math.LO
The existence of spaces where every subset can be sifted through a countable collection of open neighborhoods turns out to be equivalent to the existence of a colossal measurable cardinal.
Abstract

Q-spaces and Δ-spaces are topological constructions with special rules: in Q-spaces, every set can be expressed as a countable intersection of open sets, while in Δ-spaces, any decreasing sequence of sets can be enveloped by a decreasing sequence of open sets with empty intersection. The main result: the existence of such spaces with extra properties (dense, without isolated points, carrying a probability measure that vanishes on points) is equivalent to the existence of a measurable cardinal — an infinity so large it cannot be reached by adding smaller ones. This connection is like a pair of watches: either both run or both stop in sync. Additionally, Q-spaces that are Lindelöf and have weight at most the continuum are forcibly limited in size, ruling out many large examples.

1

In mathematics there is a sieve that sifts not dust, but infinity itself. It’s called a Q-space: here any subset turns out to be a Gδ set — a countable intersection of open sets, as if sifted through an unimaginably fine mesh. A much more intriguing question: can such a sieve be “full-bodied” — crowded (no isolated points) and possessing the Baire property (dense in the sense of category)? The answer, as Ivanov and Székely discovered, is tucked away in the foundations of mathematics and requires the existence of a gigantic measurable cardinal.

A measurable cardinal is not just a huge number. It’s a special point on which one can define a nontrivial two-valued measure: it assigns either 0 or 1 to every subset, and this measure is not concentrated on individual points. John von Neumann first made sense of such a construction. The existence of measurable cardinals is neither provable nor disprovable in the standard axiomatic system — it adds an entirely new layer to set theory. Using the method of forcing (a forced expansion of the set-theoretic universe), the authors took a model with a measurable cardinal κ and tossed in κ random reals via stochastic algebra — a bit like numerically simulating a new probabilistic world. In the resulting universe they constructed a special ideal that turns κ into a Q-space, and one with a strictly positive measure to boot (open sets have positive weight, while points have zero).

Ordinary Q-sets (say, under Martin’s Axiom) are topologically “meager” — they can be broken down into nothing. Those built from a measurable cardinal, on the other hand, are “fat” and possess the Baire property, meaning that in the categorical sense they behave like full-blooded spaces.

The crystal-clear takeaway: the existence of a measurable cardinal is exactly equivalent to the possibility of constructing several exotic spaces. If the cardinal exists, you get a crowded Baire Δ-space, its Q-counterpart, as well as Δ- and Q-spaces with a measure that nullifies points. If the cardinal doesn’t exist, all these objects vanish. Along the way they proved a restriction for Lindelöf Q-spaces (those covered by countably many open sets): the cardinality of X is strictly less than the cofinality of the continuum: $$|X| < \operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak{c})$$ This formula follows from cardinal inequalities from information theory: the number of open sets o(X) is at most c, and moreover o(X) ≤ |C(X)| ≤ o(X)^ω, where C(X) is the set of continuous functions. From this we get c^|X| ≤ c, and by König’s lemma |X| must be small. In plain terms: for “large” Lindelöf, locally compact, or compact Q-spaces there is no room in ordinary mathematics — they must either have weight greater than the continuum, or else not exist at all without super-strength axioms.

The work builds a bridge between topology and large cardinals, a reminder that even abstract branches of mathematics often hinge on questions of incompleteness. Just as the graph of possible mathematical universes depends on the assumption of a measurable cardinal, whole classes of spaces pop in and out of existence. Now the optimization of a space’s structure is directly tied to the choice of axioms. An enticing open question remains: can we construct a Lindelöf Q-space with weight larger than the continuum without invoking giant cardinals? The answer will affect the famous Moore problem on L-spaces. Meanwhile, the probabilistic logic of large cardinals keeps sifting infinity ever more finely through the sieve of topology.
