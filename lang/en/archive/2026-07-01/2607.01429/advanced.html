The Data Watershed: How a Binary Instrument Unlocks Multidimensional Models

Original: "A condition for the identification of multivariate models with binary instruments -- with Corrigendum and Addendum"
· Florian Gunsilius
arXiv:2607.01429 · 2026-07-01 · CC BY 4.0 · 4 min
econ.EM
Turns out, even a simple on/off toggle can fully reconstruct a complex model with dozens of variables—provided the hidden factors flow without forming cycles.
Abstract

The paper describes an empirical condition for nonparametric point identification of multivariate IV models with continuous endogenous variables and binary instruments. The condition is first-stage cyclic monotonicity, which generalizes rank invariance. It enables identification of nonlinear models with general heterogeneity using a single binary instrument, extending existing results that required restrictions on heterogeneity or instrument support. Asymptotic convergences of empirical distributions are derived for practical testing. Identification relies on the convergence of cyclically monotone mappings between quasi-concave functions. In the correction of the proof of Lemma 1, the transition from Brenier maps to inverse Brenier maps (multivariate ranks) broadens applicability to smooth non-quasi-concave and multimodal distributions with compact support under a nondegeneracy condition; for smooth parametric classes, the condition holds almost surely.

Context

On a topographic map, raindrops choose their path: falling on one slope, they flow into River A; on the other, into River B. An observer sees only the winding rivers (response variables Y) and knows which slope the water came from (instrument Z), but cannot see the drops themselves (unobserved factors ε). This is the econometrician's dilemma: how to reconstruct the landscape's shape (structural equation m) from two runoff maps if the slopes are determined by a single binary choice? The classical answer required either a rich continuous instrument or heavy restrictions on the model's form. But rivers work differently: water flows downhill, never looping back—in mathematics, this is called cyclical monotonicity. Gunsilius's insight is that this property alone is enough for a simple slope switch to recover the entire terrain. Underlying it is a dance of two distributions and their optimal matching.

Methods

In a multidimensional world, the classic control variable trick fails: you cannot uniquely order vectors. Instead, the author builds a bridge to optimal transport theory. For each raindrop U, its path X = h(Z,U) on slope Z follows a cyclically monotone mapping—a multivariate analogue of non-decreasing. Thanks to Brenier's theorem, such a mapping is unique and invertible almost surely. Thus, one can define a 'rank map' R_z—the inverse mapping that sends points from one slope to points on the other with the same rank. The watershed is the set where the rank map does not shift the point: R_z(x) = x. Technical conditions require smooth densities, boundedness, and non-degeneracy: the Jacobian matrix of R_z(x) − x must have constant rank in a neighborhood of the watershed. Then an iterative process—switching slopes and applying the rank map—forces any starting point to slide down to the watershed, while the conditional distribution of the rainfall (ε) is preserved at each step. This is the key to identification.

Results

The main result is a theorem: if the watershed has dimension less than k and thus Lebesgue measure zero, then the structural function m(x,ε) is uniquely determined for almost all x and ε. In other words, two economists looking at the same data will obtain exactly the same model—except on a vanishingly small set of initial conditions. Moreover, the condition is testable: the author derived the asymptotic of the empirical process of the difference between two conditional cumulative distribution functions. It turns out that as the sample size grows, this process converges to a Gaussian process—meaning one can construct uniform confidence bands and statistically test the existence of the watershed directly from the data. A striking example is two multidimensional variables with identical marginal distributions but different dependence (copula). Classical element-wise approaches are blind here and yield no identification; the new method sees the whole structure and pins down the model, like revealing a hidden contour on a map.

Implications

For applied science, this lifts a longstanding limitation: contrived instruments with broad support are no longer needed. A simple binary factor—participated or not, received treatment or placebo—is enough to nonparametrically estimate complex multidimensional effects. The work significantly expands the class of identifiable models, including those where the dimension of unobserved factors exceeds the dimension of the response (e.g., many latent abilities affect a single test score). This opens the door to more honest causal analysis in economics, epidemiology, and social sciences.

Future development

Ahead lies charting routes through uncharted territory. First, we must learn to handle discrete and mixed data, where the method currently stumbles. Second, cyclical monotonicity is a strong requirement; perhaps it can be relaxed using generalized Brenier maps. Third, efficient computational algorithms for high dimensions need to be developed—for now, each iteration is like taking a step in the mountains blindfolded. Once these peaks are conquered, the watershed will become not just a mathematical abstraction but a precise tool in the analyst's hands.

Impact

The result directly impacts applied econometrics, biostatistics (Mendelian randomization), and data analysis in sociology—anywhere instruments are often binary.

Next steps

The immediate task is to create a software package for testing the condition on real data and to develop semiparametric estimators that achieve the efficiency bound.

Key open problems

The article is directly connected to the key problem of identifiability of nonseparable models with endogeneity. It advances us toward answering the question: what are the minimal sufficient variations in an instrument to fully recover complex causal structures?
