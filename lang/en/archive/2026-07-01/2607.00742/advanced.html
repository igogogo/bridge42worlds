Voting Odd: How Symmetric Difference Speeds Up Automata

Original: "Algorithms and fine-grained complexity for nondeterministic and symmetric difference automata"
· Dmitry Chistikov, Radosław Piórkowski, Neha Rino, Brink van der Merwe
arXiv:2607.00742 · 2026-07-01 · CC BY · 2 min
cs.FL
A fresh look at fine-grained complexity: can the word acceptance problem in nondeterministic automata be reduced to a parity variant, and can algorithms be sped up?
Abstract

Symmetric difference automata (XNFA) are a twist on finite automata: they accept a word precisely when the number of accepting runs is odd—equivalently, they are weighted automata over the field with two elements. We dive into the fine-grained complexity of the big questions: membership (does the automaton accept a given word?), emptiness, and equivalence, aiming to shave down the polynomial exponents in running times. Assuming polynomial ambiguity, we give a randomized reduction from NFA membership to XNFA membership. For automata with bounded ambiguity (say, unambiguous ones), membership becomes faster than in the general case. Without any ambiguity assumptions, we craft speedier verification algorithms for candidate certificates of (non)emptiness and (non)equivalence. Several results lift to weighted automata over other semirings and fields.

1

Context

Since the 1970s, it’s known that the word acceptance problem for NFAs is complete for NL, while for XNFAs it’s complete for ⊕L. The question of whether NL ⊆ ⊕L remains open: essentially, can we ‘re-vote’ a trial of one type into a trial of the other? This work shifts the question into the realm of graph complexity and fine-grained time analysis, where specific exponents matter, not just polynomial bounds.

Methods

For k-ambiguous automata, where each juror-path appears at most k times, a dynamic algorithm tracks states using only k|Q| operations per symbol. When reducing an NFA to an XNFA, we build an ensemble of k parallel ‘court sessions’ over the extension field GF(2^k); randomly chosen weights ensure that the original NFA’s verdict is reflected in the oddness of at least one XNFA with error probability ≤ 1/2. This trick relies on randomization and linear algebra, with the error bounded by the Schwartz–Zippel lemma. Additionally, a stochastic analysis of ambiguity speeds up standard dynamic programming.

Results

First outcome: for unambiguous automata, acceptance time is O(|w||Q| + |δ|), solving an open problem in 0-1 matrix multiplication. Second: if there were an algorithm for XNFA running in O((|δ||w|)^{1-ε}) time, then polynomially ambiguous NFAs could be solved almost as fast—this is a counterplay to the hardness conjecture for NFAs (Bringmann et al.). Third: emptiness and equivalence certificates for XNFA can be checked in O(|Σ|n^ω) time, with ω < 2.38 the matrix multiplication exponent. Essentially, the trial transcript lets a verifier-judge confirm in O(n^ω) steps that a guilty verdict is impossible. Proving cubic (n^3) time optimality via SETH would require refuting NSETH.

Implications

The line between ‘easy’ rings and ‘hard’ semirings blurs: with a reasonable number of voices, XNFA is no harder than NFA. The certificate technique, relying on the structure of the automaton, paves the way to lower bounds, linking automata theory with conjectures about matrix multiplication hardness.

Future development

The main challenge is to drop the polynomial ambiguity requirement. If that succeeds, the key target becomes the exponent ω itself: each shift from 3 toward 2 brings us closer to when checking an XNFA verdict takes nearly quadratic time.

Impact

Automata theory, software verification, regular query processing in databases—wherever automata serve as ‘courts’ over strings.

Next steps

Build a deterministic reduction without parallel sessions and investigate the exact complexity of XNFA without ambiguity assumptions.

Key open problems

Directly tied to the NL vs ⊕L problem and the NFA acceptance conjecture (Bringmann et al., 2024).
