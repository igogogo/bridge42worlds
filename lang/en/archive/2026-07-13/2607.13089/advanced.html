Resurrecting the Z3: How Relays and Memory Recreate a Lost Computing Masterpiece

Original: "The Reconstructions of Konrad Zuse's Z3 Computer"
· Raul Rojas
arXiv:2607.13089 · 2026-07-13 · CC BY · 3 min
cs.GL cs.AR History and Philosophy of Physics
Replicas of the world’s first fully automatic programmable computer, destroyed in 1943, bring back to life the logic and spirit of Konrad Zuse’s machine.
Abstract

1941, Berlin: Konrad Zuse unveils the Z3 — one of the first programmable computers. Decades later, to preserve this legacy, four reconstructions have been built. The first was done by the inventor himself in the 1960s and resides in the German Museum in Munich. A second, with educational aims, was constructed in Berlin in 2001. The third — the Z3r — was assembled by Horst Zuse for his father's centenary. The fourth, Swiss-built, was developed by Christoph Traber. Each machine is more than a copy; it's an attempt to replicate the original's logic and design, much like restorers reconstruct a historical mechanism from fragments. This review systematizes these projects, highlighting their role in preserving the cultural heritage of early cybernetics.

1

Context

December 1943. Berlin. A bomb strike destroys not just a building, but the world’s first fully automatic programmable computer, the Z3. Like a painting consumed by fire, the original vanished, leaving behind only circuit sketches and fragmentary memories. But just as restorers reconstruct lost artworks from drawings, engineers and historians set out to recreate this computing masterpiece—not to make an exact copy, but to preserve its spirit and logic. These numerical simulation of the past have become a bridge between eras.

Methods

The first copy was built in 1962 by Zuse himself at his company—it served as physical evidence in a patent dispute. It used modern small relays and changed the layout. In 2001, a hybrid was created in Berlin: control theory was handled by a microcontroller, and signal processing generated clock signals, but memory and Graphs remained relay-based. The Z3r project (2010) became a teaching tool—2,500 relays (about as many as stars visible to the naked eye) were fitted with LEDs, and 60 buttons were hidden under the console for manual optimization of bit manipulation. Finally, the Swiss reconstruction of 2024 came as close as possible to the original: scarce relays were replaced with diode arrays, and the topology of the wiring was done according to historical information theory circuit diagrams, preserving electromagnetic compatibility and even the tilt of stepping switches for visual effect.

Results

Every machine reproduces the key mechanism: 22-bit floating-point words, separate arithmetic for exponent and mantissa, sequential reading of commands from punched tape. The Z3r works like a transparent clock—you can see relays switching, registers shifting, the number being normalized. The Swiss version uses electromechanical stepping switches, making the rotation of the Digital Image Processing tangible. Tests show that all reconstructions execute the same algorithms as the Zuse Z3, albeit at different speeds.

Implications

These machines are not museum mummies. They have become a testbed: historians analyze the numerical simulation logic, engineers study the fault tolerance of relay circuits. Monthly public demonstrations in Solothurn, Switzerland prove that computing power isn't measured in gigahertz—sometimes the clicks of 80-year-old technology are enough to captivate an audience.

Future development

Interest in relay computers in education is growing: slow, visually rich operation is ideal for explaining the basics of computer architecture. Perhaps the next generation of replicas will feature a tactile interface—visitors will be able to manually program using patch plugs.

Impact

Affects the preservation of technical heritage and museum pedagogy. Breaks the stereotype that early computers were exclusively an Anglo-American invention.

Next steps

Creating an open digital model of the Z3 with a web simulator. Incorporating reconstructions into university courses on computing history.

Key open problems

Not directly linked to unsolved problems in physics. However, it raises an important question: how to recover lost digital systems when source code and documentation have vanished—a problem relevant to preserving the cultural heritage of the digital age.

🎯 The original Z3 weighed about a ton but performed only 3–4 additions per second—slower than a calculator in a 1980s wristwatch. Yet it used advanced binary floating-point arithmetic that wouldn't appear in mass-market processors for decades.

🎬 In William Gibson's novel 'The Difference Engine,' an alternate history is filled with mechanical computers. The Z3 is a real embodiment of such fantasies: relay logic, punched tape, and binary code seem to have stepped right out of steampunk pages, but 40 years earlier.

Key numbers

  • 1941: year of original's creation
  • 3-4: additions per second performed by Z3
