In 1941, engineer Konrad Zuse built the first fully automatic programmable computer, the Z3. It consisted of several thousand clicking switches and was controlled by a punched paper tape. The machine could add, subtract, multiply, and divide numbers. The original burned in a bombing raid in 1943, but over the past 80 years, several replicas have been built. These replicas preserve not only the design but the living rhythm of the first computer. Students and engineers can see firsthand how simple switches, working together, perform complex calculations. It’s a bridge between past and future—a slow, noisy, yet perfectly understandable great-grandfather of today’s processors.

Restoring the Z3: How Relay Clicks Brought a Lost Computer Back to Life

Original: "The Reconstructions of Konrad Zuse's Z3 Computer"
· Raul Rojas
arXiv:2607.13089 · 2026-07-13 · CC BY · 3 min
cs.GL cs.AR History and Philosophy of Physics
Destroyed by a bomb in 1943, the Z3 lives on today in replicas that reproduce not only its computations but the living rhythm of relay logic.
Abstract

In 1941, Konrad Zuse built the Z3 — one of the first programmable computers. To preserve the original, four reconstructions were created: like hand-copied duplicates of an ancient manuscript. The first, in the 1960s, was made by Zuse himself for the German Museum. In 2001, an educational model appeared in Berlin; later, his son Horst built the Z3r for his father's centenary, and Christoph Traber created an independent version in Switzerland. Step by step, engineers are recovering a lost mechanism.

1

Berlin, December 1943. A bomb flash—and the first fully automatic programmable computer in history, the Z3, vanishes in flames. Its creator, Konrad Zuse, doesn’t even try to rebuild the hardware: after the war, he constructs new machines. But the idea of the Z3—like a lost painting, with only sketches and memories left—awaits its moment. Restorers, armed with schematics and modern components, reconstruct the logic of the masterpiece, restoring its voice—the steady click of relays.

The first ‘restoration’ happened in 1962, when Zuse himself built a replica for a patent dispute. In it, the original bulky relays were replaced by smaller equivalents, the wiring was simplified—like a copyist slightly altering the brushstrokes but keeping the composition. In 2001, a hybrid was created in Berlin: control was handed to a microcontroller, clock signals were assigned to electronics, while memory and logic circuits remained relay-based—a bridge between eras. The Z3r project (2010) turned the calculator into a teaching tool: each of the 2500 relays got an LED, and 60 toggle switches under the console allowed manual bit manipulation. Then in 2024, Swiss engineers presented the most authentic reconstruction yet: scarce relays were replaced with diode assemblies, but they preserved the angled stepping switches, electromagnetic compatibility, and even the visible rotation of shafts.

The 2500 relays in the Z3r are about as many as the stars you can see with the naked eye on a moonless night. Each one clicks in its own rhythm, turning the machine into a mechanical orchestra.

At the heart of all versions is a 22-bit floating-point word, with separate storage for sign, exponent, and mantissa. The formula for a number is (−1)^s × 1.m × 2^(e−64), where s is the sign bit, m the mantissa, and e the exponent. The processor reads commands from punched tape—a long paper strip with holes—and executes them strictly sequentially. Step by step, relays open and close, registers shift, numbers normalize. In the Swiss version, the stepping switches rotate openly, and the discrete computing machine comes to life: you can see bits moving through the circuits like blood through veins. Even numerical simulation on modern processors can’t convey this kinetic beauty.

These restorations are not museum mummies. They’ve become a research playground: historians analyze the recreated logic, engineers test the fault tolerance of relay circuits. Public demonstrations in Solothurn draw crowds who listen to the clicks of 80-year-old technology and realize: computing power isn’t measured only in gigahertz. Interest in relay computers is growing in education: the slow, visual execution of operations is ideal for explaining computer architecture. Perhaps future replicas will get a tactile interface—visitors will be able to program manually through patch cords.

The original Z3 weighed about a ton, consumed 4000 watts, and performed 3–4 additions per second. A modern microcontroller does millions in the same second—but none of them sing a relay symphony.

Eighty years after the bombing, Zuse could walk into a hall where stepping switches rotate and relays click, and recognize his creation. His Z3, destroyed but not vanished, has found new life in restorations that preserve not only the principle but the soul of the first programmable automaton. In that steady rhythm lies not just history, but an anticipation of our digital world.

🎯 The original Z3 weighed about a ton, yet performed only 3–4 additions per second—slower than the calculator in a 1980s digital watch. Yet its 22-bit floating-point arithmetic was decades ahead of its time: mainstream processors didn't adopt it until the 1980s.

🎬 It recalls the alternate history from William Gibson’s novel 'The Difference Engine,' where the world runs on mechanical computers. The Z3’s relay logic is a living embodiment of steampunk, except it was built in earnest in 1941.
