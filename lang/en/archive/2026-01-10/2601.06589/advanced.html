Cosmic Vortex: Void Rotation Reveals the Secrets of the Universe ✦

Original: "Constraining Inflation Models with Spinning Voids"
· Geonwoo Kang, Jounghun Lee
arXiv:2601.06589 · 2026-01-10 · CC BY 4.0 · 4 min
Cosmology Galaxies General Relativity
The asymmetry of galaxy redshifts inside voids offers an independent measure of matter density and the strength of primordial fluctuations—no dark energy assumptions needed.
Abstract

A new diagnostic tool offers a robust, independent way to nail down the running of the scalar spectral index—a measure of how the primordial density fluctuations deviate from a perfect scale-invariant spectrum. The trick? Looking at the coherent spin of galaxies inside cosmic voids, which shows up as a redshift asymmetry when you compare one side of the void’s rotation axis to the other. Based on AbacusSummit simulations, the team built a non-parametric model of this asymmetry and found it to be remarkably universal across a swath of cosmologies, from dynamic dark energy models to different initial conditions within ΛCDM. The universality cracks only when the scalar spectral index runs: positive running consistently shrinks the asymmetry relative to the model predictions, while negative running stretches it. Because many exotic inflation models do forecast a running index, and this void asymmetry is something we can actually observe, the method blazes a new trail toward understanding what really powered inflation.

1

Context

Modern cosmology has hit a wall: standard tests—weak lensing, galaxy clusters, CMB anisotropies—can’t disentangle matter density Ωm from the amplitude of primordial fluctuations σ8. They’re locked in the combination S8 = σ8(Ωm/0.3)^0.5, but nature demands we know each separately. Voids—giant empty patches in the galactic web—preserve a near-pristine memory of the early universe. Spanning hundreds of millions of light-years, with galaxy densities dropping tenfold, their emptiness turns them into ideal laboratories of initial conditions. Back in 2006, Korean astrophysicists predicted that voids should spin under tidal forces from large-scale structure. The universe’s dynamics are governed by Friedmann equations, and the observed galaxy redshifts follow Hubble's law, discovered by Edwin Hubble. The missing mass hinted at by Vera Rubin through galaxy rotation adds to the intrigue, pushing for fresh independent tests.

Methods

The analysis draws on public data from the AbacusSummit simulations—over 330 billion dark matter particles in an 8 h⁻³ Gpc³ box, evolved from z=99 to 0.1 across 33 cosmologies: 20 ΛCDM, 3 wCDM, and 10 w₀wa CDM. From a catalog of dark matter halos with masses above 10^11.5 h⁻¹ M⊙ at z=0.1, ‘galaxy’ samples were built. The Void-Finder algorithm then carved out voids with at least 15 such objects. To avoid size bias, samples were controlled by effective radius Rv. For each void, we scanned bisector directions across the sky, computing the asymmetry of redshift radial velocities between two halves—a shift influenced not only by cosmic expansion but also by galaxies’ own motions via the Doppler effect. The orientation giving the maximum difference was taken as the projected spin axis. The resulting Δz_max values were collected into p(Δz_max) distributions for each cosmology.

Results

Remarkably, all 33 distributions are beautifully described by a generalized gamma distribution with shape parameter k and scale θ. The shape k = 6.551 proved nearly constant across all initial conditions, while θ showed a strong linear dependence on Ωm and σ8. To verify, we calculated the Kullback–Leibler divergence: θ’s sensitivity to parameter changes is 40 times higher than k’s. Plotting θ as a function of Ωm and σ8 at fixed counterpart, we found Pearson correlation coefficients r_p ≈ 0.998, confirming near-perfect linearity. This led to a bilinear model: 10⁵θ_model = 9.12 Ωm + 2.72 σ8 − 1.69. Its coefficients, derived from ΛCDM, describe dark-energy cosmologies to within fractions of a percent. The model is universal: recalculating coefficients separately for wCDM and w₀waCDM gave values consistent with ΛCDM within uncertainties. Additional tests varying simulation volume, resolution, and void center definition confirmed robustness—all results lie within the 99% confidence region.

Implications

This bilinear model turns voids into a standalone cosmological test, rivaling the precision of gravitational lensing or baryon acoustic oscillations. Its key edge: independence from w. Unlike standard methods, void asymmetry doesn’t degenerate with the dark energy equation of state. It cuts through the Ωm–σ8 knot, pulling out each parameter separately. In essence, we have a ‘cosmic speedometer,’ calibrated by primordial CMB fluctuations but reading local dynamics in today’s universe.

Future development

The immediate goal: apply the model to real data. The upcoming LSST survey on the Vera Rubin Observatory and DESI’s spectroscopic programs will deliver galaxy samples with redshift precision of 1%. Meanwhile, refining coefficients with higher-resolution simulations is needed—current results are limited by a particle mass of 2×10⁹ h⁻¹ M⊙. Eventually, void asymmetry could join the standard toolkit alongside cosmic microwave background, supernovae, and baryon oscillations.

Impact

This method will directly impact extragalactic astronomy and cosmology, offering a new tool to test models of dark matter and energy. It also spurs the development of void-finding algorithms for observational data.

Next steps

Measure the asymmetry distribution in real galaxy catalogs (e.g., SDSS, DES) and compare with model predictions. Analyze simulations that include baryonic physics to assess systematic effects.

Key open problems

The work tackles a key cosmological puzzle—the degeneracy between parameters Ωm and σ8—which limits our ability to pin down dark energy’s nature and test inflation.
