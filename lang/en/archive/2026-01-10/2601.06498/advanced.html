Spec-o3: a spectroscopy agent hunting for rare stars ✦

Original: "Spec-o3: A Tool-Augmented Vision-Language Agent for Rare Celestial Object Candidate Vetting via Automated Spectral Inspection"
· Minghui Jia, Qichao Zhang, Ali Luo, Linjing Li, Shuo Ye, Hailing Lu, Wen Hou, Dongbin Zhao
arXiv:2601.06498 · 2026-01-10 · CC BY 4.0 · 3 min
Computation and Language Instrumentation
A visual-language model with a magnifying glass mimics an astronomer’s logic and decides in 0.2 seconds whether an object will make the catalog.
Abstract

Modern surveys (LAMOST, SDSS, DESI) deliver millions of spectra, but the final selection of rare objects is still done manually by astronomers — automated classifiers don't provide reliable explanations. Spec-o3 solves this problem: it's a language agent with computer vision and tools that mimics expert analysis, building a chain of multimodal reasoning. The agent is trained in two stages: first on demonstrations of astronomers' work, then fine-tuned with reinforcement learning based on the final outcome for rare classes. On LAMOST data, it achieved a macro-F1 of 76.5 (versus 28.3 for analogues) with a 7-billion-parameter model, outperforming both general and specialized systems. When transferred to SDSS and DESI data, its accuracy is maintained. Experts confirm: the agent's reasoning is physically meaningful, making it suitable for trusted automated verification.

1

Context

Astronomers conducting spectroscopic surveys are like detectives examining clues with a magnifying glass. Each spectrum is a fingerprint of a star's chemical composition and physical conditions. To compile a catalog of rare objects—for instance, white dwarfs or cataclysmic variables—machine learning algorithms first sift through millions of records, leaving hundreds of thousands of suspects. But the final word belongs to a human: only an expert, looking at the plot, can reject noise and artifacts. As data volumes grow, this bottleneck chokes science—a single catalog of 170,000 candidates takes months of manual labor.

Methods

Spec-o3 is a visual-language agent based on Qwen2.5-VL, trained to think like Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin at the dawn of spectral classification. It is given a full-view spectrum and a question: “Is this a cataclysmic variable?” The agent then decides on its own which region to zoom in on. It calls a visualization tool, receives a detailed plot in, say, the 6500–6600 Å range, and reasons: “I see broad emission wings around the Balmer line Hα—this indicates fast-moving gas in an accretion disk, where the Doppler effect stretches the lines.” If needed, it then examines the Hβ line and renders a verdict. This multimodal chain of thoughts (iMCoT) alternates text and images. Spec-o3 was trained in two stages: a cold start on a thousand trajectories recorded by astronomers, followed by reinforcement learning based solely on the final answer (correct/incorrect), without ready-made chains.

Results

After training, Spec-o3-7B raised the macro-F1 across five rare types from 28.3% to 76.5%—nearly tripling the baseline model’s score. For comparison, the commercial o3 achieved 52.3%, and specialized deep networks trained on the same data only 64.9%. The agent doesn’t just guess: its reasoning was approved by six experts as coherent and physically meaningful. Moreover, on spectra from other telescopes—SDSS and DESI—accuracy barely drops, meaning Spec-o3 latches onto genuine stellar features, not instrumental noise. Even on unfamiliar classes (O, B, A stars) or at low signal-to-noise ratios it maintains 76–78% F1.

Implications

The key achievement is transparency. Instead of a black box spitting out a probability, the agent issues a report: what it saw, where it looked, and why it decided that way. This radically changes trust in automated systems in astrophysics. Specialists can audit decisions, and when in doubt, send the object back for manual inspection. In essence, Spec-o3 becomes an assistant that takes over routine work, allowing scientists to focus on unusual discoveries.

Future development

For now, the agent only looks at spectra. Real cataloging requires cross-checking with photometry, images, and databases. The next logical step is to teach Spec-o3 to access external archives and compare heterogeneous evidence. Moreover, the cold start currently relies on manual labeling, but experiments show that even three reference trajectories suffice to synthesize dozens of new ones, meaning that expertise amplification via large language models can rapidly expand the set of tasks.

Impact

The technology will directly impact all major projects: LAMOST, SDSS, the future DESI. Automating final inspection will slash catalog publication time from months to days and increase their homogeneity.

Next steps

The immediate next steps are validating the agent on a wider set of spectral types and implementing confidence estimation mechanisms to automatically flag contentious cases for human review.

Key open problems

The work is directly connected to quasars and other objects where spectral features are hard to distinguish. It also raises the broader issue of AI interpretability in science: how much can we trust models that think not in words but in images? Spec-o3 offers a partial answer—by mimicking an expert’s gaze, which can be verified against the same criteria as a human’s.

🎯 To find just 323 cataclysmic variables in the LAMOST archive, astronomers manually inspected 170,000 spectra—about as many frames as in three feature-length films, translated into hours of painstaking work.

Key numbers

  • time to process one spectrum by the agent: 0.2 s
  • speedup compared to an expert: ~50×
  • macro-F1 on test tasks: 76.5%
  • number of spectra inspected by astronomers for a single catalog: 170,000
  • number of finally confirmed objects: 323
