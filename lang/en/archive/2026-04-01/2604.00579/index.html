Mysterious Gamma Note: A Trace of Dark Matter? express

Original: "A Universal 1.5 GeV Gamma-Ray Line in Active Galactic Nuclei"
· Shi-Ju Kang, Yue Yin, Yong-Gang Zheng, Qingwen Wu
arXiv:2604.00579 · 2026-04-01 · CC BY · 1 min
High Energy
A stable gamma signal found in the centers of distant galaxies—possibly the first direct hint of dark matter particles.
Abstract

Analyzing 17 years of observations from the Fermi telescope, astronomers have detected a steady gamma-ray line at an energy of ~1.5 GeV in three active galactic nuclei: two blazars (their jets are aimed at Earth) and one radio galaxy (jet seen from the side). Unlike the variable glow of the nuclei, the line remained constant. Its presence in objects with different geometries is hard to reconcile with jet emission models and matches the expected signal from dark matter particle annihilation. This finding is one of the most promising candidates for the “fingerprint” of dark matter.

The Fermi telescope spent 17 years recording gamma rays from the active nuclei of galaxies with giant black holes at their centers. The glow of their jets resembles a chaotic cacophony: it constantly changes. Amid this noise, scientists found one unchanging note—at an energy of 1.5 GeV in the spectrum (i.e., the energy distribution), a narrow and absolutely stable peak appeared.

Gamma rays are light a billion times more energetic than visible light. Earth's atmosphere absorbs them completely, so Fermi operates in space.

Such stability is inexplicable for jet radiation—they would have long disrupted this note. However, it fits perfectly with the dark matter hypothesis: its particles, upon colliding, turn into gamma-ray photons, and their energy equals precisely the particle mass. The detected 1.5 GeV indicates a mass slightly heavier than a proton—an unexpected coincidence, as if nature mirrored ordinary matter. This is precisely the signature Vera Rubin and Fritz Zwicky predicted half a century ago. Remarkably, the telescope was originally designed to detect explosive gamma-ray bursts, but instead captured a quiet, eternal note.

🎯 The 1.5 GeV energy roughly corresponds to the rest mass of a proton—the particles that make up atomic nuclei.

🎬 Dark matter annihilation is a favorite sci-fi trope: for example, in 'The Three-Body Problem' by Liu Cixin.

$E_\gamma \approx m_\text{DM} c^2$
The energy of the gamma-ray photon (E_γ) is approximately equal to the mass of the dark matter particle (m_DM) multiplied by the square of the speed of light (c²).
