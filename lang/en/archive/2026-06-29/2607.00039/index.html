in short · 20 seconds

Researchers have found a way to turn a car into a device that updates over the air, like a smartphone. Modern cars have up to a hundred electronic control units, each 'speaking' its own language tied to specific hardware. Updating them all simultaneously via the internet is nearly impossible. Stuttgart researchers examined various 'interpreters'—mediators between software and chips that help components understand each other. It turns out the ideal approach is to combine two methods. The first is strict isolation: steering and braking units run in a separate protected environment where faults from other systems can't penetrate. The second is a flexible protocol for infotainment and navigation, allowing new features to be added easily. The hybrid approach prevents a glitch in the music player from affecting the controls, and enables 'over-the-air' updates without replacing parts. The technology is already being tested in new models and promises to make cars safer and smarter.

Diplomatic Protocol for Chip and Code

Original: "Evaluating Hardware Abstraction Layer Concepts for Software Defined Vehicles: Insights into Applicability and Effectiveness"
· Akshay Narla, Johannes Stümpfle, Souvik Saha, Nasser Jazdi, Michael Weyrich
arXiv:2607.00039 · 2026-06-29 · CC BY · 3 min
cs.SE cs.SY eess.SY
A comparative analysis of hardware abstractions for software-defined vehicles: lessons from IT, industry, and the automotive sector itself.
Abstract

A Software-Defined Vehicle (SDV) can update and change its behavior through software, but the tight coupling of code and hardware gets in the way. Engineers proposed a Hardware Abstraction Layer (HAL) — a 'translator' between the metal and the code. By comparing approaches from cars, smartphones, networks, and factory automation, they found that middleware-based HALs are great for porting software, while a hypervisor provides the isolation and security crucial for over-the-air updates. They recommend a hybrid that combines the hypervisor backbone with standard middleware interfaces. This will speed up the transition to truly programmable machines.

1

Stuttgart, 2025. A software engineer plugs a laptop into a test car, not for diagnostics, but to replace the car's 'personality'—like flashing a smartphone's firmware. But instead of a quick update, he faces a cascade of incompatibilities. Each electronic control unit (ECU) speaks its own dialect, tied to a specific supplier's chip. Software written for a Bosch braking system won't understand a Continental processor without a complex 'interpreter'. This interpreter is the hardware abstraction layer (HAL), a diplomatic protocol between the world of silicon and the realm of algorithms. Without it, the car will never become a software-defined vehicle (SDV)—a machine whose functions are defined by code, not soldering.

The QNX Hypervisor spends less than 2% of CPU time on its own overhead—like a diplomat at a summit devoting only a couple of seconds per hour to translation.

Researchers at the University of Stuttgart, like seasoned negotiators, staged a review of HAL solutions. They didn't limit themselves to the automotive realm but peered into neighboring 'states': smartphones with their Android Treble, the industrial automation of Industry 4.0 with its Asset Administration Shell, the network virtualization of NFV, and cloud interfaces like VirtIO. Two diplomatic approaches crystallized: a lightweight messaging protocol (middleware) or full isolation of the 'embassy' inside a virtual machine (hypervisor). Automotive candidates—AUTOSAR Adaptive, Android Automotive, QNX, EB Corbos, and others—were evaluated against 14 criteria (from determinism to Machine Learning support), sorted by the MoSCoW method into must-haves, should-haves, and could-haves. The maximum score is 45, reflecting the protocol’s maturity.

The results sharply delineated the diplomatic ranks. Hypervisor-based solutions (QNX, EB Corbos, RTA-LWHVR) unequivocally lead in safety and readiness for over-the-air (OTA) updates. Their overhead is nearly imperceptible, and the critical loop—steering, instantaneous hazard response—is shielded by hardware armor. The middleware camp falls short on determinism but offers flexibility and portability: applications for video stream analysis or self-learning can easily migrate between chips. A curious outlier—SOAFEE, a standard yet unborn—theoretically scores a perfect 45 points, promising to encompass everything.

The cold numbers revealed an architectural rift familiar to diplomats since the days of John von Neumann: how to combine the reliability of strict separation with the evolutionary flexibility of open interfaces. Today, no single HAL is universal. The solution is a hybrid protocol. The critical loop (braking, steering) lives under hypervisor isolation, while everything else—from infotainment to cloud telemetry—communicates via standardized middleware, much like web services over HTTP. This diplomatic pact ensures that a navigation glitch can't affect the brakes and allows the infotainment system to be updated over the air without rebuilding the entire firmware.

The Stuttgart engineer opens his laptop again. This time the protocol works: the car accepts the new code in seconds, silently reconfiguring its behavior. The diplomatic success of the HAL determines whether millions of cars become full citizens of the Internet of Things or remain closed 'iron islands'. Tomorrow, this hybrid interpreter could head into series production—first on the pages of the Eclipse SDV standard, and then onto the roads.
