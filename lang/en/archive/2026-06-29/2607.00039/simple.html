in short · 20 seconds

Researchers have found a way to turn a car into a device that updates over the air, like a smartphone. Modern cars have up to a hundred electronic modules, each 'speaking' its own language tied to specific hardware. Updating them all simultaneously via the Internet is nearly impossible. Scientists from Stuttgart explored different kinds of 'interpreters'—intermediaries between software and chips that help the components understand each other. The perfect approach, it turns out, is to combine two methods. The first is strict isolation: steering and brake units run in a separate protected environment, shielded from failures in other systems. The second is a flexible protocol for infotainment and navigation, making it easy to add new features. The hybrid approach prevents a music glitch from affecting driving and allows over-the-air updates without replacing parts. The technology is already being tested in new models and promises to make cars safer and smarter.

A Car That Understands Without a Word

Original: "Evaluating Hardware Abstraction Layer Concepts for Software Defined Vehicles: Insights into Applicability and Effectiveness"
· Akshay Narla, Johannes Stümpfle, Souvik Saha, Nasser Jazdi, Michael Weyrich
arXiv:2607.00039 · 2026-06-29 · CC BY · 2 min
cs.SE cs.SY eess.SY
Engineers found a way to give cars a common language so they can be updated over the air, just like smartphones.
Abstract

The car updates like a smartphone. New features arrive wirelessly. We need to decouple software from hardware. Engineers studied methods from phones and factories. Conclusion: it's best to combine a protective layer (like armor) and a common command language (like a translator).

1

Stuttgart, 2025. A mechanic plugs a laptop into a car to update its software. But instead of a one-click job, he gets a cascade of errors. Every electronic control unit in the car, whether the brake system or the infotainment, runs its own “dialect.” They’re made by different companies and tied to a specific chip, like a key to a lock. To get them to work together and accept updates, you need a universal interpreter—called a hardware abstraction layer (HAL). Think of it as a diplomatic protocol: it translates from the language of computer hardware to the language of software.

Researchers from Stuttgart looked at other fields that already use such interpreters: smartphones (so apps run on any phone), industrial robots (for flexible factory setups), and network equipment (which must adapt quickly). They compared over a dozen automotive interpreters against strict criteria: how fast they respond to danger (instant reaction), whether they can support self-learning features like traffic sign recognition, and how easy it is to port the software to another chip.

The results split the interpreters into two camps. Some work like a soundproofed negotiation room—they fully isolate vital parts (steering, brakes) from the rest, preventing any error from compromising safety. Such systems use almost no resources: for instance, one of them, QNX, uses less than 2% of the processor’s power. Others are like an open forum: they let different programs talk freely, but without strict oversight. This is handy for cameras and radars that transmit images, or for algorithms that learn from mistakes. But for critical systems, such flexibility is dangerous.

QNX spends less than 2% of processor time on translation. Imagine an interpreter at a one-hour meeting who zones out for just a minute—barely noticeable.

So the researchers proposed a hybrid approach—a diplomatic pact. Safety-critical nodes (brakes, steering) are placed in isolated rooms with strict access control. Everything else—navigation, music, climate control—communicates via a universal protocol, much like web services. That way, a glitch in the entertainment system won’t affect driving, and a new version of the navigation software can be loaded over the air without reflashing every module. This idea has been pondered since the days of von Neumann, but now it’s hitting the road.

The Stuttgart mechanic plugs in the laptop again. This time, the car swallows the code instantly, as if it understands commands by intuition. It’s thanks to these interpreters that millions of cars will either become full citizens of the digital world or remain a heap of uncomprehending metal.

🎯 QNX spends less than 2% of processor time on its own overhead—like an interpreter at a high-stakes meeting who gets distracted for just a couple of seconds per hour.
