An Old Cable Catches Storms and Earthquakes express

Original: "Per-Span Microwave Frequency Fiber Interferometry in Subsea Cables for Scalable Deep-Ocean Geophysical Monitoring"
· Georgios Aias Karydis, Nicolas L. Celli, David Craig, Örn Jónsson, Andrés Arnar Hlynsson, Eoin Kenny, Charis Mesaritakis, Christopher J. Bean, Adonis Bogris
arXiv:2606.21905 · 2026-06-20 · CC BY 4.0 · 1 min
Optics Geophysics
Engineers turned an ordinary transoceanic cable into a sensitive detector of changes in the ocean.
Abstract

A low-cost microwave interferometric sensing method is proposed, enabling monitoring of each span (the section between optical amplifiers) of a submarine cable. The technology was tested on an active 1,770-km communication line connecting Ireland and Iceland. During four months of continuous measurements, signals related to tides, storms, and teleseismic earthquakes were identified. The spatial resolution was about 50–80 km, matching the typical distance between repeaters. These results pave the way for using existing telecommunications infrastructure for affordable global ocean monitoring.

The planet-spanning web of undersea internet cables acts like a giant ear. The glass thread inside responds to water movements: currents, tides, and distant earthquakes bend the cable. Light traveling along the thread takes slightly longer—this delay is measured on shore against a reference. That’s how the speed of light reveals itself.

Engineers tested the idea on the Ireland–Iceland cable (1,770 km), sending a microwave signal instead of a laser. Over four months, instruments recorded the rhythm of tides, the footsteps of storms, and the jolts of distant earthquakes. The same principle is used in gravitational wave detectors—except here they catch not the trembling of space, but the sway of the ocean.

The first such device was built by Albert Michelson in 1881, as he tried to detect Earth’s motion through the "ether." Today his heirs listen to both the cosmos and the sea.

The most surprising part: the cable stayed online and kept carrying internet traffic, without interfering with the measurements at all. This vast network already lying on the seabed becomes a single ear for the ocean at no extra cost.

🎯 Modern submarine cables are so sensitive they can hear whale songs — though it does require special signal processing.
