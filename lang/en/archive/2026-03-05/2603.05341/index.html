The Quantum 'Cook': Superfluidity of the Quantum Hall Effect express

Original: "Observation of Superfluidity and Meissner Effect of Composite Bosons in GaAs Quantum Hall System"
· Yuanze Li, Renfei Wang, Jiahao Chen, Wenfeng Zhang, Adbhut Gupta, Kirk W. Baldwin, Loren Pfeiffer, Rui-Rui Du, Yang Liu, Tian Liang
arXiv:2603.05341 · 2026-03-05 · CC BY · 1 min
Mesoscale Strongly Correlated
Experiment proves: the quantum Hall effect is a superfluid, where electrons and magnetic flux quanta form exact pairs, like ingredients in a recipe.
Abstract

The quantum Hall effect (QHE) is thought to be a superfluid liquid of composite bosons—bound pairs of electrons and magnetic flux quanta. Direct evidence of superfluidity, like the Meissner effect, was long missing. In new experiments using Corbino disks, alongside the familiar quantized Laughlin charge pumping, a new phenomenon has been discovered: quantized charge accumulation following ΔQ/e = ν ΔΦ/Φ0. The system actively maintains a fixed ratio of electrons to flux, pulling in electrons as the field changes. This collective behavior occurs uniformly throughout the entire volume, like a flock of birds instantly adjusting to a gust of wind.

The quantum Hall effect has long been suspected of superfluidity — the ability to flow without friction. Imagine a cook following a recipe: for each unit of magnetic flux, he must add exactly a certain number of electrons. Electrons pair up with field quanta into couples that merge into a common cloud and flow without resistance.

In the experiment, a ring-shaped contact and an alternating magnetic field were used. Precise measurements revealed: as the field changed, the system pulled in exactly as many electrons as needed to maintain the proportion.

Charge accumulated not in a continuous stream, but in discrete portions — as if the cook counts ingredients one by one.

Accumulation occurred uniformly throughout the volume, not just at the edges. This means the entire 'team' of particles is working, not just individual edge currents.

This proves the superfluid nature: the substance acts as a single whole, without dissipating energy. Moreover, scientists saw how the system screens out external disturbances by managing thermal chaos — much like a cook not only follows the recipe but also keeps the kitchen tidy.

🎯 The most unexpected twist: this cook's talent only shows up in a flat, two-dimensional world. Add thickness — and the perfect recipe falls apart.

🎬 Such a self-tuning liquid resembles 'smart' materials from science fiction — for example, from Iain M. Banks' 'Culture' series, where matter itself adapts to conditions.
