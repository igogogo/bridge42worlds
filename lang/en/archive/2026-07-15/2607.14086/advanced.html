Brain Signals: Learning to Understand Without a Teacher express

Original: "Leveraging unlabelled data for generalizable neural population decoding"
· Ximeng Mao, Nanda H. Krishna, Avery Hee-Woon Ryoo, Matthew G. Perich, Guillaume Lajoie
arXiv:2607.14086 · 2026-07-15 · CC BY 4.0 · 1 min
Machine Learning Neurons and Cognition
The MOJO method teaches neural interfaces to understand the brain's electrical language just as a person learns a language without a textbook: first absorbing rhythms, then refining meaning from a handful of examples.
Abstract

MOJO is introduced—a framework for training models that tokenize neural activity at the spike level, merging self-supervised learning (masked autoencoder) with classic supervised learning. Across three datasets—monkey motor cortex during movement, multi-region mouse recordings in vision and decision tasks, and human electrocorticography during speech—MOJO outperformed purely supervised models, especially when labels were scarce (few-shot fine-tuning). It also builds more interpretable neural representations, boosting brain region classification and spike-statistic prediction without explicit optimization. MOJO generalizes to continuous ECoG signals, rivaling specialized neural foundation models. This work charts a path to flexibly and scalably leveraging unlabeled neural data for foundational brain models.

The brain communicates in short electrical clicks. Previously, to learn this language, thousands of examples with translations from the experimenter were needed. MOJO takes a different path: like learning a language through immersion, it first listens to the 'stream of consciousness' without any hints and finds recurring patterns on its own. Then, given a tiny dictionary—samples of signals linked to movements or words—it turns indistinct noise into clear commands.

Experiments on monkeys and mice showed that even one or two hints dramatically improve the results, outperforming classical algorithms hungry for data.

The most unexpected part: the algorithm, tuned on animals, without any changes began to distinguish speech rhythms in the human brain—as if learning one dialect, you start understanding a related one.

This cascade reduces uncertainty and allows working with any signals—from single neurons to large groups. It also clarifies the workings of living networks made of carbon. And since self-learning doesn't require expensive labels, vast accumulated archives of brain recordings can be put to use—bringing closer the era of assistive devices for people with paralysis. The idea is not new: as early as John von Neumann pondered the kinship of mind and machine.

🎯 In a second of recording from a hundred neurons, up to 20,000 electrical discharges flash by—a true orchestra where each impulse plays its own note.

🎬 Technology for directly reading motor intentions resembles the neural interfaces from the movie 'Avatar,' where a human mind controls the avatar's body.
