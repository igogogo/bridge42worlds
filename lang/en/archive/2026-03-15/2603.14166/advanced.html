Spacetime in Lenses: How Einstein's Physics Enables Super-Resolution express

Original: "Minkowski-Space Modeling of Hyperbolic Lenses"
· Enrico Maria Renzi, Simon Yves, Sveinung Erland, Diana Strickland, Eitan Bachmat, Andrea Alù
arXiv:2603.14166 · 2026-03-15 · CC BY 4.0 · 1 min
Optics Mesoscale
Physicists used the geometry of curved spacetime to create lenses that discern nanoscale details.
Abstract

Extreme anisotropy in hyperbolic materials unlocks ultimate wave compression, but at a cost: misaligned phase and energy flows, complicating device modeling. The solution: a Minkowski-space framework shows this challenge is geometric in nature. Embedding the anisotropy into an effective Lorentzian metric yields a rational design method for hyperbolic interfaces and lenses. Analytical transfer functions and resolution limits were derived, enabling ultra-high numerical apertures and deep sub-diffraction focusing. Full-wave simulations of a planar polariton lens from van der Waals materials in the mid-infrared confirmed the approach.

A river flows in one direction, but a wave on its surface travels in the opposite direction. The same splitting happens in hyperbolic materials: the energy of a light wave and its fronts diverge, preventing a sharp lens from being created. Physicists tamed this chaos by applying the geometry of spacetime from special relativity, created by Einstein based on the work of Lorentz. The material's properties are transformed into a metric, where the speed of light plays the role of a fundamental constant, and everything falls into place.

In these materials, wave surfaces resemble a saddle—stretched in one direction and compressed in another.

Scientists derived universal formulas that allow designing lenses that focus light with enormous precision, bypassing the natural blur inherent to any lens. Such optics distinguish details hundreds of times finer than the wavelength—for example, a magnifying glass capable of reading text on an atom. In the experiment, a flat lens was simulated for the mid-infrared range, using polaritons—quantum oscillations of the crystal lattice.

🎯 The same equations that predict the curvature of spacetime around stars helped design lenses capable of distinguishing molecular-sized details.
