A newborn neutron star's pulse heard in a 90s gamma-ray burst express

Original: "Detection of a Millisecond Periodicity in BATSE Short Gamma-Ray Bursts"
· Run-Chao Chen, Jun Yang, Yi-Han Iris Yin, Bin-Bin Zhang
arXiv:2601.12108 · 2026-01-17 · CC BY · 1 min
High Energy
Astronomers found ultrafast pulsations in archival gamma-ray burst data, revealing the spin of a newborn magnetar.
Abstract

In short gamma-ray bursts, quasi-periodic oscillations (nearly regular pulsations) were previously noticed, hinting at the rotation of a super-dense remnant after a merger. Analyzing an archive of 532 short bursts from the BATSE detector, a statistically significant case was found: GRB 960616, where the main 30-millisecond emission is modulated at a frequency of 1100 Hz, most strongly above 320 keV, with an amplitude of about 47%. The fraction of energy involved in such modulation does not exceed 8%. This is like a cosmic strobe light and confirms that millisecond periodicity can manifest during the birth of a millisecond magnetar.

Short gamma-ray bursts — instantaneous, but most powerful explosions — are born from the mergers of neutron stars (the superdense remnants of dead stars). Sometimes after the collision, a rapidly spinning magnetic top remains — a magnetar, whose field is billions of times stronger than anything that can be created in a laboratory.

Sifting through data from a 90s satellite, scientists stumbled upon the flash GRB 960616. It lasted only 30 milliseconds, but its gamma radiation pulsated 1100 times per second — like the feverish heartbeat of a cosmic body. The rhythm signature stood out especially clearly in the hardest gamma rays, revealing the spin of a recently born pulsar — a lighthouse star, whose beam scratches across space 1100 times per second.

Each rotation of the star fits into 0.0009 seconds — a moment in which light can travel only 270 kilometers. Such a discovery is only the second case so far where a gamma-ray burst reveals the pulse of a newborn magnetar. By hunting for such signals, astronomers will be able to measure the spin of the most distant and fastest cosmic 'spinning tops'.

🎯 During those same 30 milliseconds that the flash lasted, Earth barely noticeably wobbled, while the neutron star made 33 full rotations.
