Sifting for Gold: Optimizing AI Pipelines with Database Techniques

Original: "Query-Centric Optimization of AI Workflows via Approximate Query Processing and Proxy Models"
· Huayi Wang, Jun Xu, Gromit Yeuk-Yin Chan
arXiv:2607.00254 · 2026-06-30 · CC BY · 4 min
cs.DB
Classic database tricks—approximate aggregation and surrogate models—slash the number of calls to expensive neural networks by up to 19x without sacrificing quality.
Abstract

Many AI workflows — from post-training large language models to agent-based reasoning — can be framed as queries with an expensive predicate evaluated by a big model. It turns out that classic database techniques — approximate query processing (AQP) and proxy models (PM) — can reduce those costly calls without touching the models themselves. AQP works like online aggregation: it repeatedly samples records, updates the estimate along with a confidence interval, and stops once the interval shrinks to a predefined error bound. PM — a compact decision tree on a CPU — is trained on a small labeled dataset and filters out records where its prediction confidence is high. On TPC-DS queries, AQP drops the number of calls to 10–15% (an 85–90% reduction) with aggregation error under 10%; PM cuts calls by 60–70%. In LLM pipelines (math, instruction following, code generation), AQP halts after using just 20–50% of the calls with less than 5% accuracy loss on structured tasks; PM accelerates reward model evaluation up to 19× while keeping accuracy loss below 10%.

Context

When fine-tuning models like Llama 3, you might generate up to 32 candidate answers for each training example—only the one with the highest score makes it to the next round. Rinse and repeat six times, and the quality model gets called tens of millions of times. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become standard, but its computational price tag is staggering. Reframing the whole process as an SQL query—something like `SELECT * ORDER BY score LIMIT 1`—makes it obvious: you don’t have to run the pricey evaluator on every single candidate. That insight opens up a whole new space for optimization at the intersection of Large Language Models and probability theory.

Methods

The authors propose two complementary approaches. The first is an approximate query processing (AQP) strategy: think of it like prospecting—you take soil samples, estimate the average gold content, and once the confidence interval narrows to your target, you stop digging. Computationally, that means progressively sampling rows, calling the oracle on each, updating a running estimate of the aggregate (sum, average, count), and halting when the result stabilizes. For ranking queries with a LIMIT clause, the criterion shifts to order statistics: sampling ends when the kth best result stops budging. The second approach is a surrogate filter model (PM): you train a compact decision tree on shallow features (answer length, presence of formulas, special characters) using a small subset of oracle-labeled data. Unlike neural network proxies that need GPU inference for every candidate, a decision tree runs on a CPU in milliseconds. The tree then rapidly scans all records and passes only “borderline” cases to the expensive oracle—anything obvious gets discarded or accepted on the spot. This creates cascaded gating: if the spread of predictions within a group is tiny, the whole group is skipped; otherwise, only the likely weak answers get weeded out.

Results

On TPC-DS benchmarks with aggregation queries, the AQP strategy hit less than 10% error while using only 10–15% of the oracle calls—a savings of 85–90%. On real fine-tuning pipelines for LLaMA and Qwen models, covering math reasoning (GSM8K) and code generation (HumanEval+), AQP finished sampling with just 20–50% of the calls and an accuracy loss under 5%. Meanwhile, the surrogate PM slashed scoring time by up to 19x—for example, from 1079 seconds to 56 seconds for LLaMA-8B—with a quality drop below 10%. The stochastic estimation converges faster when the data is more uniform. On open-ended instructions (General), the savings were more modest but still significant: throughput improved by 1.2–2.5x. Confidence intervals behaved predictably: they narrowed quickly on uniform label distributions and needed more samples when things were skewed. Crucially, neither strategy modifies the underlying models or requires a database engine rewrite.

Implications

The results show that classic query optimizations—approximate computation and proxy filtering—get a second life in the age of LLMs. They’re agnostic to the internals of the evaluator and can be bolted onto any existing pipeline. This busts the myth that speeding up AI demands new architectures: often, you just need to rethink the data flow. For data scientists, it means lower energy costs and cheaper experiments; for developers, a drop-in tool that doesn’t require deep refactoring.

Future development

Down the road, such query-centric approaches could extend to multi-step agentic reasoning and retrieval-augmented generation. Instead of chaining models across every document, the system could—like a seasoned geologist—rule out barren patches after a quick glance. Pairing the idea with adaptive query routing to models of different costs would tighten the compute budget even further.

Impact

Areas affected: language model training, large-scale analytical pipelines, automatic data labeling, recommendation systems.

Next steps

Integrate these strategies into database engines (e.g., DuckDB) and test them on more complex agentic scenarios where LLM call chains involve external tools.

Key open problems

The paper tackles the challenge of scaling RLHF and making large-model inference more efficient—a critical issue for sustainable AI. It also throws a bridge to the unsolved problem of automatically optimizing declarative pipelines where computation cost depends on data distribution.

🎯 The most eye-popping result: for the Qwen3-32B code generator, the proxy model cut the reward scorer’s runtime from 175 seconds to just 10 seconds—an 18x speedup—while keeping accuracy (pass@1) within 1% of the full run.

🎬 While there’s no direct analog, the idea of “smart sifting” echoes the concept of using limited resources to extract the most value—a timeless theme in engineering fiction, from ore smelters to data refineries.

Key numbers

  • scoring time speedup: up to 19x
  • reduction in oracle calls with AQP: 85–90% on uniform distributions
  • accuracy loss in math tasks: <5%
  • fraction sampled by AQP on LLM pipelines: 20–50%
  • quality drop on general instructions: ~15% from full scoring
