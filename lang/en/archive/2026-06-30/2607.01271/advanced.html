A Bridge That Only Holds the Finite: On Strong Structural Completeness

Original: "On Strong Structural Completeness of Varieties and Quasivarieties"
· Metropolitan Telecommunications, NewYork USA Alex Citkin
arXiv:2607.01271 · 2026-06-30 · CC BY 4.0 · 3 min
math.LO cs.LO
For many logical systems, a rule’s admissibility doesn’t guarantee its derivability—and when infinite rules enter the picture, the chasm becomes uncrossable.
Abstract

We explore how rich a variety’s construction can be if we use only free algebras as building blocks. Structural completeness (SCpl) means that from them, you can build the entire variety as a quasivariety; strong structural completeness (SSCpl) means you can build it as a prevariety (a more restricted tool set). It is proved: for quasivarieties of finite type with the congruence extension property (CEP), generated by finite algebras, the presence of an infinite subdirectly irreducible algebra blocks strong completeness. For congruence-semidistributive varieties of finite type, assembled from finite algebras, strong completeness is equivalent to tabularity—reducibility to a single finite “template” algebra. Hence, Dummett’s and Medvedev’s logics are built as SCpl but not as SSCpl. The concept of primitiveness is introduced (a property stable in subvarieties), and it is shown that in primitive congruence-distributive varieties, only tabular subvarieties are strongly primitive.

1

Context

1971. Logician W. Pogorzelski introduces structural completeness: a logical system is complete when every admissible rule (one that adds no new theorems) can be derived from the axioms. Think of a sturdy bridge over a roaring river—everything that preserves truth is already built into the rules of the game. Classical propositional logic has this property, but for many non‑classical logics—intuitionistic, modal—the bridge proves shaky. In the algebraic picture, a logic is represented by a class of models: algebras. Structural completeness means the variety is generated by its free algebras. Yet what happens when rules become infinite? Alex Citkin’s work supplies the answer by introducing κ‑structural completeness, where κ is a cardinal bounding the number of premises and variables. It turns out that once κ > ω, the bridge almost always collapses.

Methods

The analysis of strong structural completeness uses bounding κ‑quasi‑equations. Construct an infinite set of equations: for each unequal pair of elements in an algebra, add a condition tying them to a distinguished pair from the monolith. If the algebra is finite, all these conditions hold and force the conclusion. For infinite algebras one can always find a refuting valuation—a trick that generalizes Jankov’s method. The crucial tool is characteristic κ‑quasi‑equations for indecomposable algebras, which act like atoms in the structure of the variety.

Results

Main result: if a congruence‑distributive variety of finite type is generated by finite algebras, then κ‑structural completeness for any κ > ω is equivalent to tabularity. In plain terms, the bridge holds only when the whole system can be compressed into a single finite algebra. For structurally complete logics like LC and ML, whose model classes contain infinite indecomposable algebras, this spells failure: an admissible infinite rule (e.g., a bounding ω‑quasi‑equation) is not derivable. Hence, generating such logics from finite models does not guarantee a finitary consequence relation.

Implications

The finding throws a bridge from pure logic to the foundations of mathematics: infinite inference rules, however natural they appear, almost always shatter the harmony between syntax and semantics. Strong structural completeness is a rare bird—it nests only in finitely generated systems.

Future development

Open problems include axiomatizing strongly structurally complete fragments of intuitionistic logic. Would adding a bounding rule to Visser’s rules give all admissible rules? This is like an optimal‑basis search. Also awaiting study are primitive varieties—those where every subvariety is strongly complete.

Impact

The results matter not only to logic but to computer science (type theory, verification), where infinite rules crop up in higher‑order systems.

Next steps

Test whether Visser’s bounding rule really axiomatizes strong completeness; extend the methods to other non‑classical logics.

Key open problems

The work ties strong structural completeness to questions of finite axiomatizability and decidability, and shines a light on the fine line between finitary and non‑finitary consequence.

🎯 A Heyting algebra of order ω+2 looks like a countable chain with two extra elements tacked on top. That microscopic difference is enough to render an infinite rule non‑derivable.

\{ (x_j \leftrightarrow x_k) \to z \approx (x_j \leftrightarrow x_k) \to w \mid 0 \le j < k < \omega \} \Rightarrow z\approx w
This rule is true on all finite Heyting algebras, but is refuted on the countable algebra of order ω+2.

Key numbers

  • ω: the first infinite cardinal (countable infinity)
  • ω⁺: the next cardinal after ω, threshold for non‑finitarity
  • ω+2: order type of the counterexample algebra (just 2 elements more than a countable chain)
  • number of variables in the bounding rule: finite (less than ω)
  • rank of the free algebra: ω, countable
