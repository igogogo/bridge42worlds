A trait like beak size changes from generation to generation: selection picks up successful variants, while random changes introduce novelty. The Price equation breaks this process into two parts—selection and transmission.

In practice: Scientists use it to predict how quickly bacteria will become resistant to a new antibiotic, or how viruses mutate to evade immunity.
The Price equation was derived by George Price, who worked in London in the late 1960s. Originally a chemist, he became interested in altruism and evolution. In 1970, he published a paper presenting a universal formula describing evolutionary change. Its beauty lies in its generality—it applies not only to genes but to any reproducing entities: ideas, firms, cultural practices. The formula proved so fundamental that the renowned biologist Steven Frank wrote, 'If you have Price's theorem, you need nothing else.'

How it works

In biology, the equation is used to analyze evolutionary experiments: for example, measure shell size in snails, then select the largest ones, let them reproduce, and calculate how much of the increase is due to selection alone and how much to offspring variability.

💡 The Price equation is a mathematical identity, meaning it is always true, like 2+2=4. But that is precisely what makes it such a powerful tool for uncovering the mechanisms of evolution.
Imagine each generation of birds is a print run of a cookbook with a beak recipe. Some recipes give an advantage, so those books are reprinted more often. When copying, occasional typos occur—small changes. The Price equation clearly separates how much change is due to selection (which recipes survived) and how much due to transmission errors. This allows you to calculate how much the average beak size will change in the next generation.

💡 Price himself suffered from depression and took his own life in 1975; his contribution was only appreciated years later. Today, the Price equation is one of the most cited results in evolutionary biology.
The Price equation describes the change in the mean value of a trait in a population from one generation to the next. It is written as Δz̄ = Cov(w, z)/w̄ + E(w Δz)/w̄, where z̄ is the mean trait value, w is fitness (number of surviving offspring), Cov is the covariance between fitness and the trait, and the second term is the average change in the trait during transmission, weighted by fitness. The first term describes selection: if individuals with larger trait values leave more offspring, the mean increases. The second term is the contribution from imperfect transmission (mutations, measurement errors, etc.). The equation requires no assumptions about the genetic nature of the trait, so it applies to any evolutionary system, including cultural evolution.

Discovery

George Price, an American scientist who moved to England in 1967, became fascinated with the problem of the evolution of altruism. Working with William Hamilton, he sought to provide a rigorous mathematical formulation of natural selection. In 1970, his paper 'Selection and Covariance' was published in Nature, presenting the equation for the first time. Price showed that trait change could be decomposed into a component related to the correlation between trait and fitness, and a component due to changes during inheritance. His work remained little known for a long time but later became a cornerstone in quantitative genetics and game theory.

How it works

The Price equation is widely used in theoretical biology to model quantitative traits, in ecology to analyze species interactions, and in social sciences to study the spread of innovations. Limits of applicability: it describes only one-step change in the mean, not dynamics over time. Moreover, applying it requires precise data on individual traits and fitness, which is rarely feasible in natural populations.

Caveats

Does not predict long-term dynamics without additional assumptions; Is a tautology—does not provide a physical mechanism for selection; Requires data on absolute fitness, which is difficult to measure; Does not distinguish the causes of correlation between trait and fitness

\Delta \bar{z} = \frac{ \text{Cov}(w_i, z_i) }{ \bar{w} } + \frac{ \mathbb{E}(w_i \Delta z_i) }{ \bar{w} }
Δz̄ — change in the mean trait value in the population per generation; Cov(w_i, z_i) — covariance between fitness w_i (number of offspring from individual i) and trait value z_i; w̄ — mean fitness of the population; E(w_i Δz_i) — expected value of the product of fitness and the change in the trait during transmission (Δz_i — difference between offspring and parent trait). The first term reflects the effect of selection, the second — distortions during inheritance.
