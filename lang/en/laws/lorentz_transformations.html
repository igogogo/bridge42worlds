Imagine a train traveling almost at the speed of light. A passenger on the train and a person on the platform measure the length of the same carriage and the time on the clocks — and get different results! The Lorentz transformations show how these measurements are related so that the speed of light remains the same for both observers. It is as if space and time compress and stretch, adjusting to the motion.

How it works

In everyday life we do not notice this, but at speeds of tens and hundreds of kilometers per second the effects become noticeable. For example, in particle accelerators, protons are accelerated to near-light speeds, and without relativistic corrections their trajectories cannot be calculated. Even the lifetime of unstable particles (muons) in cosmic rays increases because they move at high speed.

💡 GPS satellites move at high speed, and their clocks tick slightly slower than those on Earth. Without taking the Lorentz transformations into account, the navigator would accumulate an error of several kilometers every day!
In the late 19th century, physicists faced a contradiction: Maxwell's equations predicted a constant speed of light, while classical mechanics required velocity addition. Hendrik Lorentz, trying to explain the results of the Michelson-Morley experiment, derived mathematical relationships linking coordinates and time in different inertial frames of reference. Later, Henri Poincaré gave them their modern form and name. Essentially, it is a 'recipe' for recalculating spatial and temporal intervals for observers moving relative to each other, which works at any speeds, but is especially important at speeds close to the speed of light.

The Lorentz transformations are linear transformations of the coordinates of four-dimensional spacetime between two inertial frames of reference moving at a constant velocity v relative to each other. For motion along the x axis, they are written as t' = γ(t - vx/c²), x' = γ(x - vt), y' = y, z' = z, where γ = 1/√(1 - v²/c²). They ensure the invariance of the interval s² = c²t² - x² - y² - z² and the constancy of the speed of light in all frames. This is the mathematical foundation of special relativity.

Discovery

In 1892–1904, Hendrik Lorentz developed these equations, independently starting from the FitzGerald length contraction hypothesis, to explain the null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment. In 1905, Henri Poincaré was the first to give the complete mathematical form and showed that they form a group, naming them 'Lorentz transformations'. In the same year, Albert Einstein derived them from two postulates — the constancy of the speed of light and the equivalence of inertial frames.

How it works

They are used in all problems of special relativity to recalculate physical quantities between frames of reference. They allow for the correct description of electrodynamics of moving media, relativistic kinematics, time dilation and length contraction effects. Limits of applicability: only for inertial frames; in the presence of gravity, they are replaced by the more general transformations of general relativity.

Caveats

The concept of simultaneity is relative: events that are simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in another.; The twin paradox requires accounting for acceleration during turnaround, which goes beyond the Lorentz transformations.; Compatibility with quantum mechanics at extreme scales remains an open problem.
