Gross–Pitaevskii equationequation

27 Related articles

The Gross–Pitaevskii equation describes the behavior of an ultracold cloud of atoms merged into a single quantum droplet. It predicts how this droplet moves, twists into vortices, and interacts with its surroundings.

In practice: It is used to design atom lasers and calculate the properties of superfluid helium—a liquid that can flow without friction through the finest capillaries.
In 1961, Eugene Gross and Lev Pitaevskii independently derived the equation that became the main tool of ultracold atomic physics. In a Bose–Einstein condensate, thousands of particles occupy a single quantum state, forming a macroscopic wave. The equation resembles the Schrödinger equation of quantum mechanics, but with an important addition: a term describing the 'friction' of particles against each other (nonlinearity). Thanks to it, quantum vortices—tiny whirlwinds that align themselves into neat lattices—are born in the condensate. By solving the equation, physicists predict such patterns and verify them in experiments with rubidium, sodium, and potassium.

How it works

In a typical experiment, lasers create a magnetic trap for rubidium atoms. The equation predicts that if the trap is suddenly turned off, the cloud expands in a distinctive mushroom-like shape—just what scientists observe. The same calculations explain why superfluid helium does not freeze even at zero temperature: its atoms obey collective wave dynamics.

💡 If you 'pinch' a condensate with a focused laser beam, solitons—solitary waves that do not spread out over time—will run along it. It is as if a stone thrown into a pond produced not ripples, but a single hump gliding across the water unchanged.
When a gas of atoms is cooled almost to absolute zero, all atoms begin to 'march in step'—a Bose–Einstein condensate (a single quantum wave) forms. The Gross–Pitaevskii equation shows how this wave changes over time. It accounts for three things: the spreading of the wave (like ripples on water from a thrown stone), the action of external fields (such as a magnetic trap holding the atoms), and the influence of collisions between atoms—like a slight friction inside the cloud. By solving the equation, a physicist gets a 'snapshot' of the condensate: where the density is higher, where microscopic vortices are swirling.

They live for tens of milliseconds and resemble miniature stars made of ultracold gas.
The Gross–Pitaevskii (GP) equation is a nonlinear Schrödinger equation describing a Bose–Einstein condensate in the mean-field approximation at zero temperature: iħ ∂ψ/∂t = -(ħ²/(2m))∇²ψ + V_ext(r)ψ + g|ψ|²ψ. Here ψ is the macroscopic wave function of the condensate, m is the atomic mass, V_ext is the external potential (e.g., a magnetic trap), g = 4πħ²a/m is the interaction constant, depending on the s-wave scattering length a. The equation is derived from many-body theory in the limit of weak interaction (na³ ≪ 1) and yields the ground state, collective excitations, and dynamics of the condensate. It applies to ultracold gases and superfluid helium-4.

Discovery

The equation was proposed in 1961 independently by Eugene Gross and Lev Pitaevskii. Gross proceeded from a kinetic approach to superfluidity, Pitaevskii from methods of quantum field theory. Both sought to describe the coherent behavior of many Bose particles. For a long time the work remained a theoretical curiosity, but in 1995, when Eric Cornell, Carl Wieman, and Wolfgang Ketterle first obtained a Bose condensate in a dilute gas (rubidium, sodium), the GP equation instantly became indispensable. Today it serves as the basis for modeling superfluid systems, atom lasers, and quantum simulators.

How it works

The equation is solved numerically to calculate the shape and dynamics of a condensate in traps, the interference of two condensates, and the formation of vortex lattices. It is valid for weakly interacting, dilute condensates at temperatures well below the critical temperature (typically nanokelvins). At higher temperatures or stronger interaction, one must account for thermal clouds and three-body losses, which go beyond the mean-field approximation.

Caveats

The equation does not describe the thermal cloud of non-condensed atoms—a generalization to finite temperatures is needed (Zaremba–Nikuni approach).; At high densities, three-body collisions become important, leading to atom losses and energy shifts.; For rapidly rotating condensates, quantum fluctuations can exceed the mean field, requiring the inclusion of Lee–Huang–Yang corrections.
