Neural Network Oscillations

2

The brain is not silence but a pulsating rhythm. Neural network oscillations are waves of electrical activity that run through the brain like ripples on water, and without them, sleep, memory, or attention would be impossible.

In practice: Reading brain rhythms (EEG) helps diagnose epilepsy, control prostheses through brain-computer interfaces, and develop therapies for attention disorders.
Neural network oscillations are periodic bursts of total electrical activity of large populations of neurons. They arise due to mutual excitation and inhibition between cells. Rhythms are distinguished by frequency: delta (0.5–4 Hz) — deep sleep, theta (4–8 Hz) — drowsy state, alpha (8–13 Hz) — relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed, beta (13–30 Hz) — active attention, gamma (30–100 Hz) — information processing. These oscillations are not just noise, but a functional tool: they synchronize remote areas for data exchange (the binding problem), as if two computers tuned to a common carrier frequency.

History

In 1924, psychiatrist Hans Berger first recorded the electrical activity of the human brain by attaching electrodes to the scalp. He saw waves that he called the alpha rhythm. Since then, scientists have found many different rhythms (delta, theta, gamma, and others) and realized that they are connected with our thoughts, emotions, and memory.

How it works

Neurons communicate through electrical impulses. When a large group of neurons sends impulses at about the same time, a wave arises that can be detected from outside. Strong connections between neurons help maintain the rhythm, like a clock pendulum that is pushed in time.

💡 In newborns, the alpha rhythm is almost absent and only develops by the age of three, while slow waves of deep sleep help 'wash out' toxic proteins accumulated during the day from the brain.
Oscillations (fluctuations) in neural networks are when many nerve cells (neurons) in the brain begin to turn on and off synchronously, creating rhythmic waves. It's like an orchestra where each instrument plays its note, but together they make music. Such rhythms are slow during sleep and fast when solving problems. They help different parts of the brain communicate with each other.

Oscillations in neural networks are a macroscopic phenomenon generated by the collective dynamics of excitatory and inhibitory neurons. Mathematically, they are modeled by systems of differential equations, such as the Wilson-Cowan model (excitation-inhibition) or the Kuramoto model for coupled oscillators. At the micro level, oscillations arise from reverberating activity in closed circuits, pacemaker neurons, and resonant properties of membranes. These rhythms modulate synaptic plasticity, thereby controlling learning and memory. For example, theta rhythm in the hippocampus organizes sequences of place cell activation, while gamma rhythm promotes precise spike synchronization during sensory information processing. Disturbances in oscillations are biomarkers of neurological diseases (epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, schizophrenia).

History

History began with the invention of electroencephalography. Hans Berger performed the first human EEG recording in 1924, but recognition came only after 1934 when Edgar Adrian repeated the experiments. Berger described alpha and beta rhythms. In the 1930s–50s, William Grey Walter and others studied the delta rhythm in brain tumors. In the 1960s, computer analysis of EEG allowed the extraction of evoked potentials. A breakthrough was the discovery of local field potentials (LFP) and the link between oscillations and cognitive processes: in the 1980s, Wolf Singer proposed the synchronization hypothesis for feature binding. Today, optogenetics and modern imaging methods allow targeted manipulation of oscillations.

How it works

Oscillations are maintained by a balance of excitation (via glutamatergic neurons) and inhibition (via GABAergic interneurons). The key mechanism is reverberating circuits: excitation from one group of neurons activates inhibitory cells, which after a short delay suppress the first group, after which the cycle repeats. The frequency of oscillations is determined by the time constants of synapses and the length of chains. For a global rhythm to arise, synchronization through gap junctions (electrical synapses) and a common synchronizing projection from subcortical structures, such as the thalamus, is necessary. Gamma rhythm (30–80 Hz) is locally generated by pyramidal neurons and fast-spiking interneurons, which create a temporal window for precise spike synchronization. Theta rhythm coordinates the interaction between the hippocampus and cortex, organizing temporal compression of sequences. From the perspective of nonlinear dynamics, the transition to a synchronized state is a phase transition described by order parameters.
