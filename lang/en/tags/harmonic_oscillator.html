harmonic oscillator

A swing, pushed very gently, always returns to the same rhythm. This is a harmonic oscillator — a system that sways back and forth around a rest point with an unchanging beat.

In practice: A quartz crystal in a wristwatch vibrates exactly 32,768 times per second, setting the pace for the hands. The same law gives lasers their pure color and keeps bridges steady when wind blows over them.
The harmonic oscillator is the basic model for almost all of physics. It appears in mechanics (pendulum, spring), acoustics (strings, air columns), optics (light as an oscillating electric field), and quantum theory (vibrations of atomic bonds). The reason for this universality is simple: near a stable equilibrium, the potential energy curve of any system resembles a parabola — and that is the 'signature' of an oscillator. The restoring force grows in direct proportion to the displacement, giving rise to sinusoidal motion.

History

Galileo noticed a swaying chandelier in a cathedral and realized that the period of a pendulum depends only on its length. Later, Christiaan Huygens built the first pendulum clock in 1656, making timekeeping tens of times more accurate.

How it works

A swing has its own frequency — the round-trip time. Frequency is determined by two things: how strongly the restoring force 'springs' back and how heavy the oscillating object is. A heavy child on long ropes swings more slowly than a light one on short ropes. In a car, the springs and shock absorbers are also an oscillator, but they dampen vibrations to make the ride smooth.

💡 Surgeons use a harmonic scalpel vibrating 55,500 times per second. It cuts soft tissue and simultaneously seals blood vessels — coagulation without excess heat.
The pendulum of a clock swings smoothly and predictably. A harmonic oscillator is anything that, when displaced from a comfortable position, receives a restoring force pulling it back, and by inertia overshoots, starting a cycle. Imagine a mass on a spring: stretch it — the spring pulls back; the mass gains speed — overshoots and compresses the spring from the other side. Energy dances between stored tension and motion. Every oscillator has a favorite rhythm — its natural frequency. Push it in time, and it will ring louder.

A classical harmonic oscillator obeys the equation d²x/dt² + ω²x = 0, its solution being x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ). Energy alternates between kinetic and potential, remaining constant in the absence of friction. In quantum mechanics, this is one of the few exactly solvable problems. The Hamiltonian is H = p²/(2m) + ½mω²x², energy levels are quantized: E_n = ℏω (n + ½). The fraction ½ℏω is the zero-point energy, which does not disappear even at absolute zero. The ladder operators a and a† raise and lower the quantum number n — the entire quantum field theory is built on this language.

History

In 1602, Galileo described the pendulum, initiating the quantitative study of oscillations. Euler and Bernoulli later formulated the linear oscillator equation. In 1900, Max Planck used the quantization of oscillator energy to derive the blackbody spectrum — this step began the quantum era. Erwin Schrödinger solved the quantum harmonic oscillator in 1926, and the algebraic method with creation and annihilation operators was proposed by Paul Dirac. Ludwig Boltzmann applied the classical oscillator to molecular heat capacity, linking atomic motion to temperature.

How it works

Near the stable equilibrium point x₀, the potential V(x) is expanded in a Taylor series: V(x) ≈ V(x₀) + ½k(x−x₀)², where k = V''(x₀). The force F = −kV'(x) = −k(x−x₀) is linear, and the oscillation frequency ω = √(k/m). In the quantum case, position and momentum become operators; the Schrödinger equation yields eigenfunctions expressed via Hermite polynomials. The equidistant energy spectrum is the reason why fields in physics can be represented as a set of independent oscillators for each mode.

\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + \omega^2 x = 0
x — displacement, ω — angular frequency (ω = 2πf, where f is the frequency in hertz)
E_n = \hbar \omega \left( n + \frac{1}{2} \right)
E_n — energy of the state with quantum number n (n = 0, 1, 2, ...), ℏ — reduced Planck constant (ℏ ≈ 1.054571817×10⁻³⁴ J·s)
