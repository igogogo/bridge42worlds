Tag

random matrix

A crowd of people in a square moves chaotically, but the overall density at each point is predictable. A random matrix—a table of numbers governed by probabilistic laws—helps capture order in apparent disorder.

In practice: Random matrices are used in signal processing: they separate important data from noise in radar and medical tomographs.
A random matrix is a matrix whose elements are random variables with a given joint distribution. The best-known are the Wigner Gaussian ensembles: orthogonal (GOE), unitary (GUE), and symplectic (GSE), differing in symmetry under time reversal. The main result is the Wigner distribution for spacings between eigenvalues, which shows level repulsion: the probability of finding two very close values is small.

History

The idea was proposed by physicist Eugene Wigner in the 1950s to describe the energy levels of heavy atomic nuclei, where exact calculations are impossible.

How it works

Instead of calculating every interaction in a complex system (e.g., among all neutrons and protons in a nucleus), scientists replace them with random numbers, preserving only the overall symmetry properties. The distribution of the resulting numbers exactly matches experiment.

💡 The distribution of gaps between consecutive buses in some cities also resembles the Wigner distribution, because drivers tend to avoid bunching.

History

In the 1950s, Eugene Wigner, while investigating resonance spectra of heavy nuclei, proposed replacing unknown elements of the Hamiltonian matrix with random numbers. Together with Dyson, Mehta, and others, he developed the statistical theory of spectra. Later, a deep connection emerged with eigenvalue distributions in numerical methods, number theory (distribution of zeros of the Riemann zeta function), and growth of random surfaces.

How it works

The eigenvalues of a random matrix behave like a distribution of charged particles on a wire repelling each other logarithmically. As a result, they are spaced unevenly: the probability of a small spacing s between them is proportional to s^β (β is the repulsion index). The spacing distribution is exactly described by the Wigner formula for GOE: p(s) = (πs/2) exp(−π s²/4). For comparison, a Poisson process (independent levels) would yield p(s) = exp(−s).

p(s) = \frac{\pi s}{2} \, e^{-\pi s^2 / 4}
s — spacing between neighboring levels, normalized so that the mean spacing is 1; p(s) — probability density
P(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N) = C_N \, e^{-\sum \lambda_i^2} \prod_{i<j} |\lambda_i - \lambda_j|^\beta
λ_i — eigenvalues; C_N — normalization constant; β = 1 for orthogonal ensemble, 2 for unitary, 4 for symplectic
