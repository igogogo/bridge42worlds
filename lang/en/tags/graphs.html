Graphs

16

Points connected by lines, like cities on a map with roads.

In practice: Graphs help find the shortest path in a navigator and recommend new friends on social networks.
A graph is a mathematical structure consisting of vertices and the edges connecting them. Unlike a geographical map, what matters here is not the shape of the lines but the presence of a connection and, possibly, its weight (for example, road length or travel time). This abstraction allows applying the same search algorithms to social networks, traffic flows, and even chemical reaction chains.

History

The idea of graphs was born in 1736 when mathematician Leonhard Euler solved the puzzle of the Seven Bridges of Königsberg. He realized to look only at the landmasses and the bridges between them, ignoring distances.

How it works

A graph works as a simplified model of connections. When you need to figure out how to get around traffic jams, the navigator turns streets into edges and intersections into vertices, and quickly iterates through options. The same logic finds friends of friends on a social network.

💡 In Facebook's social graph, the average distance between any two users is only 3.5 steps—this is the 'six degrees of separation' theory in practice.
A metro map is a classic graph: stations serve as vertices (points), and track lines as edges (connections). On such a drawing, the curves of the line don't matter—only which points it connects. A graph turns the tangle of real connections into a clear pattern of circles and lines.

A graph is a mathematical object defined by a set of vertices and a set of edges (pairs of vertices). Distinguish between directed graphs (edges have a direction) and undirected ones. Additional structures are introduced: weighted graphs (each edge is assigned a weight), multigraphs (multiple edges allowed), etc. Graphs serve as the foundation for network analysis, algorithm theory, coding theory, and statistical physics. Complex systems break down into nodes and their interactions—just as the first navigator reduced the street maze to points and lines.

History

The birth of graph theory is linked to Leonhard Euler's work 'The solution of a problem relating to the geometry of position' (1736), where he demonstrated the impossibility of traversing all seven bridges of Königsberg without crossing one twice. The term 'graph' was introduced by K. König in 1936. In the 20th century, graph theory flourished thanks to problems in discrete optimization and computer science.

How it works

Graphs model systems of pairwise interactions. Analysis relies on adjacency and incidence matrices. Graph properties are studied through invariants: connectivity, cyclomatic number, chromatic number. Efficient algorithms on graphs (shortest path, maximum flow) are based on dynamic programming and greedy strategies. In physics, graphs are used in diagrammatic techniques (Feynman diagrams are directed graphs) and in the study of random media.
