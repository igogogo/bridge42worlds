Bayesian inference

5

You update your beliefs upon seeing new evidence. A detective at a crime scene recalculates the suspects' odds with every new detail found.

In practice: Spam filters learn from your markings, medical tests refine diagnoses based on new symptoms.
Bayesian inference is a mathematical method for estimating the probability of a hypothesis based on new observations. It requires three components: a prior probability (initial degree of belief in the hypothesis), a likelihood function (how probable the data are given the hypothesis is true), and a normalization constant (the total probability of the data under all possible hypotheses). The result is the posterior probability—what you believe after considering the evidence. Unlike classical statistics, the Bayesian approach directly answers the question "What is the probability that the hypothesis is true given these data?" rather than "What is the probability of obtaining such data if the hypothesis is true?"

History

The idea is named after Thomas Bayes, an 18th-century English clergyman who laid the foundation for updating probabilities. Later, Pierre-Simon Laplace developed it into a coherent theory.

How it works

You start with an initial guess (how likely the hypothesis is before the experiment). Then you obtain evidence and look at how often such evidence occurs if the hypothesis is true, and how often if it is false. These two numbers are multiplied by the initial probability and give an updated estimate.

💡 British codebreaker Alan Turing used Bayesian methods to crack the Enigma code during World War II, assigning probabilities to various machine settings based on intercepted messages.
Bayesian inference is a way to recalculate the probabilities of events when new data appears. Imagine you're unsure if there's chocolate in a box. You shake the box and hear a rattle—your confidence grows. Each new sound (evidence) changes your estimate. A detective works the same way: an initial version (e.g., 'the butler is guilty with a 20% chance') is refined after finding a glove or a fingerprint.

Bayesian inference is a fundamental framework of mathematical statistics, based on Bayes' theorem. It allows for quantitative assessment of uncertainty and updating beliefs in light of empirical data. The central concept is subjective probability as a measure of confidence. The process begins with a prior distribution over the space of hypotheses, which is then refined through a likelihood function reflecting the observed data. Computing the posterior distribution often requires high-dimensional integration, making practical implementation nontrivial. Bayesian methods naturally incorporate regularization through prior knowledge and provide interval estimates of parameters in the form of credible intervals. They are applied in machine learning (Bayesian networks, Gaussian processes), signal processing, econometrics, medical diagnostics, and cognitive sciences.

History

Bayes' theorem was first formulated in the posthumously published work of Thomas Bayes (1763), and later independently rediscovered and disseminated by Pierre-Simon Laplace in 1774. Laplace applied it to estimating the mass of Saturn and to demographic research. In the 20th century, interest revived thanks to the work of Harold Jeffreys, who introduced non-informative prior distributions, and Leonard Savage, who developed the axiomatics of subjective probability. With the advent of powerful computational methods such as the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm (1970) and Gibbs sampling, Bayesian inference became applicable to complex models in data science.

How it works

Inference relies on Bayes' theorem: P(H|D) = P(D|H) * P(H) / P(D), where H is a hypothesis, D is data. P(H) (prior) reflects the initial degree of belief, P(D|H) (likelihood) is the probability of obtaining data D if H is true, P(D) is the marginal probability of the data, serving as normalization. In practice, direct computation of the denominator, an integral over all hypotheses, is often impossible, and one resorts to Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to approximate the posterior distribution. Modern packages such as Stan and PyMC automate these calculations.
