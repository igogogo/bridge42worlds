CRISPR

CRISPR is molecular scissors that can cut DNA at a precisely specified location, allowing editing of the genetic code.

In practice: CRISPR technology is used to treat hereditary diseases, create pest-resistant plants, and modify animals for research.
CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) is a bacterial immune system turned into a universal tool for genetic engineering. It consists of two components: a guide RNA (sgRNA) complementary to the target DNA segment, and the Cas endonuclease (most often Cas9). The complex scans DNA, recognizes the target site by complementarity, and creates a double-strand break. Repair of the break via non-homologous end joining leads to insertions/deletions, disabling the gene; homologous recombination with a donor template allows precise changes to be made. This technology is significantly simpler and cheaper than previous methods.

History

The CRISPR/Cas system was discovered by Francisco Mojica and other scientists studying bacterial defense mechanisms. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier adapted it for gene editing.

How it works

A guide RNA (a molecular guide) directs the Cas protein to the desired location in the DNA. The protein cuts both strands, and the cell initiates emergency repair. Scientists can add a template with the correct sequence, and the cell will copy it during repair.

💡 With CRISPR, pigs resistant to African swine fever have already been created, and human embryo genes have been edited to correct a mutation causing heart disease.
CRISPR is a tool for editing genes (hereditary instructions). It works like a 'find and replace' function in text: a scientist specifies the desired DNA sequence (like a word), and the Cas enzyme finds it in the genome and cuts it. Then the cell repairs the break itself, making changes or inserting a new fragment. It’s as if a proofreader in a printing house cut out a typo and pasted in the correct letter.

History

The CRISPR/Cas system was discovered by Francisco Mojica and other scientists studying bacterial defense mechanisms. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier adapted it for gene editing.

How it works

A guide RNA (a molecular guide) directs the Cas protein to the desired location in the DNA. The protein cuts both strands, and the cell initiates emergency repair. Scientists can add a template with the correct sequence, and the cell will copy it during repair.

💡 The CRISPR/Cas system can be used not only for cutting, but also for DNA methylation or attaching fluorescent labels, turning it into a tool for observing chromatin in real time.
CRISPR/Cas9 is an adaptive system based on RNA-guided DNA cleavage. Originally, it is part of the adaptive immunity of bacteria and archaea: they integrate sequences from invading viruses into their CRISPR locus, then use transcribed RNA copies to recognize and destroy phage DNA upon reinfection. For gene editing, Cas9 from Streptococcus pyogenes or other variants (Cas12a, Cas13, Cas9 nickases) are used. Specificity is determined by the length of the guide RNA and the presence of a PAM (protospacer adjacent motif) — a short sequence near the target site. After creating a break, the cell initiates repair mechanisms: NHEJ (non-homologous end joining) and HDR (homology-directed repair). Off-target effects are possible, minimized by engineered Cas variants with increased accuracy. The technology is used for gene knockout, transcriptional activation/inhibition, epigenetic editing, and diagnostics (SHERLOCK, DETECTR).

History

The idea of genome editing with nucleases evolved from zinc fingers and TALEN, but the discovery of CRISPR was a revolution. Francisco Mojica in 1993 discovered repetitive sequences in the archaeal genome, later their function in immunity was elucidated. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier reprogrammed the system for laboratory use. Feng Zhang and colleagues in 2013 first applied CRISPR for editing eukaryotic genomes. For this, Doudna and Charpentier received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020.

How it works

The mechanism is based on complementary binding of RNA to DNA and subsequent hydrolysis of phosphodiester bonds. Cas9 contains two nuclease domains: HNH cleaves the strand complementary to the RNA, and RuvC cleaves the non-complementary strand. The guide RNA consists of crRNA (containing a spacer complementary to the target) and tracrRNA, necessary for binding to Cas. Combining them into a single sgRNA simplified use. The DNA break triggers a cellular response: NHEJ quickly ligates the ends, introducing insertions/deletions, which often inactivates the gene; HDR requires the presence of donor DNA and occurs less frequently, but allows precise editing. Specificity is limited by the thermodynamic stability of the hybrid and the possibility of mismatched binding, but engineered Cas mutations (HiFi-Cas9) reduce off-target cleavage.
