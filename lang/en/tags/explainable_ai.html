Explainable AI

2

A black box that says 'trust me' — that's how ordinary neural networks work. Explainable AI opens the lid and shows: 'look, this is why I decided this way.'

In practice: The doctor sees not only the AI's diagnosis but also the image with the tumor highlighted to verify for themselves. The bank explains to the client why the loan was denied.
Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is a field that makes the operation of machine learning models transparent to humans. Unlike traditional neural networks — 'black boxes', XAI provides an interpretation: why the model came to a particular conclusion, which features were important, and how reliable the prediction is. Approaches are divided into interpretable models (decision trees, linear models) and post-hoc explanations for already trained complex models (LIME, SHAP, gradient maps). This is critical in medicine, finance, and law, where an error can cost lives or freedom.

History

The problem arose in the 2010s when neural networks began making vital decisions, and no one could explain their logic. The first XAI methods emerged from model interpretation work. In 2018, the European Union introduced the right to explanation of algorithmic decisions (GDPR).

How it works

The simplest XAI method is LIME. It takes a complex model and builds a simple, understandable model around a specific example (e.g., a list of rules). Then it shows how the answer would change if details were substituted — as if we were covering parts of the image and checking whether the AI stops seeing the cat. Other methods look at which neurons in the network were most strongly activated.

💡 One of the XAI methods, SHAP, is named after Shapley from game theory — it fairly distributes the 'contribution' of each feature to the decision, like a bonus among team players.
A person brings test results, and the computer says: 'cancer.' Without explanations — it's scary. Explainable AI (XAI) turns a fortune-teller into a teacher. A neural network recognized a cat in a photo — XAI highlights the whiskers and ears that were decisive. It's as if a calculator not only showed '4' but also added: 'because 2+2.' Thus, AI ceases to be a black box and becomes a partner that says: 'look, I see this and this, so I think so.'

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is a research area at the intersection of machine learning, cognitive science, and human-computer interaction. The goal is to create models whose decisions can be understood and trusted by humans. Key concepts: interpretability (the degree to which internal mechanisms can be understood), explainability (the ability to provide understandable explanations), model transparency, and causality. Methods are divided into: 1) intrinsically interpretable (linear regression, decision trees, attention-based models), 2) post-hoc explanations (LIME, SHAP, Integrated Gradients, DeepLIFT, Grad-CAM). Explanations are evaluated for reproducibility, accuracy, and consistency with human intuition.

History

The black box problem intensified with the deep learning renaissance in 2012. Early work on model interpretation includes weight analysis in the 1990s, but XAI as a separate discipline took shape after 2016, when DARPA launched the Explainable AI program. The formal right to explanation of algorithmic decisions was established by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU.

How it works

XAI is based on methods that approximate a complex decision function with a simple interpretable model in the local neighborhood of each example (LIME). SHAP uses game theory: the contribution of each feature is computed as its marginal contribution over all possible subsets of features. Gradient methods (Grad-CAM) visualize which parts of the input image most strongly activated the neurons of the last convolutional layer, using gradients of the target class. For text models (transformers), the attention mechanism allows highlighting important words.

\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|! (|N|-|S|-1)!}{|N|!} [f(S \cup \{i\}) - f(S)]
ϕ_i — contribution of feature i, N — set of all features, S — subset, f — model; computes the weighted average change in prediction when adding feature i to all possible subsets.

Open problems & fun facts

Lack of a unified metric for evaluating explanation quality.; Explanations can be misleading, creating a false sense of understanding (illusion of explanatory depth).; Trade-off between model accuracy and interpretability.; Social and legal aspects: who is responsible if a doctor makes an error by trusting the AI's explanation.
