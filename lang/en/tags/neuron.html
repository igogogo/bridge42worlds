neuron

7

A conductor cell that transmits electrical discharges along a neural circuit. Like a relay, it closes if the incoming signal exceeds a threshold.

In practice: Artificial neural networks, inspired by the work of neurons, recognize faces, translate texts, and drive cars. Understanding neurons has allowed treating epilepsy and Parkinson's disease through brain stimulation.
A neuron is an electrically excitable cell, the basis of the nervous system. The neuron's membrane uses ion channels to create a potential difference (resting potential around -70 mV). Upon excitation, sodium channels open, sodium ions enter, and the potential becomes positive—this is the action potential. It propagates along the axon at speeds up to 120 m/s. At synapses, the electrical signal is converted into chemical: neurotransmitters (e.g., glutamate) are released and bind to receptors of the next neuron, continuing the relay.

History

Spanish doctor Santiago Ramón y Cajal in the late 19th century used staining (Golgi method) to see individual neurons. He proved that the nervous system consists of cells, not a continuous web.

How it works

When a neuron receives enough signals from neighbors, the charge inside it rapidly changes, and an impulse runs along the axon. At the end of the axon, chemical messengers are released that excite or inhibit the next neuron. Thus, information is transmitted from the sensory organs to the brain and back to the muscles.

💡 The conduction speed along some axons exceeds 400 km/h. And the total number of synapses in the brain is estimated at a quadrillion (10^15).
A neuron is a cell of the brain and nervous system that can receive and send signals. It has a body and processes: short ones (dendrites) catch signals, a long one (axon) transmits them further. The signal runs like an electric wave (impulse). Between neurons there are gaps (synapses), where the signal jumps using chemical substances (mediators).

A neuron is the fundamental structural and functional unit of the nervous system, specialized for generating and conducting electrical signals. It consists of a soma (body), a dendritic tree, and an axon ending in synaptic terminals. The cell membrane contains passive and active ion channels that maintain the membrane potential. The action potential is a universal electrical signal that propagates without attenuation thanks to the regenerative activation of voltage-gated sodium channels. Myelination of the axon (by Schwann cells or oligodendrocytes) dramatically speeds up conduction via the saltatory mechanism—jumping between nodes of Ranvier. At synapses, depolarization opens calcium channels, Ca²⁺ triggers exocytosis of vesicles with neurotransmitters. Postsynaptic receptors (ionotropic and metabotropic) convert the chemical signal back into an electrical one. Spatial and temporal summation of synaptic potentials determines whether an action potential will occur. Neurons are classified by neurotransmitter (glutamatergic, GABAergic, etc.), structure (unipolar, bipolar, multipolar), and function (sensory, motor, interneurons).

History

In 1873, Camillo Golgi developed a method for staining nervous tissue with silver nitrate (the Golgi stain), which made it possible to see neurons in their entirety. Santiago Ramón y Cajal, using this method in the 1880s, detailed the histology of the nervous system and formulated the neuron doctrine—the idea that the nervous system is composed of discrete cells. Their opposition to the reticular theory ended in the 20th century with the invention of the electron microscope, which confirmed the synaptic cleft. In 1952, Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley created a mathematical model of the action potential in the giant squid axon, laying the foundations of computational neuroscience.

How it works

The functioning of a neuron is determined by the laws of electrochemistry. The membrane separates ions: more Na⁺ outside, more K⁺ inside. The sodium-potassium ATPase (Na⁺/K⁺ pump) actively transports 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, creating ion gradients. The resting potential is close to the potassium equilibrium potential, described by the Nernst equation: E_K = (RT/F) ln([K⁺]out/[K⁺]in). Upon stimulation, voltage-gated sodium channels open, Na⁺ enters, depolarizing the membrane; when the potential reaches a threshold (~-55 mV), more sodium channels open—a positive feedback loop generates the spike. Then sodium channels inactivate, potassium channels open, expelling K⁺ and returning the potential to rest. The refractory period ensures unidirectional impulse propagation. At synapses, the action potential activates calcium channels, Ca²⁺ enters, causing vesicle fusion with the membrane and release of the neurotransmitter. The neurotransmitter diffuses to receptors; excitatory neurotransmitters (glutamate) open sodium channels, inhibitory ones (GABA) open chloride or potassium channels, shifting the potential closer to the equilibrium. Synaptic plasticity (long-term potentiation) underlies memory.
