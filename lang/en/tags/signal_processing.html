signal processing

4

A guitar string vibrates 440 times per second. The ear hears the note A. Signal processing turns this vibration into numbers and breaks it down into pure tones, just as a prism decomposes light. Without it, a noisy ringtone would not become music in headphones.

In practice: Noise cancellation in phones, music compression into MP3, medical tomographs, radars, data transmission over Wi-Fi — all of this works thanks to signal processing algorithms.
Signal processing is a mathematical tool for working with information encoded in changes of a physical quantity (sound pressure, voltage, brightness). An analog signal is digitized: instantaneous values are measured at a given frequency, yielding a stream of samples. Then algorithms are applied for filtering (removing interference), compression (MP3 uses psychoacoustics to discard inaudible frequencies), and restoration (removing blur in photos). The central idea is the Fourier transform, which moves a signal from the time domain to the frequency domain: a graph of oscillations becomes a spectrum where the amplitude of each frequency component is visible. The entire telecommunications, audio recording, and medical imaging industries are built on this.

History

It all began with sound analysis in the 19th century. Jean Fourier devised a way to break any wave into a sum of simple sinusoids. In the 20th century, with the development of radio and telephones, methods grew more complex, and with the advent of computers, processing became digital — signals turned into chains of ones and zeros.

How it works

A signal, such as a voice recording, is measured thousands of times per second — a sequence of numbers is obtained. Then this digital footprint is decomposed by frequencies: we determine how much low sound (bass) and how much high sound (whistling) it contains. It's like a soup recipe: a cook tastes and says how much salt, pepper, vegetables there are. Knowing the recipe, you can remove the excess (noise) or compress the file, leaving only what the ear hears.

💡 The MP3 format discards about 90% of the data, yet to our ear the difference is almost imperceptible — the algorithm relies on how the brain masks weak sounds with loud ones at nearby frequencies.
Signal processing is a way to extract useful information from a tangle of electrical, sound, or light vibrations. Take a noisy image: a face is visible but stripes interfere. The program decomposes the picture into waves of different frequencies and removes those resembling stripes. A clean portrait remains. Similarly, we clean up sound, align a radio signal, and predict earthquakes from seismic vibrations.

Signal processing is a broad engineering discipline that studies methods for analysis, modification, and synthesis of signals — time series, spatial distributions, or more general fields. Key operations: sampling (the Kotelnikov–Shannon theorem establishes the minimum sampling rate), quantization, filtering (linear and nonlinear), spectral analysis, statistical processing. It is applied in telecommunications (modulation, coding, compression), sonar and radar, seismology, audio and video processing, biomedical engineering (ECG, MRI). With the rise of neural networks, the field of deep learning on signals has emerged, where convolutional networks replace classical filters.

History

Its roots go back to the harmonic analysis of Jean Fourier (1822), though decomposition into trigonometric series appeared even earlier. In the 20th century, the works of Nyquist and Shannon on sampling gave an impetus, and then the digital revolution of the 1960s-1970s produced the Fast Fourier Transform algorithms (Cooley and Tukey, 1965), making spectral analysis fast. The development of integrated circuits allowed real-time processing — from CD players to cellular communications. In the 21st century, compression methods (JPEG, MPEG), wavelet transforms, and neural network approaches have expanded the capabilities of signal processing beyond recognition.

How it works

The basic cycle: an ADC converts the signal into a sequence of samples x[n]. Then a digital filter described by a difference equation is applied, e.g., y[n] = Σ b_k x[n-k] – Σ a_k y[n-k] (recursive filter). To analyze the frequency composition, the Discrete Fourier Transform is computed: X[k] = Σ x[n] e^{-j2πkn/N}. In practice, the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is used, reducing the complexity from O(N²) to O(N log N). For non-stationary signals (where frequencies change over time), the Short-Time Fourier Transform or wavelets, localized in both time and frequency, are used. Modern communication systems are built on Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM), which divides the channel into thousands of subcarriers to combat multipath fading.

X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j 2 \pi f t} dt
x(t) — original signal, X(f) — frequency spectrum, f — frequency
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-j 2 \pi k n / N}
x[n] — signal samples (n = 0...N-1), X[k] — spectrum coefficients at frequency k/N * sampling_rate

Open problems & fun facts

Blind signal processing: recovering the original signal without exact knowledge of channel characteristics; Processing of ultra-wideband signals at high frequencies (terahertz signals); Robustness to interference at low signal-to-noise ratio; Energy-efficient algorithms for IoT devices; Extracting a signal from a mixture with unknown structure (cocktail party problem)
