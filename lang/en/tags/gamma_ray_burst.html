gamma-ray burst

At a random point in the sky, a source flares up for an instant, a hundred times brighter than all the stars combined — the most powerful explosion since the Big Bang.

In practice: Gamma-ray bursts help study the birth of black holes, and their radiation allows us to date the most distant galaxies and probe interstellar matter.
Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are divided into two classes: long (lasting over 2 seconds) — arising from the core collapse of a rapidly rotating massive star into a black hole, and short (under 2 seconds) — accompanying neutron star mergers. In both cases, the central engine is an accreting black hole that launches an ultra-relativistic jet, piercing the star or the ejected material shell. An observer sees a gamma-ray burst if the beam is directed toward Earth.

History

The first gamma-ray bursts were accidentally detected in 1967 by the American Vela satellites, which were monitoring nuclear tests. For a long time their nature remained a mystery until the 1990s, when their cosmic origin was proven.

How it works

Imagine a super-fast whirligig of hot gas spiraling into a black hole. Narrow jets of this gas, accelerated to near-light speeds, slam into the surrounding material and generate a flash visible across the entire Universe.

💡 The most distant known burst, GRB 090423, occurred 630 million years after the Big Bang — meaning its light traveled 13 billion years to reach us.
A gamma-ray burst is a sudden, lasting from milliseconds to minutes, flash of gamma rays (the most energetic electromagnetic radiation), arriving from a random direction in the sky. It is the 'death cry' of a dying massive star or the merger of two neutron stars.

A gamma-ray burst (GRB) is a cosmic pulse of high-energy (tens of keV to tens of MeV) radiation, detected by gamma-ray observatories roughly once per day from random directions. Long GRBs (>2 s, average ~30 s) are associated with collapsars — hypernovae of type Ic-BL, where the core of a rapidly rotating massive star collapses into a black hole, forming an accretion disk and a relativistic jet. Short GRBs (<2 s, average ~0.3 s) arise from binary neutron star mergers (kilonovae). The afterglow in X-rays, optical, and radio persists for days to weeks, following a power-law decay.

History

Discovered on July 2, 1967, by the American Vela 4a satellite, which recorded a double flash of gamma rays. Initially thought to be signs of extraterrestrial civilizations. In 1997, the Italian-Dutch satellite BeppoSAX first measured the afterglow coordinates of GRB 970508, establishing its extragalactic nature. In 2017, the joint detection of GW170817 and GRB 170817A definitively confirmed the link between short bursts and kilonovae.

How it works

The central engine is a compact object (black hole or rapidly rotating neutron star — magnetar) surrounded by an accretion disk. Energy is extracted through neutrino cooling or the Blandford–Znajek electromagnetic mechanism, forming a collimated jet with a Lorentz factor >100. Internal shock waves within the jet produce the prompt gamma-ray emission, while the afterglow arises as the jet decelerates against the interstellar medium, according to the Bland–McKee model.

Open problems & fun facts

Physics of relativistic jet generation and collimation; Nature of the central engine in particularly long (ultra-long) bursts; Origin of repeating and unusually soft bursts; Precise mechanism of energy transfer in the jet and conversion into gamma-ray emission
