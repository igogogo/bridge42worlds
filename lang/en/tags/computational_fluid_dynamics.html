computational fluid dynamics

Air flows around a Formula 1 race car at 300 km/h — a digital wind tunnel slices the flow into millions of cells and computes the physics of shocks and eddies in each. Computational fluid dynamics turns the invisible dance of liquids and gases into a picture on the screen.

In practice: It optimizes aircraft wings, forecasts weather and ocean currents, helps design quiet fans and heart valves — all without expensive prototypes.
Computational fluid dynamics solves the conservation equations of mass, momentum, and energy on a discrete grid that describes the flow domain. These differential equations — Navier–Stokes — are notoriously nonlinear and turbulent. CFD methods (finite volume, finite element, spectral) approximate derivatives and advance the solution through iterations. Turbulence models (RANS, LES, DNS) handle the chaotic small scales. The result is a detailed map of velocities, pressures, and temperatures, allowing engineers to test designs virtually and scientists to study galaxy formation and blood flow.

History

Flow calculations began in the 1960s in aerospace laboratories, when it became necessary to understand how spacecraft enter the atmosphere. Early computers could handle only simple two-dimensional flows. By the 1990s, CFD was used in designing cars, submarines, and even artificial hearts.

How it works

First, a grid is built around the airplane or pipe. The fluid motion equations — the Navier–Stokes equations — are too complex for manual calculation, so the computer approximates them on each grid cell. Tiny corrections are recalculated step by step in time, like frames of a movie, until the entire flow is known. It resembles predicting the path of confetti in a drafty room — but with rigorous physical laws.

💡 The first CFD calculation of an entire aircraft (F-16) was performed in 1983 on a Cray supercomputer with 8 million grid nodes. Today, toy CFD apps run on a smartphone.
Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is the simulation of liquid and gas motion on a computer. Just as a crowd simulation shows human flows around obstacles, CFD breaks air or water into a grid of small cells. Each cell is assigned velocity, pressure, and temperature, and the computer solves the equations linking them. Step by step, the whole flow picture emerges: vortices, drag, heat transfer.

Computational fluid dynamics is the numerical simulation of liquid and gas flows and heat transfer by solving conservation laws (mass, momentum, energy) in the form of differential equations. The most common method is the finite volume method: the domain is divided into control volumes, and the equations are integrated over each, yielding algebraic relations for averaged quantities. Spatial schemes (upwind, central, TVD) and temporal integration (explicit, implicit) affect accuracy and stability. For turbulent flows, direct numerical simulation (DNS) resolves all scales but is often unfeasible; large eddy simulation (LES) and Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations with closure models are used instead. CFD is inseparable from mesh generation, high-performance computing, and visualization.

History

Ideas date back to the early 20th century and Lewis Fry Richardson's dream of a 'weather prediction factory' with human computers. True CFD emerged in the 1960s with the work of Francis Harlow at Los Alamos (particle-in-cell method) and panel methods in aerodynamics. In the 1980s, commercial codes FLUENT and CFX appeared, and in the 1990s, unstructured meshes and massive parallelization. Today, CFD is a pillar of engineering design and environmental sciences.

How it works

The Navier–Stokes equations for incompressible fluid: ∇·v = 0 and ρ(∂v/∂t + v·∇v) = -∇p + μ∇²v + f. They are discretized on a grid. In the finite volume method, fluxes through cell faces are computed for each cell using Gauss's theorem. Convective terms are treated with limiters to prevent spurious oscillations. The pressure–velocity coupling is resolved by algorithms like SIMPLE. The resulting large sparse linear systems are solved iteratively (multigrid, Krylov subspace methods). Boundary conditions specify inlets, walls, and far field. Postprocessing extracts forces, moments, and particle trajectories.

\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0
\mathbf{v} — velocity vector
\rho \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + \rho (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{v} = -\nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{f}
\rho — density; p — pressure; \mu — dynamic viscosity; \mathbf{f} — external forces (e.g., gravity)
