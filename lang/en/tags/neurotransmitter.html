neurotransmitter

A molecular courier that delivers messages between nerve cells, like a postman delivering letters to addresses.

In practice: Brain function, emotions, movement — everything depends on neurotransmitters; imbalances underlie depression, Parkinson's disease, and other disorders.
Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that transmit signals between neurons, and from neurons to muscle or gland cells. They are synthesized in the neuron, packaged into vesicles (bubbles), and released into the synaptic cleft in response to an electrical impulse. The molecules diffuse to the postsynaptic membrane and bind to receptors, causing either excitation or inhibition. This process underlies all functions of the nervous system.

History

The idea of chemical signal transmission in the nervous system was first proven by Otto Loewi in 1921. He conducted an experiment with frog hearts and showed that one heart could slow down another through a fluid, releasing a then-unknown substance — later named acetylcholine.

How it works

Imagine two people throwing a ball: the neurotransmitter is the ball. One neuron throws the ball into the gap between cells (the synaptic cleft), and the other catches it with a special glove (receptor) and knows what to do next.

💡 Some neurotransmitters, such as oxytocin, act not only in the brain but throughout the body — it stimulates uterine contractions during childbirth and milk ejection, as well as forming the bond between mother and child.
A neurotransmitter is a substance that one nerve cell (neuron) releases to transmit a signal to another cell. It can be compared to a key: it only fits a particular lock (receptor) on the surface of the receiving cell. When the key is inserted, a chain of events is triggered in the cell. Different neurotransmitters perform different tasks: for example, dopamine is linked to pleasure and motivation, serotonin to mood, acetylcholine to muscle movements.

Neurotransmitters are endogenous chemicals that transmit signals across the synapse from one neuron to another or to an effector cell. They belong to various classes: amino acids (glutamate, GABA), monoamines (dopamine, serotonin), peptides (endorphins), and others. Synthesis occurs in the neuron cell body or presynaptic terminal; after packaging into vesicles and exocytosis triggered by Ca2+ ions, the neurotransmitter binds to specific receptors (ionotropic or metabotropic), initiating intracellular signaling cascades. Inactivation occurs via reuptake, enzymatic degradation, or diffusion. The balance of different neurotransmitter systems determines complex behavior and cognitive functions.

History

The concept of chemical transmission arose from Otto Loewi's experiments (1921), which demonstrated humoral transmission in the frog vagus nerve. Later, Henry Dale identified acetylcholine. In the 1950s, the discovery of serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine expanded the understanding of synaptic transmission. Current research focuses on the role of neurotransmitters in plasticity, learning, and mental illnesses.

How it works

The process includes: 1) synthesis and loading into vesicles, 2) action potential opening calcium channels, 3) vesicle fusion with the membrane and release of the neurotransmitter, 4) diffusion across the cleft, 5) binding to receptors, 6) postsynaptic response, 7) inactivation. Autoreceptors and reuptake transporters play important roles. Disruptions at any step lead to neurological pathologies.
