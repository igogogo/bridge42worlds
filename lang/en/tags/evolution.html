evolution

10

Species do not remain unchanged — they adapt to the environment, like clay changing shape under fingers. Those with favorable random changes survive and reproduce.

In practice: Understanding evolution helps create vaccines against rapidly mutating viruses and explains bacterial resistance to antibiotics. Plant and animal breeding is accelerated evolution under human control.
Biological evolution is the change in heritable characteristics of populations over generations. Its main mechanisms: mutations (DNA changes), gene recombination during sexual reproduction, gene flow (exchange of individuals between populations), genetic drift (random fluctuations in gene variant frequencies), and natural selection. Natural selection directs evolution: traits that increase fitness (the ability to survive and reproduce) gradually spread. Population separation (geographic barriers) can lead to speciation.

History

The idea of evolution was proposed by Charles Darwin after a round-the-world voyage. In 1859, he published the book 'On the Origin of Species,' where he proved that all species are related by common descent and change through natural selection.

How it works

In every population, there are different variants of traits (variation). Those that help survive and leave more offspring become more common over time. The environment filters out less successful variants. Thus, millions of years ago, fish developed fins, and land animals developed legs.

💡 The octopus eye and the human eye are structured very similarly but developed independently — this is an example of convergent evolution: similar conditions produce similar solutions.
Evolution is the gradual change of living beings from generation to generation. Imagine a flock of sparrows: some have slightly stronger beaks, others weaker. In a hungry year, birds with strong beaks can crack more seeds, survive, and leave more chicks with similar beaks. After many years, the beak of the entire population becomes stronger. This is natural selection (nature 'chooses' the fittest). Random changes — mutations (errors in genes) — provide the material for selection.

Evolution in biology is the process of change in the genetic composition of populations over time, leading to adaptation, speciation, and biodiversity. The modern synthetic theory of evolution integrates Darwinism, Mendelian genetics, and population genetics. Mutations are the source of genetic variation; recombination and sexual reproduction create new allele combinations. Natural selection acts on phenotypes, changing allele frequencies in the gene pool. Genetic drift is especially important in small populations. Microevolution (changes within species) and macroevolution (origin of new species and taxa) are connected. Modern methods of molecular phylogenetics allow reconstructing evolutionary trees by comparing DNA sequences.

History

The idea of species transformation dates back to antiquity, but scientific substantiation emerged in the 19th century. Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace independently formulated the concept of natural selection; Darwin published it in 1859 in 'On the Origin of Species.' Gregor Mendel discovered the laws of heredity in 1865, which later formed the basis of genetics. In the early 20th century, the synthesis of Mendelism and Darwinism led to the formation of the synthetic theory of evolution (S.S. Chetverikov, J.B.S. Haldane, R. Fisher, S. Wright). The discovery of the DNA structure (1953) and the development of molecular biology provided a direct mechanism for heredity and mutations.

How it works

The evolutionary process is based on four main factors: mutations, recombination, genetic drift, and natural selection. Mutations randomly change the DNA sequence; most are neutral or harmful, but some confer an advantage. Recombination during meiosis shuffles paternal and maternal chromosomes, creating new allele combinations. Genetic drift causes random changes in allele frequencies, especially in small populations. Natural selection increases the frequency of alleles that enhance fitness in a given environment. Mathematically, this is described by the Hardy-Weinberg law, which shows equilibrium in the absence of evolutionary forces. Speciation often occurs through geographic isolation, where accumulated differences lead to reproductive incompatibility.

p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1
p — frequency of the dominant allele; q — frequency of the recessive allele; p + q = 1; p² — proportion of homozygous dominant genotypes; 2pq — proportion of heterozygotes; q² — proportion of homozygous recessive genotypes.
