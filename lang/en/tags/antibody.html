antibody

Antibody — like a molecular watchdog: recognizes a stranger by its unique smell (antigen) and latches onto it to mark it for destruction.

In practice: Antibodies are used in therapy (e.g., against COVID-19), in diagnostic tests (pregnancy tests), and in scientific experiments to detect proteins.
Antibodies are glycoproteins of the immunoglobulin superfamily, produced by plasma cells (activated B-lymphocytes). Each antibody consists of two heavy and two light chains linked by disulfide bonds, and has a variable region for antigen recognition and a constant region that determines effector functions. There are five classes: IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE, IgD. Binding to an antigen can directly block infectivity, as well as opsonize the pathogen, activate complement and natural killer cells (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity). Monoclonal antibodies are a key tool in biotechnology and medicine.

History

Antibodies were discovered by Emil von Behring in 1890 when he used blood serum to treat diphtheria. Later, the structure of antibodies was deciphered by Gerald Edelman and Rodney Porter.

How it works

When a foreign agent (e.g., a virus) enters the body, specialized cells recognize it and begin producing antibodies. They act like 'Velcro', sticking to the surface of the virus and preventing it from entering cells, and also serve as tags for scavenger cells.

💡 The human body can produce more than a billion different antibodies, although the number of genes is relatively small — diversity is achieved through the shuffling of genetic segments.
An antibody (immunoglobulin) is a Y-shaped protein produced by the immune system. It works like a lock that is opened by only one key: each pair of ends of the Y-shaped structure recognizes only one specific antigen (foreign molecule). After binding to the antigen, the antibody either neutralizes it or attracts other cells to destroy the intruder.

Immunoglobulins are a family of Y-shaped proteins that provide humoral immunity. Basic structure: a tetramer of two identical heavy chains (H) and two light chains (L). Each chain contains a variable domain (V) and constant domains (C). An antibody has two antigen-binding fragments (Fab), each comprising VH, VL and CL/CH1, and one crystallizable fragment (Fc), responsible for interaction with Fc receptors and complement. Generation of diversity occurs through V(D)J recombination, somatic hypermutation, and gene conversion. Affinity maturation in germinal centers increases the strength of antigen binding. Monoclonal antibodies are produced by hybridoma technology or recombinant methods (phage display). Bispecific antibodies, conjugates with toxins and radionuclides expand therapeutic possibilities.

History

Antibody-based immunity was discovered by Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato in 1890 with the demonstration of passive immunization against diphtheria toxin using serum. In 1959, Gerald Edelman and Rodney Porter independently determined the structure of antibodies, for which they received the Nobel Prize. The invention of monoclonal antibody technology by César Milstein and Georges Köhler in 1975 opened the era of therapeutic antibodies.

How it works

Antigen recognition is based on non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, electrostatic, hydrophobic) between the hypervariable regions (CDRs) of the antibody and the antigenic epitope. Binding alters the antibody's conformation, and through the Fc fragment effector mechanisms are initiated: complement-dependent cytolysis (C1q is recruited to the Fc, triggering the cascade), antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (FcγRIII on NK cells), opsonization of phagocytes. Different isotypes perform specialized functions: IgG is the main one in blood, IgA in mucous membranes, IgE in allergies and parasites. The principle of genetic recombination and selection underlies adaptive recognition.

Open problems & fun facts
