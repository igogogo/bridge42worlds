quantum-like models

6

The brain processes uncertainty much like a particle can be in several states at once—until the moment of choice.

In practice: Helps create more flexible AI algorithms that can handle contradictory data.
Quantum-like models transfer the mathematical apparatus of quantum mechanics (e.g., Hilbert spaces—abstract vector spaces for states, operators—ways of measurement, probability amplitudes—quantities whose squared modulus gives probability) to the description of cognitive processes, social interactions, or biological systems. They do not claim that the brain is literally quantum but use a convenient formalism to account for contextuality, inconsistency, and question order.

History

The idea of applying quantum formalisms to psychology and economics began to develop in the late 20th century.

How it works

Like a coin spinning in the air is both heads and tails at once, so a person deciding exists in a 'superposition' of options.

💡 These models have explained decision-making paradoxes, such as violations of classical probability laws in surveys.
Some complex systems, such as the brain or society, behave as if they follow the rules of quantum physics (where superposition—being in multiple states at once—and entanglement—instant connections between particles—are possible). Similar mathematical techniques are used to describe, say, fluctuations in public opinion.

Quantum-like models apply the structure of quantum theory—states in Hilbert space, superpositions, probability interference, entanglement—to model phenomena where classical probabilistic approaches fail: order effects in surveys, violations of the law of total probability, context-dependent judgments. They do not postulate a physical quantum nature but use mathematical isomorphism.

History

Pioneering work in this area was done by mathematicians and cognitive psychologists such as D. Aerts and A. Khrennikov starting in the 1990s. They showed that many 'irrational' human decisions are well described by quantum-like probabilities. Later, the field was taken up by quantum information specialists. David Deutsch contributed to quantum computing, providing tools; John Archibald Wheeler reflected on the participation of the observer, which resonates with quantum-like approaches.

How it works

The state of an agent is described by a vector in Hilbert space (a complex vector space with an inner product). Choices are represented by projection operators. The probability of an outcome is given by the squared modulus of the projection (Born rule). Context influences like a change of basis. This allows quantum-like effects: interference (when the sum of probabilities does not reduce to the classical sum) and violation of distributivity. The principle of superposition and interference of states is applied.

P = |\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2
P is probability, |ψ⟩ is the initial state (vector in Hilbert space), |φ⟩ is the final state, ⟨φ|ψ⟩ is the inner product (probability amplitude)
|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |e_i\rangle
|ψ⟩ is the state vector, c_i are complex amplitudes, |e_i⟩ is an orthonormal basis (set of mutually exclusive states)

Open problems & fun facts

Testing whether quantum-likeness is truly necessary or if classical models with nonlinearity suffice; Lack of a unified physical basis for quantum effects in macroscopic systems; Difficulty in interpreting quantum-like probabilities in psychology
