immunity

1

Immunity is an army of defenders inside us that remembers every enemy and strikes more accurately each time.

In practice: Vaccination trains the immune system to recognize real viruses, and cancer immunotherapy teaches it to attack tumors.
Immunity can be innate and acquired. Innate immunity reacts instantly and identically to any threat: skin and mucous membranes create a barrier, macrophages and neutrophils phagocytize (engulf) intruders, and the complement system punches holes in bacterial membranes. Acquired (adaptive) immunity starts slower but acts precisely: lymphocytes recognize specific antigens, after which B cells turn into plasma cells and release antibodies, and killer T cells destroy infected cells. This response leaves memory cells, ensuring a rapid reaction upon repeated contact.

History

English doctor Edward Jenner in 1796 noticed that milkmaids who had cowpox did not get smallpox. He made the world's first vaccination: he took fluid from a cowpox blister and introduced it to a boy – thus vaccination was born.

How it works

When an unfamiliar microbe enters the body, the immune system studies its special characteristics (antigens) and starts producing antibodies that fit these characteristics exactly, like a key fits a lock. The antibodies cling to the enemy and neutralize it. Some memory cells remain alive for years, so that upon a repeat encounter, the reaction is immediate – this is the secret of immunity after illness or vaccination.

💡 Breast milk contains ready-made antibodies that protect the infant from infections in the first months of life, while its own immunity is still immature.
Immunity is the body's defense system against microbes and viruses. White blood cells (leukocytes) stand guard in the blood and tissues – they are like border guards. Upon detecting a stranger, they either immediately engulf it or produce special protein tags (antibodies) that help other defender cells find and destroy the enemy.

The immune system is a complex defense network that distinguishes 'self' from 'non-self'. It is based on recognition of antigens (foreign molecular structures) via lymphocyte receptors. Innate immunity (macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, the complement system, cytokines) provides an immediate response and activates the adaptive arm. Adaptive immunity is realized through clonal selection: from a vast repertoire of lymphocytes with unique receptors, those that recognize a specific antigen are selected and multiplied. T helper cells (CD4+) coordinate the response, T killer cells (CD8+) destroy infected cells, B cells differentiate into plasma cells and produce antibodies of five classes (IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE, IgD). Central tolerance (in the thymus and bone marrow) eliminates autoreactive lymphocytes.

History

Since ancient times, it was observed that those who recovered from the plague did not get reinfected. Edward Jenner scientifically justified vaccination in 1796. Louis Pasteur in the 1880s created vaccines against chicken cholera and rabies. Ilya Mechnikov (1882) discovered phagocytosis, Paul Ehrlich (1891) – humoral immunity and antibodies. In 1959-62, Macfarlane Burnet and Peter Medawar uncovered the mechanisms of immunological tolerance. In 1975, Susumu Tonegawa showed how immunoglobulin genes rearrange to create billions of antibody variations.

How it works

The process begins with the capture of an antigen by dendritic cells, which migrate to lymph nodes and present antigen fragments on MHC II molecules to T helper cells. Activated T helpers secrete interleukins, stimulating the proliferation of B cells and their maturation into plasma cells that release millions of antibody molecules into the bloodstream. T killer cells recognize foreign peptides on MHC I of infected cells and induce apoptosis via the perforin-granzyme mechanism. Some B and T cells are preserved as memory cells (sometimes for life), ensuring a secondary response. Key molecules: antibodies (immunoglobulins), T-cell receptors (TCR), major histocompatibility complex (MHC), cytokines (interleukins, interferons).

Open problems & fun facts

Etiology of autoimmune diseases and ways to safely suppress them; Creation of effective vaccines against chronic infections (HIV, malaria); Evasion of tumors from immune surveillance and overcoming immunosuppression of the microenvironment
