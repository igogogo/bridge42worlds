Tag

organic molecules in space

In the vastness between stars drift the building blocks of life — carbon-based molecules.

In practice: These findings explain the origin of the raw materials for life on Earth and guide the search for extraterrestrial organisms.
In the Orion Nebula, a radio telescope captures the 'song' of methanol—a line at a frequency of 12.2 gigahertz. Astrochemists call any carbon-containing molecule organic, not necessarily of biological origin. In molecular clouds like the Orion Nebula, radio telescopes detect emission lines from methanol, formic acid, cyanoacetylene, and even long carbon chains such as HC11N. Temperatures there are only 10–50 kelvins, but cosmic rays and ultraviolet light trigger chemistry. Molecules not only float in the gas but also freeze onto the icy mantles of dust grains. Today, the catalog of interstellar and circumstellar molecules exceeds 250 entries.

History

The first interstellar molecules (CH and CN) were spotted back in the 1930s in visible light. The real breakthrough came with the advent of radio telescopes: by the early 1970s, dozens of complex organic compounds had been found, and their numbers continue to grow.

How it works

Atoms in cosmic clouds occasionally collide and, if they find a suitable partner, form molecules. This works especially well on the surface of dust grains: a dust grain acts as a matchmaker, holding atoms together until they react.

💡 The Murchison meteorite, which fell in Australia in 1969, contained over 70 amino acids, many of which are rare on Earth—a clear sign that space itself can cook up complex organic compounds.
Space is far from empty: it is filled with tenuous gas and dust, where amazing molecules hide—from simple formaldehyde to sugars. Chemical telescopes (radio telescopes) capture their invisible 'radio voices'—each type of molecule sings at its own frequency. It's like finding on a distant shore a handful of amino acids—the building blocks from which proteins of living cells are assembled.

In 1969, the Murchison meteorite was found to contain more than 70 types of amino acids—the building blocks of proteins. This discovery confirmed that the interstellar medium is rich in complex carbon compounds. Organic molecules in space are a class of carbon-based substances detected in gas-dust clouds, protoplanetary disks, comets, and meteorites. They are identified by their rotational and vibrational spectra in radio and infrared bands. Key ingredients include water (H2O), carbon monoxide (CO), formaldehyde (H2CO), methane (CH4), methanol (CH3OH), and more complex ones up to fullerenes (C60) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Formation occurs in the gas phase and on the surfaces of ice-coated dust particles. Dust shields against harsh radiation and catalyzes reactions.

History

The first optical lines of CH and CN in the interstellar medium were identified in the late 1930s. With the advent of millimeter astronomy in the 1960s, ammonia, water, and formaldehyde were discovered. In 1969, Fred Hoyle and Chandra Wickramasinghe proposed that interstellar dust contains organic polymers. Later, infrared space telescopes (ISO, Spitzer, JWST) confirmed the presence of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Today, the record-holder by mass is methanol (CH3OH), and by complexity—fullerenes C60 and C70.

How it works

In cold clouds, gas-phase reactions proceed via ion-molecular chains and neutral chemistry on dust grain surfaces. Dust shields UV radiation and acts as a catalyst. The resulting molecules are detected by their rotational spectra in the radio band—each has a unique set of lines, allowing its structure, temperature, and concentration to be determined. Isotopic shifts help reconstruct the history of nucleosynthesis.
