ultraviolet radiation

Invisible neighbor of violet — light whose energy burns skin, kills microbes, and makes some substances glow.

In practice: Tanning beds and sterilization of instruments in hospitals; checking the authenticity of banknotes by the glow of marks.
Ultraviolet radiation occupies the spectrum from 10 to 400 nm, divided into near (UVA, 315–400), middle (UVB, 280–315), far (UVC, 100–280), and vacuum ultraviolet. The main natural source is the Sun, but only UVA and a little UVB reach the surface; UVC is completely absorbed by the ozone layer. UV radiation initiates chemical reactions (e.g., vitamin D synthesis) and can cause many materials to fluoresce.

History

UV was discovered by German physicist Johann Wilhelm Ritter in 1801, when he noticed that chemical reactions proceed faster beyond the violet edge of the rainbow. Later, James Maxwell described all electromagnetic waves with a unified theory.

How it works

UV is emitted by the Sun and artificial lamps. Its energy is absorbed by DNA molecules (the substance of heredity), causing mutations (changes in DNA), so nature created a protective ozone layer in the atmosphere. In sterilization lamps, special gases emit UV during discharge, which kills bacteria.

💡 Medical quartz lamps emit UVC that destroys viral DNA, so air in operating rooms can be disinfected with light.
Ultraviolet (UV) is radiation, like light, but with shorter waves (distance between wave crests) that our eyes cannot see. It carries more energy than visible light, so it can damage cells. It is the sun's UV rays that cause tanning and burns. Bees, unlike us, see in ultraviolet, so flowers are painted with bright patterns for them.

Ultraviolet radiation is electromagnetic waves with lengths from ~10 nm to 400 nm, having photon energy of 3–124 eV. In this range, quanta can break chemical bonds, cause the photoelectric effect, and ionize atoms (starting from the extreme ultraviolet). UV is strongly absorbed by the Earth's atmosphere thanks to the ozone layer (Hartley band), which protects the biosphere. In astrophysics, the UV range carries information about hot stars, the interstellar medium, and the exospheres of planets.

History

In 1801, Ritter, working with a prism, determined that beyond the violet part of the spectrum there are rays that accelerate the decomposition of silver chloride. At the end of the 19th century, it was realized that UV is part of a unified electromagnetic spectrum, whose theory was created by Maxwell. In the 20th century, the development of quantum mechanics led to the understanding of the photoelectric effect caused by UV (Einstein, 1905). The creation of efficient semiconductor UV LEDs based on gallium nitride (Nakamura's work) opened up compact radiation sources.

How it works

According to the Planck–Einstein formula, the energy of a photon E = hν determines the ability of UV to cause chemical processes. The photoeffect is explained by Einstein's equation E_k max = hν – W, where W is the work function. In astrophysics, the spectrum of hot stars obeys Wien's displacement law λ_max = b/T (T > 10000 K gives a peak in UV). UV telescopes on satellites (e.g., GALEX) use multi-mirror systems to focus hard radiation, since ordinary lenses absorb UV.

E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
E — photon energy, h ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s — Planck constant, ν — radiation frequency, c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s — speed of light, λ — wavelength
E_{k\,\text{max}} = h\nu - W
E_k max — maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electron, h — Planck constant, ν — frequency of incident UV, W — work function of the material (energy needed to detach an electron)
\lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{b}{T}
λ_max — wavelength of maximum emission, b ≈ 2.898×10⁻³ m·K — Wien's constant, T — temperature of the black body
