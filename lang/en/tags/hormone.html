Tag

hormone

Hormones are the body's chemical couriers: the brain and glands send them through the blood to remotely control organ function.

In practice: Hormones are used to make insulin for diabetics, birth control pills, and adrenaline to stop anaphylactic shock.
Hormones are biologically active substances produced by endocrine glands and carried via the bloodstream. They act in extremely low concentrations (nanograms per milliliter) and regulate metabolism, growth, reproduction, and mood. They are classified into peptide, steroid, and amino acid derivative hormones, which bind to receptors on the surface or inside target cells.

History

The first hormone—adrenaline—was isolated by Japanese Jokichi Takamine and American John Abel in 1901.

How it works

The hormone travels through the blood until it finds a cell with a special receptor protein. The binding of the hormone to the receptor triggers a cascade of chemical reactions inside the cell, causing it, for example, to speed up the heartbeat or store glucose.

💡 Oxytocin, the 'cuddle hormone', is released in mothers during breastfeeding and in lovers during touch, enhancing feelings of trust.
Hormones are signaling substances. Glands, like post offices, release hormones into the blood—'letters' with an address. Each cell with a matching receptor (lock) receives the letter and acts according to instructions.

Hormones are chemical messengers that provide humoral regulation. Synthesized by specialized cells (often in glands), they are secreted into the blood and act on target cells via high-affinity receptors. The hormone-receptor interaction triggers intracellular signaling cascades: adenylate cyclase, phosphoinositide, tyrosine kinase pathways, which alter enzyme activity or gene expression. Hormone concentrations are regulated by feedback mechanisms, such as the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis.

History

Claude Bernard's experiments (1855) showed that the liver releases glucose into the blood, laying the foundations of endocrinology. In 1901, J. Takamine and J. Abel independently crystallized adrenaline. Frederick Banting and Charles Best isolated insulin in 1921, saving millions of lives. Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally in the 1970s revealed the structure of hypothalamic releasing hormones.

How it works

Steroid hormones penetrate the plasma membrane and bind to nuclear receptors, regulating gene transcription. Peptide hormones and catecholamines bind to membrane receptors, activating G-proteins or enzymatic cascades, leading to the formation of second messengers (cAMP, IP3, Ca²⁺). This achieves signal amplification: one hormone molecule can trigger the synthesis of thousands of target enzyme molecules. Negative feedback maintains homeostasis: for example, thyroid hormones inhibit thyrotropin production.
