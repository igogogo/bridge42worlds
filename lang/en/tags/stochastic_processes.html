stochastic processes

15

A random process is a line drawn by luck: stock quotes, noise in a radio receiver, or Brownian motion of pollen.

In practice: Predicting exchange rates, modeling traffic jams, and even generating realistic textures in computer games rely on random processes.
A random process defines a probabilistic rule according to which a system transitions from one state to another over time. Unlike deterministic laws (like an apple falling), we can only talk about the probabilities of various trajectories. For example, radioactive decay of atoms is a Poisson random process: decay times are unpredictable, but the average number of decays per minute is stable.

History

The first random process was described by mathematician Louis Bachelier in 1900 while trying to predict prices on the Paris stock exchange. His work was long overlooked, but later it became the foundation of financial mathematics.

How it works

Tossing a coin, you step left or right. After many tosses, your position will fall somewhere within a wide bell-shaped curve. This random walk is the simplest random process. Similarly, smoke particles move uncertainly as they collide with air molecules.

💡 In 1905, Albert Einstein used a random process (Brownian motion) to prove the existence of atoms — he linked the chaotic movement of pollen to thermal jolts from molecules.
A random process is a mathematical model of how a random variable changes over time. Take a temperature sensor that outputs a new value with error every second. The sequence of these values is a realization of a random process. An individual measurement cannot be predicted, but the process has statistical patterns and average behavior.

A stochastic process is defined as a family of random variables {X(t), t ∈ T}, indexed by parameter t (often time). Major classes include Markov processes, stationary processes, processes with independent increments, and martingales. Key characteristics are the distribution function, expected value, correlation function. The process is fully specified by finite-dimensional distributions. For continuous time, properties of sample paths are important: continuity, differentiability, variation. So the stock ticker quivers from second-by-second jumps to long-term trends — that is the imprint of this very process.

History

The formal theory of stochastic processes began to take shape in the early 20th century with the works of Louis Bachelier (1900) on Brownian motion and financial markets. Norbert Wiener in 1923 built a rigorous mathematical model of Brownian motion (Wiener process). Andrey Kolmogorov in 1931 laid the foundations of the axiomatic theory of random processes. The development of the theory was strongly motivated by problems in physics (diffusion), telecommunications (noise), and finance.

How it works

A stochastic process is described through transition probabilities between states. In discrete time, this can be a chain with given transition probabilities. In continuous time, the dynamics are given by stochastic differential equations (e.g., the Langevin equation for a Brownian particle). For analysis, the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation and the Fokker-Planck equation are often used. A key property — Markov property (the future depends only on the present) — leads to significant simplifications.

dX_t = \mu dt + \sigma dW_t
X_t — stochastic process (particle position), μ — drift coefficient (average drift), σ — fluctuation amplitude (volatility), W_t — standard Wiener process
