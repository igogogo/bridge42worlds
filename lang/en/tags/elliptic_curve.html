elliptic curve

An elliptic curve is like a smooth, curved lock whose latch points combine to form a secret combination. It’s what keeps your online banking safe.

In practice: HTTPS protocols, cryptocurrency wallets, digital signatures—anywhere a fast and secure digital lock is needed.
An elliptic curve is defined by the equation y² = x³ + ax + b. Its graph is a smooth, symmetric curve without self-intersections. On the set of its points (including the point at infinity) one can define an addition operation, turning the curve into a commutative group. This means addition follows the same laws as ordinary numbers: there is a 'zero', an inverse element, and associativity. But the geometry of addition is unusual: to add two points, we draw a secant line through them, find the third intersection with the curve, and reflect it across the x-axis. In cryptography (e.g., the Diffie–Hellman protocol on elliptic curves), security relies on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem: given an initial point P and an endpoint Q = n·P, computing the number n is virtually impossible.

History

First noticed by 19th-century mathematicians, these 'locks' only saw their true potential for secrecy in 1985, when digital signatures on them were invented.

How it works

To lock a message, you take a public generator point (like a well-known lock base) and a secret multiplier. By repeatedly 'turning' the point, you derive a key. An attacker would need to guess the multiplier—like trying an astronomical number of combinations by feel.

💡 Perhaps the most famous elliptic curve is the one connected to Fermat's equation. Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem by establishing a link between elliptic curves and modular forms.

An elliptic curve over a field K is a smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a distinguished point (usually O, the point at infinity). Such a curve is given by a Weierstrass equation: y² = x³ + ax + b, with 4a³ + 27b² ≠ 0. Over the real numbers, the graph consists of one or two components. The key property is the ability to define a group operation on the set of curve points with O as the identity. Geometrically, addition P + Q is formed by drawing a line through P and Q (tangent if P=Q), finding its third intersection R with the curve, and reflecting that point across the x-axis to obtain R' = -R. This group is finite over a finite field and infinite over the rationals, and its rank is a deep arithmetic invariant. Elliptic curves have become central to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, modern algebraic geometry, and cryptography.

History

The study of elliptic curves began with elliptic integrals in the works of Abel and Jacobi in the first half of the 19th century. The term 'elliptic' arose from their connection to computing the arc length of an ellipse. Henri Poincaré contributed to the study of algebraic curves and their groups. In 1985, Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller independently proposed using elliptic curves in cryptography, leading to the widespread ECC systems used today. The link to modular forms, established by Wiles and Taylor, resolved Fermat's Last Theorem.

How it works

The group law on an elliptic curve forms an abelian group. For a curve over a finite field F_p, this group has order about p+1, and the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem in this group underpins cryptographic security. In protocols like ECDSA, a message is signed using a secret key—a multiplier n—and verified with the public key Q = n·P. Computing n from Q and P is the ECDLP, for which no subexponential algorithms are known in general. Additionally, elliptic curves over the rationals carry a rich structure: by the Mordell–Weil theorem the group of rational points is finitely generated, and its rank and regulator are linked to the L-function of the curve via the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture. Thus, the mathematical lock of a smooth curve guards our digital borders.
