Fermi surface

The electron gas in a metal has a clear boundary — like the surface of water in a glass. This is the Fermi surface: it separates filled states from empty ones.

In practice: The shape of the Fermi surface predicts conductivity, magnetic properties, and even the suitability of a material for superconducting magnets.
The Fermi surface is a key characteristic of a metal, determining its transport, thermal, and optical properties. It is an imaginary figure in the space of wave vectors where the electron energy equals the Fermi energy. In monovalent metals like sodium, it is almost a perfect sphere; in copper, “necks” appear connecting adjacent zones; in lead, it has a complex topology. Experimentally, it is mapped using angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) or by measuring quantum oscillations of magnetization (the de Haas–van Alphen effect).

History

In the 1920s, Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac created a statistics for electrons. Later, physicists realized that it is the shape of the Fermi surface that explains why copper is an excellent conductor and bismuth is almost an insulator.

How it works

Apply a voltage, and electrons shift in momentum space. Only those electrons at the Fermi surface contribute to current flow, because deep levels are filled and immobile. If the Fermi surface touches the Brillouin zone boundaries, the metal becomes a magnet or a superconductor.

💡 Bismuth’s Fermi surface consists of tiny pockets containing just one electron per many thousands of atoms, which is why it is such a poor conductor.
In a metal, electrons obey a strict rule (the Pauli exclusion principle): no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state. As it cools to zero, they fill all the lowest energy cells. The highest occupied energy is the Fermi energy. In three-dimensional momentum space (where we plot velocities instead of coordinates), the set of points with the Fermi energy forms a complex surface — the Fermi surface. It determines how the metal will conduct current.

The Fermi surface is an isoenergetic surface in k-space, defined by the equation E(k) = E_F, where E_F is the Fermi energy. At absolute zero temperature, all states with energy below E_F are occupied according to Fermi–Dirac statistics, and those above are empty. The shape of the Fermi surface is determined by the dispersion relations E(k) for the specific crystal and reflects its lattice symmetry. It can be closed or open, multiply connected, and even contain features such as Dirac or Weyl points. The Fermi surface determines electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, magnetism, superconductivity, and optical properties. Changes in the Fermi surface under pressure, doping, or magnetic field underlie many quantum phase transitions.

History

Fermi–Dirac statistics was proposed in 1926 by Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac. The Pauli principle (1925) by Wolfgang Pauli became the foundation. The Sommerfeld free electron model (1928) introduced the concept of Fermi energy and a spherical Fermi surface. In 1930, the de Haas–van Alphen effect allowed experimental measurement of extremal cross-sections, confirming the non-sphericity of the Fermi surface in real metals. Further development of band theory by Bloch, Brillouin, and others led to detailed calculations. Modern ARPES methods (since the 1990s) provide direct images of the Fermi surface.

How it works

Within band theory, an electron in a crystal is described by a Bloch wave and a dispersion E_n(k). Level occupation follows Fermi–Dirac statistics: f(E) = 1/(e^{(E-μ)/k_B T} + 1), where μ is the chemical potential (at T=0, μ = E_F). The Fermi surface is the set of k for which E_n(k) = E_F. When an electric field E is applied, electrons accelerate: ħ dk/dt = -eE, and those near the Fermi surface can change their k, creating a current. The density of states at the Fermi level g(E_F) is proportional to the area of the Fermi surface and the inverse velocity; it determines many properties. Quantum oscillation experiments give the relation for frequency f = (ħ/2πe) S_extr, where S_extr is the area of an extremal cross-section of the Fermi surface. The Pauli principle and Fermi–Dirac statistics are fundamental here. The de Haas–van Alphen effect manifests as oscillations in magnetization in a varying magnetic field.
