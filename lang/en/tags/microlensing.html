Tag

microlensing

A distant star suddenly brightens for several weeks and then fades back. This is an invisible lens—a dark sphere passing exactly in front of it—curving space and focusing the light like a magnifying glass.

In practice: Microlensing reveals 'invisibles': lone rogue planets, black holes, dark dwarfs. This is how dark matter is mapped and distant icy worlds are discovered.
Gravitational microlensing is a phenomenon where an unresolved massive object (with mass ranging from a planet to a star) temporarily magnifies a distant source. Light rays are deflected by the lens's gravity, forming two images that merge into one spot. Amplification depends on the alignment and mass of the lens, reaching a maximum at the smallest angular distance. The light curve has a characteristic symmetry, and when a planetary companion is present, it produces sharp spikes—caustics (sharp brightness peaks). The method does not require the lens itself to shine, making it ideal for searching for substellar bodies and dark clumps.

History

Albert Einstein derived this effect in 1936, but thought it impossible to observe. It was not until 1993 that astronomers detected the first microlensing event: a star in the Large Magellanic Cloud brightened exactly as predicted by theory.

How it works

The gravity of a massive body warps the fabric of spacetime (the unified stage where events unfold). A passing ray goes around this warp. When the body, source, and observer align, an Einstein ring (a perfect circle of light) appears—so tiny that telescopes only see the total magnification. The rise and fall last for weeks—a unique signature of an invisible object.

💡 In 2017, microlensing revealed a lone planet the size of Jupiter wandering without a parent star through interstellar darkness.
Microlensing is nature's magnifying glass. A typical event lasts a month, and the star becomes several times brighter. When a massive object (star, planet) passes exactly between a distant star and Earth, its gravity bends the light rays. The distant star brightens for a while. From the shape of this brightening, astronomers determine the mass and distance of the lens, even if it does not shine itself.

Microlensing is a transient regime of gravitational lensing with lens masses ranging from planetary to stellar (10²²–10³³ kg). The angular separation of images is on the order of microarcseconds, unresolvable directly; what is observed is a flux magnification. For a point lens, the light curve follows the Paczynski shape: A(u) = (u²+2)/(u√(u²+4)), where u is the angular source–lens distance in units of the Einstein radius. The Einstein radius θ_E = √[(4GM/c²)(D_ls/(D_l D_s))] sets the scale. Binary lenses and lenses with planets create caustic networks; when the source crosses a caustic, it produces sharp peaks that reveal the system's parameters. Microlensing is used for exoplanet statistics (including free-floating ones), searches for massive compact halo objects (MACHOs), and studying stellar atmospheres through chromatic effects. Thus a fleeting flash in the night becomes the signature of another world, momentarily caught in the focus of a gravitational lens.

History

Einstein in 1936 calculated the magnification for a star lens, but considered the event incredibly rare. In the 1960s, Refsdal and others pointed out its observational potential. The first detection came in 1993 by the MACHO project: monitoring millions of stars confirmed dark compact bodies in the Milky Way's halo. Surveys like OGLE and MOA now record thousands of events per year, discovering exoplanets and placing constraints on dark matter.

How it works

The lens equation relates the true and observed positions of the source. For a point mass, magnification is maximum at minimum u. The lens's motion relative to the line of sight gives a time-dependent u(t), producing a symmetric light curve of duration t_E. With a second mass (a planet), critical curves and caustics appear in the source plane; when the source crosses a caustic, the brightness changes sharply. Analysis of such anomalies yields the mass ratio and projected separation. Chromatic microlensing uses the absorption difference between the center and edge of the source disk, enabling reconstruction of the brightness profile of a distant star.

\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2} \frac{D_{ls}}{D_l D_s}}
θ_E — Einstein angular radius, G — gravitational constant (6.67430×10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²), M — lens mass, c — speed of light (299,792,458 m/s), D_l — distance to the lens, D_s — distance to the source, D_ls — distance from the lens to the source
