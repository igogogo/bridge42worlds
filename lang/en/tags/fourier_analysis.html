Fourier analysis

3

A tangled graph, like a dance of complex figures, can be broken down into smooth sine waves. Fourier analysis turns any oscillation — from sound to stock prices — into a set of simple waves and shows which frequencies are louder.

In practice: Mobile communication, the internet, and JPEG image compression use Fourier analysis to transmit and store data in compressed form.
Fourier analysis is not just a mathematical trick. It is a way to see through chaos. A stock chart that seems random may contain hidden cycles; an old film marred by scratches is restored by removing periodic noise. Even the shape of a musical instrument — a violin or a flute — is determined by the spectrum of harmonics it produces. In radio engineering, Fourier analysis allows hundreds of conversations to be superimposed on one carrier frequency and then separated again. And in computed tomography, doctors obtain a three-dimensional image of the body by processing X-ray detector signals using the fast Fourier transform.

History

The French mathematician Jean-Baptiste Fourier in the early 19th century studied heat propagation in solids. He realized that any thermal distribution could be represented as a sum of simple trigonometric waves. His idea was initially met with skepticism, but later became the foundation of signal processing.

How it works

The heart of the analysis is a formula that turns a signal recording into a frequency map. Essentially, we ask: 'How similar is my signal to a pure sine wave of a given frequency?' Multiplying the signal by a sine wave and averaging the result yields a number — this is the contribution of that frequency. Repeating this for all possible frequencies builds the spectrum. It's like trying many keys on a lock: each turn gives a measure of fit, and together they reveal the signal's structure.

💡 The human ear is a natural Fourier analyzer: the cochlea of the inner ear breaks down complex sound into individual frequencies, allowing us to distinguish voices in a noisy room.
A musical note seems like a single sound, but in fact it consists of a fundamental tone and many overtones (additional frequencies). Fourier analysis is like separating a chord into individual strings. It takes a signal recorded over time (for example, a sound file) and converts it into a spectrum (frequency decomposition) — a graph where the horizontal axis is frequency (pitch) and the vertical axis is intensity. This allows us to see which frequencies dominate and filter out noise. The principle is universal: bridge vibrations, seismic waves, and X-ray images are analyzed in the same way.

Fourier analysis is a mathematical method of decomposing a function into a sum of trigonometric (sines and cosines) or complex exponential functions. The result of the Fourier transform is a complex-valued function of frequency, whose modulus gives the amplitude spectrum and argument gives the phase spectrum. For discrete signals (sequences), the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and its fast algorithms (FFT) are used. Fourier analysis is based on the orthogonality of harmonic functions and the possibility of representing almost any function as an infinite series (Fourier series) or integral under certain conditions (e.g., Dirichlet conditions). It is widely used for solving differential equations, in spectroscopy, acoustics, communication systems, and computer vision.

History

In 1807, Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier presented a work on heat propagation to the Paris Academy of Sciences, where he claimed that any periodic function could be expanded into a trigonometric series. Leading mathematicians of the time (Lagrange, Laplace) were skeptical due to rigor; full convergence proofs came later (Dirichlet, Riemann). The fast Fourier transform (FFT), which made the algorithm practical, was independently discovered by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, though elements of the method go back to Gauss.

How it works

The forward Fourier transform of a function f(t) is defined by the integral: F(ω) = ∫ f(t) e^{-iωt} dt. The inverse: f(t) = (1/2π) ∫ F(ω) e^{iωt} dω. Physically, the quantity |F(ω)|² dω represents the fraction of signal energy in the frequency band [ω, ω+dω] (Parseval's theorem). In digital processing, the FFT is implemented, with complexity O(N log N) instead of O(N²) for direct DFT. Convolution in the time domain becomes multiplication in the frequency domain, which is convenient for filtering. The short-time Fourier transform adds time localization at the cost of frequency resolution — a compromise overcome by wavelets.

F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i\omega t} \, dt
f(t) — function in time domain, F(ω) — frequency spectrum, ω — angular frequency (ω = 2πν, ν — frequency), i — imaginary unit
f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega) e^{i\omega t} \, d\omega
f(t) is recovered from the spectrum, factor 1/(2π) is normalization
