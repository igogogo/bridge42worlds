brain cancer detection

3

Just as an airport scanner finds a prohibited item, algorithms analyze MRI scans and highlight suspicious tissue areas.

In practice: Helps doctors diagnose at an early stage, reduces the number of false alarms, and precisely determines tumor boundaries before surgery or radiation.
Brain cancer detection using artificial intelligence includes segmentation (delineation) of tumors on MRI images. Convolutional neural networks (CNN) with the U-Net architecture are most effective, processing 3D volumes and classifying each voxel as healthy tissue, necrosis, edema, or tumor. Models are pre-trained on large datasets (e.g., BraTS) with slice-by-slice annotation. The key challenge is distinguishing glioblastoma from metastases and non-tumor formations (abscess, demyelination). Additionally, radiomics (extraction of multiple quantitative features from images) and diffusion-weighted MRI are used.

History

Automatic analysis of medical images began in the 1980s with simple rules (if brightness is above a threshold, it's a tumor). In the 2010s, neural networks (deep learning) emerged that learn complex patterns on their own. Today they surpass humans in speed and accuracy of finding small lesions.

How it works

MRI produces many thin slices of the brain; the program treats each tiny cube (voxel) as a set of numbers: brightness, texture, shape. It looks for combinations that appeared in thousands of annotated tumors. Then it creates a heat map: suspect areas are highlighted in color, like an artist coloring an outline.

💡 Some algorithms can detect a tumor 6–12 months earlier than a radiologist by analyzing barely noticeable changes in signal intensity on consecutive scans.
Detecting brain cancer is searching for tumors using scans (MRI) or blood tests. A computer program, trained on thousands of examples, compares each brain region to those it has seen before and marks areas that look like cancer. It is similar to how an experienced mushroom picker instantly distinguishes an edible mushroom from a poisonous one by signs invisible to a beginner.

Modern computer-aided diagnostic systems for brain cancer integrate multimodal MRI (T1, T1c, T2, FLAIR, DWI) and employ deep neural networks for classification, detection, and segmentation tasks. Leading architectures: nnU-Net — a self-adapting 3D U-Net, Swin UNETR with transformers for global context, and ensembles of models with post-processing via conditional random fields. For small datasets, augmentation (rotations, contrast changes) and GAN synthesis are used. Beyond accuracy, prediction uncertainty (Bayesian networks, MC Dropout) and interpretability (Grad-CAM, saliency maps) are evaluated. In the clinic, speed matters — inference within seconds on GPU, as well as compliance with DICOM standards and integration into PACS systems. Promising directions: self-contrastive learning on unlabeled scans and multi-task models that simultaneously predict histology, mutation status, and prognosis.

History

The development of computer-aided brain cancer diagnosis began with expert systems in the 1980s that used thresholding and texture filters. In 1998, the first CAD package for MRI appeared, and in the 2010s, public BraTS (Brain Tumor Segmentation Challenge) competitions accelerated progress. The pivotal moment was the victory of convolutional networks in 2015 (DeepMedic). Since 2018, U-Net-based models and its 3D modifications became the standard. In the 2020s, the FDA approved several AI systems (e.g., icobrain) to assist in clinical decisions, although widespread adoption is hindered by scanner and protocol variability.

How it works

A typical pipeline: (1) preprocessing — intensity normalization, skull stripping, registration to an atlas; (2) an input 3D patch from four channels (T1, T1c, T2, FLAIR) is fed into a U-Net encoder that extracts hierarchical features from edges to texture; (3) the decoder restores spatial resolution with skip connections, outputting probability maps for each class; (4) the loss function is usually a combination of Dice Loss and cross-entropy, accounting for class imbalance; (5) post-processing — removal of small false spots using connected components and morphological operations. To increase robustness, activation consistency is checked under small distortions of the input image (test-time augmentation). The integrated system visualizes the segmentation in the clinician's interface, providing quantitative metrics (volume, radiomics).

Open problems & fun facts

Lack of well-annotated MRI data, especially for rare tumor subtypes and post-surgical changes.; Black box: it is difficult to explain why exactly this voxel was classified as tumor by the neural network — a problem of physician trust.; Early diagnosis of infiltrative gliomas, which in initial stages do not breach the blood-brain barrier and enhance poorly on contrast.
