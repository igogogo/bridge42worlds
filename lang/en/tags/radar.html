Tag

radar

A bat in a night forest emits a squeak. The echo returns with a delay, drawing a map of branches in its brain. Radar is an artificial echo sounder that sees in the dark.

In practice: Radar measures the speed of your car, helps airplanes land in fog, and predicts thunderstorms by detecting raindrops hundreds of kilometers away.
Radar is an active radio-location system that emits electromagnetic waves and analyzes their reflections from targets. Modern radars use complex pulse sequences (chirps) with linear frequency modulation, allowing high range resolution (down to centimeters) at moderate peak power. The receiver measures the time of flight τ, amplitude, phase, and frequency of the returned signal, calculating range, size, speed, and even shape of the object. Doppler shift gives radial velocity, and beam steering (scanning) gives angular coordinates. Pulsed radars alternate transmission and reception; Doppler radars transmit continuously and filter the reflected signal from stationary clutter.

History

The first radar for aircraft detection was built by Scottish physicist Robert Watson-Watt in 1935. By 1940, a network of radars along the British coast helped win the Battle of Britain.

How it works

Imagine you shout toward a mountain: the shout reflects, and you hear an echo. The farther the mountain, the longer you wait. Radar does the same with radio waves, but much faster—the pulse lasts millionths of a second. The target's speed is found from the pitch of the returned signal: if a car approaches, the echo becomes “denser” (higher frequency).

💡 The radar installation at the Arecibo Observatory could measure the distance to the Moon with an accuracy of a few centimeters—that's like the thickness of a finger at a distance of 384,000 kilometers.
Radar (short for Radio Detection and Ranging) works on the principle of echo. An antenna emits a short radio pulse, which reflects off an object and returns. The delay time gives the distance: each microsecond of delay corresponds to 150 meters. The change in signal frequency (Doppler effect) measures the target's speed.

Radar (radio detection and ranging) is a device for detecting, measuring coordinates, and determining motion parameters of targets using radio waves. The cornerstone is the radar equation: received signal power P_r = (P_t G_t G_r λ^2 σ) / ((4π)^3 R^4), where σ is the target's radar cross-section, P_t is transmitter power, G are antenna gains, λ is wavelength, and R is distance. Modern radars use phased array antennas (AESA) for electronic beam scanning without mechanical rotation. Coherent processing (Doppler filtering) extracts moving targets against the background of the underlying surface. Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) is used to obtain radar images of terrain with sub-meter resolution.

History

The idea of radar was proposed by Tesla as early as 1900, but practical implementation began in the 1930s simultaneously in several countries. Robert Watson-Watt in 1935 demonstrated aircraft detection at a distance of 27 km. The key invention was the cavity magnetron (1940, Boot and Randall), which enabled compact, powerful microwave radars. After the war, civilian applications—air navigation, meteorology, astronomy (radio telescopes)—came to the fore. The 1970s saw the introduction of digital signal processing, and the 21st century brought multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) radars.

How it works

Radar emits a coherent beam of electromagnetic waves (usually in the microwave band) and receives the reflected signal. The time delay Δt determines range: R = cΔt/2. Doppler frequency shift f_d = 2v/λ gives radial velocity v. Phased arrays steer the beam by changing the phase of the signal at each element, without moving the antenna. Pulse compression improves range resolution: a long chirp with frequency modulation is transmitted, and upon reception it is convolved with a matched filter to obtain a short response. In SAR, a moving platform (satellite, aircraft) uses its displacement to form an artificially synthesized aperture, whose length is proportional to the distance traveled, yielding resolution far better than a stationary antenna of real size.

P_r = \frac{P_t G_t G_r \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4}
P_r — received signal power, P_t — transmitter power, G_t, G_r — gain of transmitting and receiving antennas, λ — wavelength, σ — radar cross-section of the target, R — distance to the target, constant 4π from spherical front geometry
R = \frac{c \Delta t}{2}
R — range, c — speed of light (c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s), Δt — delay time of the reflected signal, division by 2 accounts for the round trip
f_d = \frac{2 v}{\lambda}
f_d — Doppler frequency shift, v — radial velocity of the target, λ — wavelength of the radiation
