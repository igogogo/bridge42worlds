phylogenetic network

5

Like intertwined tree branches, evolutionary lines do not always diverge — sometimes they merge, forming a network of interwoven connections.

In practice: Phylogenetic networks help track the spread of viruses (e.g., COVID-19) and identify hybrid plant species for breeding.
A phylogenetic network is a model of evolutionary relationships that, in addition to branching (speciation), accounts for reticulation events: horizontal gene transfer, hybridization, recombination. Unlike a phylogenetic tree, the network contains nodes with multiple parent ancestors, making it more suitable for describing the evolution of prokaryotes, viruses, and some plants.

History

The idea arose in the late 20th century when scientists realized that ordinary evolutionary trees do not explain all cases. With the development of genetics and computers, networks began to be constructed from the 1990s.

How it works

Evolution is like a river delta: the main flow splits into branches, but branches can reconnect. So here: species diverge, but sometimes exchange genetic material, creating a network instead of a tree.

💡 Up to 10% of the human genome may be of viral origin, and network models help trace these unusual genetic connections.
A phylogenetic network is a diagram showing kinship relations between species, like a family tree, but with 'bridges' between branches. These bridges mean that genes could be transferred directly from one species to another (horizontal gene transfer) or that populations interbred (hybridization). Unlike a simple tree, the network better reflects the tangled history of life.

A phylogenetic network is a graph structure representing evolutionary relationships between taxa, allowing for reticulation events (horizontal gene transfer, hybridization, introgression, recombination). Mathematically, it can be either a rooted or unrooted graph, often with variations: split networks, recombination networks, overlay networks. Algorithms for their construction are based on optimality methods (least squares, maximum likelihood, Bayesian inference) and combinatorial approaches (split decomposition, neighbor-net). Unlike trees, networks can model non-vertical transmission of hereditary information, which is critical for understanding the evolution of prokaryotes and viruses.

History

The concept of phylogenetic networks arose in response to discrepancies between gene trees and species trees. In the 1980s, Walter Fitch realized the need to account for horizontal transfer, and in the 1990s, with the development of sequencing methods and computer power, the first algorithms for network construction appeared (e.g., the split decomposition algorithm of Bandelt and Dress, 1992). Pioneers of computational phylogenetics included David Swofford, Joseph Felsenstein, and later David Bryant, Daniel Huson, and others who developed modern methods.

How it works

Construction of a phylogenetic network begins with the choice of an evolutionary model and data (usually DNA sequences). Algorithms estimate distances or likelihoods and search for a topology that minimizes the number of reticulations or maximizes likelihood. Networks can be explicit — where reticulation nodes denote specific transfer events — or implicit, showing alternative evolutionary scenarios as intertwined structures (e.g., split networks). Validation and network selection are often performed using information criteria (AIC, BIC) and bootstrap.

Open problems & fun facts

Development of efficient algorithms for large datasets with hundreds of genomes.; Distinguishing true phylogenetic signal from noise and incomplete lineage sorting.; Integration of fossil data into network models.; Quantitative assessment of uncertainty and statistical support for individual reticulations.
