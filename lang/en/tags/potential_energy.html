potential energy

A boulder on top of a hill. It is motionless, but inside there is tension, ready to turn into a rapid descent. This hidden force is potential energy, the reserve of position.

In practice: Hydroelectric power plants convert water's potential energy into electricity. Any object at height stores energy ready to do work — from a clock weight to a hanging chandelier.
The energy hidden in stationary objects is called potential by physicists. It comes in different forms: gravitational (associated with height in a gravitational field), elastic (compression or stretching of materials), electrical (charges in a field). All these types share a principle: the work that a system can perform is determined by its configuration. When a stone falls down, its potential melts away, turning into speed. On cosmic scales, gravitational potential energy is the main source of starlight: as a gas-dust cloud contracts, it heats up and ignites a thermonuclear furnace.

History

People guessed about hidden energy since ancient times: water flows downhill, a stone falls. But the scientific concept was formalized in the 19th century by engineer William Rankine. Later, Emmy Noether showed that the energy reserve is linked to the symmetry of time — her theorem explained why energy does not disappear, but only changes form.

How it works

Potential energy depends on the object's location in a force field. In Earth's gravity field, it increases with height: a kilogram weight on a table stores more energy than on the floor. Falling, it accelerates — potential energy transforms into kinetic energy (the energy of motion). Similarly, water falling from a dam spins a turbine.

💡 A tablespoon of neutron star matter, if it fell to Earth, would release as much energy as a powerful atomic bomb — thanks to the enormous gravitational potential energy.
The drawn bowstring freezes. Muscles are tensed, but there is no motion. Energy seems to sleep. This is potential energy — a reserve created by position or state. A stone on a mountain slope, a compressed spring, clouds above the plain — they all store the capacity to act, just waiting for a push. When equilibrium is broken, hidden power flows into visible motion — the rotation of a turbine, the flight of an arrow. So too the boulder from the top: rolling down, it can smash an obstacle, spending all the potential accumulated by height.

Potential energy is a scalar physical quantity that characterizes the ability of a system to do work by virtue of its position or configuration. It is introduced only for conservative forces (those whose work does not depend on the path but on the initial and final states). Mathematically, the change in potential energy equals the negative of the work done by the conservative force. For gravitational interaction in Newtonian mechanics, U = -GMm/r, and near the Earth's surface U = mgh. The elastic potential energy of a spring is proportional to the square of deformation: U = kx^2/2. The energy of electrostatic interaction of point charges is computed from Coulomb's law and is also potential energy. In deeper theories, such as general relativity, there is no strict concept of gravitational potential energy because gravity is described by spacetime curvature, and the energy of the gravitational field is not localizable.

History

The term 'potential energy' was introduced in 1853 by the Scottish engineer and physicist William Rankine. However, the roots of the idea go back to Galileo and Leibniz, who spoke of 'living force' (vis viva) and 'dead force'. Lagrange and Hamilton embedded potential into analytical mechanics, making it the foundation of describing motion. In 1915, Emmy Noether proved that the law of conservation of energy (and hence the rigorous definition of potential function) follows from the homogeneity of time. Today, potential energy remains a key concept from quantum mechanics to cosmology.

How it works

In mechanics, the change in potential energy dU is related to the work dA done by a conservative force: dU = -dA = -F·dr. The potential is defined up to an arbitrary constant; only potential differences have physical meaning. In the Earth's gravitational field, U = mgh, where h is measured from a chosen reference level. In general, the gravitational potential of a point mass M is φ = -GM/r, and the potential energy of a test body of mass m is U = mφ. In electrodynamics, the scalar potential φ and vector potential A form a four-dimensional potential that determines field strengths. Quantum mechanics uses the potential energy operator in the Schrödinger equation. The gravitational potential energy of a uniform sphere (e.g., a star) determines its internal thermal history and stability.
