optical microscopy

2

A light microscope is a tube with lenses that magnifies tiny objects, making the world of bacteria, cells, and crystals visible. Like binoculars for distant stars, but in reverse—for the very close.

In practice: Used in biology and medicine for cell analysis, in materials science for inspecting microchips, and in forensics for examining fibers and prints.
An optical microscope forms an image by refracting visible light through a series of lenses. Resolution is limited by diffraction: according to the Rayleigh criterion, two points are distinguishable if the distance between them is at least half the wavelength divided by the numerical aperture of the objective. Modern methods—fluorescence microscopy, confocal, super-resolution (STED, PALM)—overcome this limit, allowing visualization of structures down to single molecules.

History

The first microscopes were built in the late 16th century in the Netherlands. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in the 17th century crafted lenses with huge magnification and first saw bacteria and red blood cells. Later scientists improved the design, and today microscopes can distinguish details smaller than the wavelength of light.

How it works

Light from a lamp passes through a condenser that directs it onto the specimen. The objective lens creates a magnified, inverted image, and the eyepiece further magnifies it for the eye or camera. To see transparent cells, they are stained or phase contrast is used, which turns invisible phase changes into brightness variations.

💡 With an optical microscope, you can not only look but also move objects: laser tweezers can hold and move individual cells or bacteria.
An optical microscope collects light that passes through the specimen and magnifies it using a system of lenses. The lenses work like a magnifying glass, but much stronger: you can see things hundreds of times thinner than a hair. Special illumination helps highlight transparent details.

Optical microscopy is a set of methods for observing microscopic objects using visible or near-infrared/ultraviolet radiation. A basic brightfield microscope consists of an illumination system (source, condenser), objective, tube, and eyepiece. Image formation is described by Abbe's theory, according to which the microscope acts as a double Fourier analyzer: first a diffraction pattern is created in the back focal plane of the objective, then interference of diffracted beams reconstructs the image. Resolution d = λ/(2 NA) is determined by the wavelength λ and the numerical aperture NA = n sin θ. Modern techniques include: confocal microscopy (suppression of out-of-focus light), two-photon (excitation in the infrared range, depth penetration), super-resolution (STED – stimulated emission depletion, PALM/STORM – single-molecule activation), light sheet (SPIM – reduced photodamage).

History

Zacharias Janssen is credited with constructing a compound microscope (1590). Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1674) achieved 300x magnification with single lenses, discovering microorganisms. Robert Hooke in 'Micrographia' (1665) described cells. In 1873, Ernst Abbe developed the theory of image formation and the sine condition. Carl Zeiss and Otto Schott established industrial production of high-quality objectives. In the 20th century, Frits Zernike invented the phase-contrast microscope (1935, Nobel 1953), Marvin Minsky patented the confocal principle (1957). Super-resolution emerged in the 2000s: STED by Stefan Hell (2014 Nobel), PALM by Eric Betzig and Harold Hess.

How it works

A beam of light from the source is focused by the condenser onto the specimen. Scattered and directly transmitted light is collected by the objective, which forms a primary image in the plane of the field diaphragm. The eyepiece further magnifies it for viewing by eye or transmission to a detector. Contrast in brightfield microscopes arises from absorption or scattering; phase contrast converts a phase shift into an amplitude change. In fluorescence microscopes, the specimen is illuminated at the excitation wavelength, and emitted radiation is separated by a dichroic mirror and filters. In confocal microscopy, a point source and a detector with a pinhole provide optical sectioning. Super-resolution overcomes the diffraction limit by turning off fluorophores at the periphery of the excitation spot (STED) or by precise localization of single photoactivatable molecules (PALM/STORM).

d = \frac{\lambda}{2 \, \text{NA}}
d – minimum resolvable distance, λ – wavelength of light, NA – numerical aperture of the objective (NA = n sin θ, where n is the refractive index of the medium, θ is half the angle of the light cone entering the objective).
