Braess's paradox in biology

2

Sometimes a new path worsens the overall picture, as if adding a new branch in the tree of blood vessels made the heart work harder.

In practice: Understanding this paradox helps biologists design optimal systems in synthetic biology and better explain strange failures in the body's natural networks.
Braess's paradox occurs in networks with selfish or decentralized route choice. The classic example is road traffic: opening a new street can increase travel time for each driver. In biology, this phenomenon is observed in transport networks (vascular systems, mycelium), in metabolic pathways, and even in population genetics. Adding a new 'channel' or regulatory mechanism that seems beneficial to each element actually reduces overall performance due to overload of bottlenecks.

History

The paradox was discovered by the German mathematician Dietrich Braess in 1968 while studying traffic flow. Later, scientists found that similar problems exist in biological and chemical systems.

How it works

Ant trail: if you build a new bridge that seems shorter but creates a jam at the exit, it becomes harder for everyone to get food. Similarly, in cellular networks, an 'extra' connection redirects flows suboptimally because each part of the network selfishly chooses its own path.

💡 The slime mold Physarum polycephalum has no brain, but it builds an efficient transport network. When scientists artificially created additional connections, the network degraded instead of improving — a pure Braess's paradox.
Braess's paradox is when adding something beneficial actually ruins everything. In biology, this happens in networks: for example, in the slime mold Physarum polycephalum, which builds transport routes. If you create an additional tunnel, nutrients will be delivered more slowly to the edges. It's like how an extra wire in a complex electrical circuit causes a short circuit.

Braess's paradox is a counterintuitive property of network systems with non-cooperative equilibrium, where adding a resource (edge) worsens overall efficiency if each agent locally optimizes their cost function. In biology, the paradox has been described for transport networks (plant vascular networks, neural connections), metabolic pathways, population models, and ecological systems. It demonstrates that evolutionarily developed networks can avoid 'redundant' connections precisely because of such a non-optimal equilibrium.

History

Dietrich Braess, a German mathematician, formulated the paradox in a 1968 paper for transportation planning. However, there were anticipations by Ailing and others. In biology, the paradox began to be actively discussed in the 2000s after experiments with Physarum polycephalum networks and metabolic models. Early microscopic observations of biological structures by Robert Hooke laid the foundation for studying networks, although the paradox was not realized by him.

How it works

Consider a network where flow Q must pass from A to B. There are two paths: through node 1 and through node 2. Travel time on an edge depends on the flow f: T(f). Suppose selfish agents minimize their time, and Nash equilibrium is reached when the time on all used paths is equal. If a new edge is added connecting nodes 1 and 2 with zero travel time, initially it seems attractive. But in the new equilibrium, all agents use the new edge, redistributing flow so that the overall average time increases. Mathematically, this is expressed through a system of nonlinear equilibrium equations. In biological networks, the role of 'time' is played by flow resistance or metabolic cost. Principles of dissipation minimization are applied, but taking into account selfishness at the level of cells or organisms.

T(f) = a + b f
T(f) — travel time on the edge depending on flow f, a — time at zero flow (constant), b — delay coefficient (sensitivity to congestion)
T(f_1) + T(f_2) = T(f_3) + T(f_4)
T(f_i) — time on edge i, the sum of times on two paths from A to B must be equal in equilibrium
