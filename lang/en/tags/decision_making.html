decision making

15

Choosing one option out of several when the outcomes are not always clear. It's like choosing a path in the fog: we rely on fragmented data and gut feeling.

In practice: It is used everywhere: from everyday purchases to financial management and artificial intelligence strategies. By learning to model this process, we create smart assistants capable of advising or making decisions.
Decision making is a cognitive process of choosing among alternatives, studied in psychology, neuroscience, economics and mathematics. Classical theories (expected utility) assume a rational agent maximizing benefit. However, experiments have revealed heuristics (simplified rules) and biases: people are subject to the framing effect, avoid losses more than they seek gains, and are time-inconsistent. Modern models include quantum-like formalisms to describe context dependence and violations of the law of total probability.

History

Back in the 17th century, Blaise Pascal discussed bets and probabilities. Then economists built a coherent theory of rational choice, but in the 20th century psychologists showed that people systematically deviate from rationality.

How it works

The brain weighs different desires: 'I want something sweet' and 'I need something healthy'. A 'tug-of-war' occurs between them, and the strongest at that moment wins.

💡 If people always acted rationally, lotteries would not exist because the expected value of a ticket is always less than its price.
This is how we choose: for example, what to eat for breakfast or whether to go to the movies. Scientists study why sometimes the choice is obvious and other times we struggle. It turns out that emotions, past experience, and even the order in which we receive information influence the decision. Simple mathematical models (like counting pros and cons) do not always work because people are not computers.

Decision making refers to the process of choosing between alternatives under uncertainty, risk, or certainty. Decision theory combines normative models (how one should act) and descriptive models (how people actually act). The normative approach dates back to the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, where rationality is defined by consistency of preferences. Descriptive models (Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory) introduce a value function that reflects diminishing sensitivity and loss aversion. In quantum decision models, the agent's state is represented as a superposition, and the outcome is measured, explaining question order effects and interference effects.

History

Decision analysis began with Pascal and Fermat in the 17th century in correspondence about gambling problems. In the 20th century, von Neumann and Morgenstern formalized expected utility theory. Later, Herbert Simon introduced the concept of bounded rationality. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky experimentally demonstrated systematic biases, for which Kahneman won the Nobel Prize. David Deutsch contributed to quantum computing, which provided tools for quantum-like decision models.

How it works

Classical approach: a utility function U(x) assigns a number to each outcome, and the action maximizing expected utility E[U(x)] = ∑ P_i U(x_i) is chosen. Prospect theory replaces this with weighted value: V = ∑ π(p_i) v(x_i), where π(p) is the decision weight, v(x) is the value function, concave for gains and convex for losses. In the quantum model, the state |ψ⟩ in Hilbert space evolves unitarily until a 'question' which is described by a projector, and the probability of the answer is given by the squared modulus of the amplitude. The principle of superposition and Born's rule are applied.

EU = \sum_i p_i U(x_i)
EU — expected utility, p_i — probability of outcome i, U(x_i) — utility of outcome x_i (subjective value)
V = \sum_i \pi(p_i) v(\Delta x_i)
V — subjective value of the decision, π(p_i) — probability weight (distorted probability), v(Δx_i) — value function of the change relative to the reference point (Δx_i — gain or loss)
