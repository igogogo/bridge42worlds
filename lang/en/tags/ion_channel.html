ion channel

2

Ion channels are microscopic gates in the cell membrane that allow electrically charged particles (ions) to pass in or out. Just as a heart valve ensures blood flow in one direction, a channel controls the flow of sodium, potassium, or calcium, creating electrical signals in nerves and muscles.

In practice: Ion channels are targets for many drugs: painkillers block sodium channels, while medications for epilepsy and arrhythmia stabilize their function. Channel dysfunction causes severe hereditary diseases.
Ion channels are transmembrane proteins that form water-filled pores with a diameter of a few angstroms. They provide passive transport of Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻ ions through the lipid bilayer of the cell membrane down the electrochemical gradient. Channel selectivity is determined by the pore size and the arrangement of charged amino acids at the mouth. Channels have two key properties: conductance (the ability to pass an ion current) and a gating mechanism that switches the protein between open, closed, and inactivated states. According to activation type, there are voltage-gated, ligand-gated, and mechanosensitive channels.

History

The first ideas about the existence of pores in the membrane emerged at the beginning of the 20th century when physiologists noticed that the cell membrane allows potassium and sodium ions to pass through differently. The decisive breakthrough occurred in the 1950s thanks to Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley, who mathematically described ion currents in the giant squid axon. They suggested that there were separate pathways for sodium and potassium. Later, in the 1970s–80s, using the patch clamp technique (a microelectrode technique that allows the study of single channels), it became possible to record how a single ion channel opens and closes.

How it works

At rest, many channels are closed, maintaining different concentrations of ions inside and outside the cell—like water behind a dam. A stimulus (for example, a nerve impulse) serves as a signal to open the sluice: the channel protein changes its shape, forming a through pore. Ions rush through it according to the concentration gradient (from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration), much like air escaping from a punctured balloon. This flow generates an electric current that transmits the signal further.

💡 A single channel allows up to 100 million ions per second to pass through, yet remains remarkably selective: a potassium channel passes a potassium ion 10,000 times better than a smaller sodium ion.
Every living cell is surrounded by a membrane (a very thin fat film) that does not allow charged particles—ions—to pass through. For ions to still be able to enter and exit, there are special channel proteins in the membrane. They work like highly selective tubes: a sodium channel allows only sodium ions to pass, a potassium channel—only potassium ions. Controlling the opening of a channel resembles a door with a combination lock: some channels open in response to a change in electrical voltage across the membrane, others—when a signaling molecule (for example, a neurotransmitter) attaches to them.

Ion channels are integral membrane proteins that form an ion-conducting pathway through the hydrophobic barrier of the lipid bilayer. Their structure includes a pore formed by α-helices or β-sheets, and sensory domains that detect the stimulus. The classical model of the voltage-gated potassium channel Kv demonstrates a tetrameric architecture with a central pore and four voltage-sensing domains containing regularly spaced positively charged arginine residues. Upon membrane depolarization, these charges shift in the electric field, causing a conformational rearrangement that opens the gate. Ion selectivity is achieved through a selectivity filter—a narrowing of the pore lined with carbonyl oxygens that precisely coordinate the dehydrated ion of the appropriate size. The kinetics of transitions between states are described by Markov schemes, and the single-channel current obeys Ohm's law on a microscopic scale: i = γ(V – E_rev), where γ is the channel conductance.

History

The concept of selective ion pores originated in the works of Julius Bernstein (1902), but experimental confirmation came with the work of Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley (1952), who, studying the giant squid axon, separated sodium and potassium currents and proposed a mathematical model of excitation. The patch clamp technique, developed by Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann (1976), allowed recording currents through single channels, for which they received the Nobel Prize. The three-dimensional structure of a potassium channel was first deciphered by Roderick MacKinnon in 1998 using X-ray crystallography, confirming the hypothesis of the selectivity filter and gating mechanism.

How it works

The functioning of an ion channel is based on the laws of electrostatics and the thermodynamics of irreversible processes. The driving force for ion flow is the electrochemical gradient, determined by the concentration difference (chemical component) and the membrane potential (electrical component). The equilibrium potential for an ion is given by the Nernst equation: E_eq = (RT/zF) ln([X]_out/[X]_in). The selectivity filter uses the principle of complementarity: the pore size and the arrangement of polar groups exactly match the radius of the hydrated or dehydrated ion. For the K⁺ channel, selectivity is explained by the 'stripping' of the hydration shell and stabilization of the ion in the filter cavity by carbonyl atoms, which is energetically unfavorable for the smaller Na⁺, which is coordinated too weakly. The gating mechanism obeys stochastic dynamics: thermal fluctuations lead to random transitions between conformational states, giving rise to the so-called 'shot noise' of the current (ion channel noise). Mathematically, the behavior of an ensemble of channels is described by the Hodgkin–Huxley kinetic equations or, for a single channel, by the distribution of open and closed state durations. The Pauli principle is not relevant, since ions are distinguishable classical particles. A deeper level involves molecular dynamics calculations using the Schrödinger equation for the electron subsystem, but in practice, force fields are used.
