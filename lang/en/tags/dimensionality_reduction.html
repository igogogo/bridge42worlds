dimensionality reduction

1

Compress voluminous data into a simple map, like a geographical atlas turns the three-dimensional world into two-dimensional pages, preserving the essentials.

In practice: Accelerating image search, compressing medical images, visualizing complex relationships, for example, in genetics.
The process of transforming data from a high-dimensional feature space to a low-dimensional one with minimal loss of significant information. Used both for visualization (projecting onto 2D or 3D) and as preprocessing before machine learning to reduce computational complexity and avoid the 'curse of dimensionality' (degradation of algorithm performance as the number of features grows).

History

In the early 20th century, Karl Pearson invented principal component analysis — the first tool for such compression. Later, many other methods appeared.

How it works

The method finds new, simpler coordinates for the data, so that important differences between objects are not lost. It's like looking at the shadow of an object: a shadow is flat (lower dimensionality), but it's often clear what the object is.

💡 Principal component analysis (PCA) is often used to compress facial images: a 10,000-pixel snapshot can be transmitted with just a hundred numbers, and the face remains recognizable.
Data often has many features — for example, a photograph has millions of pixels. Dimensionality reduction is a way to preserve the most important information by removing the superfluous. It's as if an artist drew a portrait with just a few lines, but the person can still be recognized.

A set of mathematical methods that map points from an n-dimensional vector space to an m-dimensional one, where m < n, while preserving certain structural properties. Linear methods (PCA, SVD, factor analysis) construct a projection that maximizes variance or minimizes reconstruction error. Nonlinear methods (t-SNE, UMAP, autoencoders) better preserve local neighborhoods and cluster structure, which is important for visualization. In machine learning, dimensionality reduction addresses feature selection and feature extraction tasks, combating multicollinearity, overfitting, and computational costs.

History

Principal component analysis (PCA) was proposed by Karl Pearson in 1901; later independently developed by Harold Hotelling in the 1930s. In the 1960s–70s, multidimensional scaling methods appeared. The t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithm was introduced by Laurens van der Maaten and Geoffrey Hinton in 2008, revolutionizing visualization. UMAP (2018) improved global structure preservation and speed.

How it works

Linear PCA method: compute the covariance matrix of the data; its eigenvectors corresponding to the largest eigenvalues form a new orthonormal basis; projecting onto the first m eigenvectors gives the reduced representation. Nonlinear autoencoder: a neural network with a bottleneck is trained to reconstruct the input; the hidden representation in the bottleneck serves as a low-dimensional code. t-SNE models the probabilities that a point is a neighbor of another in the original and target spaces, minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence between them.

Z = X W_m
Z — data matrix in reduced dimensionality (N×m), X — original centered data matrix (N×n), W_m — matrix of m eigenvectors of the covariance matrix X^T X, corresponding to the m largest eigenvalues

Open problems & fun facts

Choosing the optimal dimensionality m is often heuristic; Difficulty in interpreting latent variables of nonlinear methods; Preserving global structure under strong compression; Scalability to very large data (millions of points)
