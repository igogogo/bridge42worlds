bioinformatics

6

Bioinformatics is a 'translator' from the language of biology to the language of mathematics and back. It helps find meaning in mountains of biological data, just as a decoder reads encrypted messages.

In practice: Thanks to bioinformatics, scientists can find bacterial antibiotic resistance genes in a matter of days and develop new vaccines by analyzing protein sequences.
Bioinformatics is an interdisciplinary field that develops methods and software tools for understanding biological data. It combines computer science, statistics, mathematics, and engineering to analyze and interpret high-throughput experimental data (genomics, proteomics, metabolomics). Key tasks include genome annotation, protein structure prediction, evolutionary analysis, and systems biology.

History

Bioinformatics emerged in the 1960s with the first computer programs for protein sequence analysis, but it truly flourished in the 1990s with the start of the Human Genome Project.

How it works

Just as a search engine finds similar phrases in different books, bioinformatics algorithms compare genes from different organisms to identify common functions or ancestors.

💡 The world's largest database of genetic sequences (GenBank) doubles roughly every 18 months and now contains trillions of nucleotides—more than the number of stars in the Milky Way.
Bioinformatics bridges biology and computer science. If a genome is a huge text, bioinformatics is the software that reads, compares, and finds errors in that text. It uses computers to store, search, and analyze biological data (DNA sequences, protein structures, etc.). Note: sequencing is reading the order of 'letters' in DNA.

Bioinformatics is a scientific discipline that encompasses the creation and application of computational algorithms, statistical models, databases, and visualizations to solve biological problems. It includes sequence analysis (alignment, motif search, phylogenetics), structural bioinformatics (protein folding prediction, molecular docking), systems biology (modeling of gene networks, metabolic pathways), and the processing of single-cell and metagenomic data. Machine learning, especially deep neural networks (AlphaFold), has revolutionized the prediction of three-dimensional protein structures.

History

The roots of bioinformatics go back to the 1960s, when Margaret Oakley Dayhoff created the first protein sequence data bank and amino acid substitution matrices. In the 1970s, the Needleman–Wunsch and Smith–Waterman sequence alignment algorithms were developed. The Human Genome Project (1990–2003) provided a powerful impetus, requiring the assembly and annotation of an entire genome. In the last decade, progress has accelerated thanks to cloud computing and artificial intelligence.

How it works

A typical bioinformatics pipeline includes: preprocessing of raw data (e.g., adapter trimming and quality control in sequencing), alignment to a reference genome or de novo assembly, variant calling, gene mapping, and functional analysis. Statistical methods (Bayesian inference, hidden Markov models) play a central role.

Open problems & fun facts

Accurate prediction of the function of uncharacterized proteins from sequence and structure.; Integration of heterogeneous data (genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, clinical records) into unified predictive models.; Analysis of vast amounts of single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics data.; Creation of complete computational models of an entire cell or organism.
