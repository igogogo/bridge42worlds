Tag

fermion

In a crowded bus, everyone needs their own seat—no one will sit on your lap. A fermion is a tiny particle that follows the same rule: two identical fermions can never occupy the same state.

In practice: Electrons are fermions. Their stubbornness prevents atoms from collapsing and creates all the diversity of chemistry. Without this rule, there would be no solid table, nor your palm.
Fermions are divided into leptons (electron, neutrino) and quarks (which make up protons and neutrons). They share a spin-½, which forces them to follow Fermi–Dirac statistics—a mathematical expression of the exclusion principle. When a gas of fermions is cooled, all lower energy levels are filled up to a boundary—the Fermi energy. This Fermi surface explains the electrical conductivity of metals: only electrons near the boundary can move. The pressure of degenerate fermions prevents white dwarfs from collapsing under their own gravity—stars are held up by the exclusion force.

History

In 1925, Austrian physicist Wolfgang Pauli introduced the “exclusion principle” to explain why electrons in atoms occupy different levels. Soon after, Paul Dirac combined quantum mechanics with relativity and discovered that every fermion must have an antiparticle twin—for example, the positron for the electron.

How it works

Imagine a row of chairs in a lecture hall. Each chair is one state with a certain height, position, and spin orientation. The first electron takes any free chair; the next is forced to take another. Filling proceeds from bottom to top, by energy. If you try to shove an extra electron onto an already occupied chair, the atom will strongly resist—this is the solidity of any body.

💡 The human body contains about 7×10²⁷ fermions (electrons and quarks). If the Pauli principle vanished, all these particles would clump into a single point—and you would shrink to the size of a grain of sand.
The entire familiar world is built from fermions. Electron, proton, neutron—these are fermions. Each such particle has an intrinsic property—spin, akin to an internal compass needle. But spin is tricky: its value is always half-integer (½, 3/2…). Because of this half-integer spin, fermions obey a strict rule: two identical fermions are forbidden to be in the same place with the same spin direction. That is why electrons in an atom occupy different shells and do not all fall onto the nucleus—the spots are already taken. This exclusion was discovered by Wolfgang Pauli, and it is responsible for the strength of a brick and the opacity of matter.

The positron, predicted purely mathematically, was found in a cloud chamber by a track twisted the “wrong” way by a magnetic field.
In quantum field theory, fermions are quanta of excitation of fermionic fields. They satisfy anticommutation relations: {ψ(x), ψ†(y)} = δ(x−y). The spin-statistics theorem rigidly links half-integer spin with antisymmetry of the wave function under particle exchange. The Dirac equation (iγ^μ ∂_μ − m)ψ = 0 describes a free fermion and inevitably predicts antiparticles. The Standard Model contains 12 fundamental fermions: six quarks (u, d, c, s, t, b) and six leptons (electron, muon, tau, and their neutrinos). Quarks carry color charge and are confined inside hadrons by the strong interaction.

History

The name “fermion” honors Enrico Fermi, who together with Paul Dirac in 1926 developed the statistics for particles with half-integer spin. A year earlier, Wolfgang Pauli formulated the exclusion principle by analyzing atomic spectra. The Dirac equation (1928) combined quantum mechanics and special relativity for the electron and led to the discovery of the positron by Carl Anderson in 1932. The quark model, proposed by Gell-Mann and Zweig in 1964, organized the zoo of strongly interacting particles.

How it works

The Pauli principle is a consequence of the antisymmetry of the wave function of a fermion system: Ψ(…,x_i,…,x_j,…) = −Ψ(…,x_j,…,x_i,…). When coordinates and spins coincide, the wave function vanishes. In a free electron gas at zero temperature, all levels below the Fermi energy E_F are filled, and those above are empty, according to the Fermi–Dirac distribution f(E) = 1/(e^{(E−μ)/k_B T} + 1). The degeneracy pressure of nonrelativistic fermions p ∝ n^{5/3} supports white dwarfs, while in the relativistic regime the Chandrasekhar limit restricts their mass.

i \gamma^{\mu} \partial_{\mu} \psi - m \psi = 0
