In the morning, the toaster, phone, and Wi-Fi router turn on at the same time, but none of them interfere with each other. This is electromagnetic compatibility — the invisible conductor of the ether.

In practice: Without it, a pacemaker would malfunction from a mobile phone, and aircraft navigation would fail in a thunderstorm.
Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) is a branch of electrical engineering that ensures the coexistence of electronic systems without mutual interference. Any current-carrying wire, antenna, or microchip emits electromagnetic fields. If the level of these fields exceeds the sensitivity threshold of a neighboring device, a malfunction occurs — from crackling in speakers to a computer memory error. EMC solves two problems: reducing emissions from sources and increasing the immunity of receivers. In practice, this involves shielding, filtering, power decoupling, and line balancing. EMC standards are mandatory for all electronics, from children's toys to satellites.

History

The first problems arose with the spread of radio in the early 20th century. Engineers noticed that transmitters interfered with each other. Later, with the advent of computers and mobile phones, the issue became universal. The foundations were laid by James Maxwell, who described electromagnetic waves.

How it works

Every device is both a source and a receiver of waves. To avoid chaos, developers reduce unwanted emissions (shielding wires, installing filters) and make devices immune to external interference (protected circuits). This is similar to soundproofing in an apartment: to sleep undisturbed by a neighbor's hammer drill, you need thick walls and earplugs.

Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) is the ability of all electronic devices to work side by side without creating mutual interference. Like in an orchestra, where violins shouldn't drown out the trumpets, and trumpets shouldn't overpower the flute. If one violin plays too loudly (interference), the others will falter. EMC sets the rules: each instrument (device) emits exactly as much as needed, and at the same time knows how to listen only to its own part, ignoring others' noise. Tests are conducted in anechoic chambers — rooms without reflections. Therefore, when you turn on the toaster, the phone doesn't crackle — the orchestra plays in harmony.

Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) is a discipline that studies the unintentional generation, propagation, and reception of electromagnetic energy, with the aim of ensuring proper equipment operation in a common electromagnetic environment. It includes analysis of interference sources (natural, such as lightning, and man-made — switching power supplies, digital buses, radio transmitters), coupling mechanisms (galvanic, capacitive, inductive, radiation), and protection methods. Key concepts: radiated and conducted emission, susceptibility, immunity. EMC standards specify emission limits and minimum immunity levels for various device classes (CISPR, IEC, FCC).

History

The phenomenon of interference has been known since the experiments of Hertz and Popov. A systematic approach emerged in the 1930s with the growth of radio broadcasting and military communications. In 1933, the International Special Committee on Radio Interference (CISPR) began developing standards. James Clerk Maxwell made a significant contribution to the understanding of wave propagation, whose equations describe all electromagnetic interactions. In the second half of the 20th century, with the advent of microelectronics and digital systems, the problem intensified, leading to the creation of mandatory EMC standards in the 1990s.

How it works

Any alternating current creates an alternating electromagnetic field, which induces stray signals in neighboring circuits. According to Maxwell's equations, a changing electric field generates a magnetic field, and vice versa, forming a wave. To reduce emissions, shielding (reflection and absorption of waves), filtering (suppression of high-frequency components), and proper PCB layout (minimizing loop areas) are used. To increase immunity — differential signaling, galvanic isolation, and static suppression circuits.
