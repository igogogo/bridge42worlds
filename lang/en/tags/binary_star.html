binary star

Two suns chase each other against a purple sky. A gravitational waltz holds them on eternal orbits around an invisible center.

In practice: Binary stars serve as cosmic scales, allowing measurement of stellar masses and discovery of invisible objects like black holes. Their mergers generate gravitational waves and supernovae—forges of heavy elements.
A binary star is a system of two stars orbiting a common center of mass. Periods can range from hours to millions of years. Such pairs are often born together from a single gas cloud, becoming true twins. Their dance allows direct determination of stellar masses—a fundamental characteristic governing stellar evolution. Binaries come in visual (seen separately), spectroscopic (detected by Doppler shift—the change in light frequency due to motion), eclipsing (when one star periodically blocks the other), and astrometric (an invisible companion revealed by wobbles of the visible star).

History

The first binary star was noticed in 1650 when Giovanni Riccioli saw Mizar split into two. A century later, William Herschel proved that pairs move in orbits. Today we know: there are more binary systems than single ones.

How it works

Two luminaries move, obeying the law of universal gravitation (the more massive and closer the bodies, the stronger the attraction). The faster the rotation, the stronger the bond. By measuring speed and distance, astronomers weigh stars—just as one can determine the mass of a weight by the tension of the rope used to spin it.

💡 The North Star, famously a guiding beacon, is itself a triple system: the bright supergiant is accompanied by two small companions.
A binary star is a pair of suns circling a common center of gravity (balance point). They are bound by gravity (the force of attraction between any masses). Like figure skaters holding hands, they spin without falling into each other. For instance, Sirius has a companion—a white dwarf, as heavy as the Sun but as compact as Earth. Most stars in the sky are not solitary; they live in such duets.

A binary star is a system of two gravitationally bound stellar objects orbiting a barycenter. Classification includes visual, spectroscopic, eclipsing, and astrometric pairs. Component masses are determined via Kepler's third law in Newtonian form: the sum of masses is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis and inversely proportional to the square of the orbital period. In close systems, mass transfer is possible through the Roche lobe—an equipotential surface bounding each star's gravitational domain. Semi-detached and contact binaries exhibit accretion disks, common envelopes, and X-ray emission. Compact pairs (white dwarfs, neutron stars, black holes) upon merger become sources of gravitational waves and kilonovae. From swift pairs whose dance lasts hours to majestic duets stretched over millennia, each binary star remains a cosmic choreography where gravity writes notes audible across billions of years.

History

The duality of Mizar was first recorded by Riccioli in 1650. In the late 18th century, William Herschel began systematic searches for binaries and proved their orbital motion. The 19th century brought computations of the first orbits. In the 20th century, spectroscopy unveiled invisible duets, and space telescopes allowed us to discern pairs in other galaxies. Today, the Gaia mission is building a three-dimensional map of billions of stars, revealing the architecture of binary systems in the Milky Way.

How it works

Motion follows Newtonian mechanics (or general relativity for compact objects). Period and distance yield total mass. If the orbital plane lies along the line of sight, eclipses are observed with characteristic light curves. When the Roche lobe is filled, matter streams onto the accretor, forming a disk and a hot spot. This process explains cataclysmic variables, X-ray pulsars, and Type Ia supernovae. Mergers of neutron stars and black holes are detected by LIGO and Virgo detectors via gravitational-wave bursts. Kepler's third law plus spectroscopy unveil the masses of exoplanets in binary systems.
