photometric distance

A lantern and a candle at a distance can shine equally. Photometric distance is a way to determine how far a source is by comparing its apparent brightness with its true power.

In practice: The method is used to measure distances to Type Ia supernovae, which led to the discovery of the accelerating expansion of the universe. Without it, modern cosmology would lose its ruler.
Photometric distance D_L relates the observed radiation flux F to the true luminosity L via the formula F = L / (4π D_L²). It includes a correction for the expansion of the universe: photons lose energy due to redshift (wavelength increases), and the time between the arrival of successive photons also stretches. As a result, D_L = (1+z) D_M, where z is the redshift, and D_M is the comoving transverse distance. For nearby objects, D_L ≈ D_M, but for distant galaxies (z ~ 1 and higher) the difference becomes significant. D_L is precisely what is measured using standard candles—objects of known luminosity, like Type Ia supernovae.

History

The concept began to take shape in the 1920s when Edwin Hubble investigated the relationship between redshift and distance. The modern meaning crystallized with the advent of standard candles—sources with known luminosity. In 1998, two teams of astronomers, using Type Ia supernova explosions, discovered through photometric distance that the universe is expanding at an accelerating rate.

How it works

Imagine an inflating balloon with light bulbs stuck on it. Bulbs of the same power dim not only because they move away but also because space stretches, 'smearing' the light. Photometric distance accounts for both effects: dimming due to geometric dispersion and additional dimming due to cosmological redshift.

💡 Looking at a galaxy with redshift z=1, we receive light dimmed by a factor of four just due to the expansion of space—the photometric distance turns out to be twice the comoving distance.
Photometric distance is not a distance you can measure with a tape measure. It says: 'If the source looked this dim in ordinary empty space, it would have to be this far away.' It is calculated by how much the light from a distant star or galaxy has weakened. But space itself expands and can further dim the light—so the photometric distance is larger than the geometric distance.

Photometric distance D_L in cosmology is defined through the relation between the absolute luminosity L of a source and the measured bolometric flux F: D_L ≡ √(L / (4πF)). In an expanding universe, this distance is larger than coordinate or angular distance because the energy of each photon decreases by a factor of (1+z), and the interval between photon arrivals is further lengthened by another factor of (1+z), giving a factor of (1+z)² for the flux and consequently D_L = (1+z) D_M. D_M is the transverse comoving distance, which depends on the model: in a flat ΛCDM cosmology, D_M = (c/H₀) ∫_0^z dz' / E(z'), where E(z') = √(Ω_m(1+z')³ + Ω_Λ). Measuring D_L with standard candles such as Type Ia supernovae is the foundation of the cosmological distance scale and led to the discovery of dark energy.

History

The concept originates from the work of Edwin Hubble, who in 1929 established the proportionality of redshift to distance. The formula for photometric distance in relativistic cosmology was derived within the framework of the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric. The Supernova Cosmology Project (Saul Perlmutter) and the High-Z Supernova Search Team (Brian Schmidt, Adam Riess) in 1998 used Type Ia supernovae as standard candles and discovered a deviation of D_L from the linear law, which indicated accelerated expansion. This became direct observational evidence for the existence of dark energy. Adam Riess and Saul Perlmutter are present in the list of scientists.

How it works

The relationship between apparent magnitude m and absolute magnitude M is given by the distance modulus: m − M = 5 log₁₀(D_L / 10 pc). For an object with known absolute luminosity (standard candle), measuring the peak apparent magnitude yields D_L. Within general relativity, D_L is expressed through solutions to the Friedmann equations, which follow from Einstein's equations for a homogeneous isotropic universe. The value of D_L depends on the densities of matter Ω_m, dark energy Ω_Λ, curvature Ω_k, and the Hubble constant H₀. Therefore, measuring D_L for a set of objects at different z allows placing constraints on cosmological parameters, in particular on the equation of state of dark energy w.

D_L = (1+z)D_M
D_L — photometric distance; z — redshift of the observed radiation; D_M — comoving transverse distance (distance in coordinates that move with the expansion of the universe).
F = \frac{L}{4\pi D_L^2}
F — observed energy flux (energy per unit area per unit time); L — absolute luminosity of the source (total emitted power); D_L — photometric distance; 4πD_L² — area of the sphere over which the energy is distributed in static space.
