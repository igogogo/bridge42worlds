cell

7

A cell is a living brick from which all organisms are built; inside it, like in a miniature city, work is in full swing: some parts extract energy, others build proteins, and still others remove waste.

In practice: Understanding the cell is the foundation of medicine: from developing antibiotics to growing organs from stem cells.
The cell is the elementary structural and functional unit of living things, capable of self-reproduction. It is separated from the environment by a plasma membrane, which regulates the transport of substances. In eukaryotes (organisms whose cells have a defined nucleus), the interior contains a nucleus containing DNA packaged into chromosomes (tightly coiled DNA threads). The cytoplasm is filled with organelles: mitochondria carry out cellular respiration, ribosomes synthesize proteins based on RNA templates (messenger molecules that copy DNA fragments), the endoplasmic reticulum transports substances, the Golgi apparatus sorts and packages proteins, lysosomes provide intracellular digestion.

History

The first person to see cells was Robert Hooke in 1665, while examining a cork through a homemade microscope. He called them 'cells' because they resembled monks' cells.

How it works

The cell receives nutrients through the membrane, like through a checkpoint. Then mitochondria convert them into energy, which other parts use to work. DNA in the nucleus gives instructions to ribosomes on how to assemble proteins. When the cell grows, it divides in two — this is how an organism grows and renews itself.

💡 There are about 37 trillion cells in the adult human body, but approximately the same number of bacterial cells live inside us, and they are important for health.
A cell is the tiniest part of a living organism that can live, feed, and reproduce on its own. It can be compared to a tiny factory surrounded by a fence-membrane (envelope). Inside, there is the nucleus — the 'director's office,' where the blueprints of the entire organism are stored (DNA — molecules with hereditary information). Around the nucleus floats cytoplasm (a jelly-like fluid), and within it work the organelles (little 'machines'): mitochondria produce energy, ribosomes assemble proteins (the main building materials), and lysosomes digest waste.

The cell is the fundamental unit of life, bounded by a plasma membrane and containing the genetic apparatus that ensures its reproduction. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea) — without a membrane-bound nucleus — and eukaryotic cells — with a nucleus containing linear DNA packaged by histones into chromatin — are distinguished. The eukaryotic cell contains numerous membrane-bound and non-membrane-bound organelles: mitochondria with their own circular DNA, converting energy from nutrients into ATP; rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes (free and bound), the Golgi complex, lysosomes, peroxisomes, and the cytoskeleton (microtubules, microfilaments, intermediate filaments), which provides shape and movement. Plant cells additionally contain chloroplasts for photosynthesis, a large central vacuole, and a cell wall made of cellulose. The cell life cycle includes phases: G1, S (DNA replication), G2, and mitosis (nuclear division) or meiosis (formation of gametes).

History

The term 'cell' was introduced by Robert Hooke in 1665, observing cork slices. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek soon described living cells, including bacteria and spermatozoa. In the 19th century, Theodor Schwann and Matthias Schleiden formulated the cell theory: all organisms consist of cells. Rudolf Virchow supplemented it with the statement 'every cell arises from a cell.' Electron microscopy in the mid-20th century revealed the ultrastructure of the cell.

How it works

Cell functioning is based on the flow of genetic information: DNA → RNA → protein. Transcription (copying a section of DNA into mRNA) occurs in the nucleus, then mRNA exits into the cytoplasm, where ribosomes carry out translation, assembling the polypeptide chain according to the genetic code. Energy metabolism is provided by mitochondria through the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation using a proton gradient. Transport of substances across the membrane is regulated by passive diffusion, facilitated transport, and active transport, including Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Cell division is controlled by cyclin-dependent kinases, and apoptosis (programmed cell death) eliminates damaged cells.
