information theory

13

Each letter in this text is an act of choosing from 33 options. Information theory does not ask what the message is about; it counts how many surprises it contains: the more options there could have been, the more bits in each symbol.

In practice: Thanks to it, the internet, JPEG photo compression, and the recovery of scratched music on CDs work. Even DNA sequencing uses an information measure to assemble the genome.
The central concept is Shannon's information entropy: H = −∑ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ. It measures the average number of bits needed to encode one source symbol. If all symbols are equally likely, H is maximized. The redundancy of language allows lossless data compression (Shannon proved the compression limit in the source coding theorem). Another cornerstone is channel capacity: C = B log₂(1+S/N). If you encode a message at a rate lower than C, you can transmit it practically without errors, despite noise. These ideas work in all communication systems: from satellites to USB flash drives.

History

Claude Shannon laid the foundations in 1948 while working at Bell Labs. He was looking for a way to transmit messages over a noisy telephone cable without interference. Shannon introduced the concept of information entropy—a measure of how unpredictable the message source is. His paper 'A Mathematical Theory of Communication' paved the way for the digital age.

How it works

Each symbol in a message carries as much information as the uncertainty it removes. The Russian alphabet has 33 letters, so one letter gives almost 5 bits (since 2⁵=32). But letters occur with different frequencies: if the letter 'O' is ten times more frequent than 'Щ', its appearance is less surprising and carries fewer bits. A message can be compressed by assigning short codes to frequent letters — that's how Morse code and ZIP archives work.

💡 The phrase 'Hello, World!' in random English text would contain about 80 bits, but due to language redundancy it can be compressed to 40-50 bits. Shannon calculated that English text carries on average only 1.3 bits per letter.
Information theory is a mathematical accounting of uncertainty. Imagine a hat with notes. Pulling out a note, you get more information the less likely it was to draw it. The unit of information is the bit. One bit is a choice between two equally likely outcomes. All digital communication from the telegraph to Wi-Fi is built on this calculation: a message is more valuable the more uncertainty it removes.

Information theory is a branch of applied mathematics, radio engineering, and computer science studying the quantitative laws of generation, transmission, storage, and processing of information. The foundational work is 'A Mathematical Theory of Communication' by C. Shannon (1948). Basic concepts: 1) Information measure: the information of an event with probability p is I = −log₂ p bits. 2) Entropy of a discrete source: H(X) = −∑ p(x) log₂ p(x) — average uncertainty. 3) Mutual information: I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y) — the amount of information Y contains about X. 4) Channel capacity: the maximum mutual information between input and output of the channel when optimizing the input distribution. Key theorems: source coding theorem (compression limit), noisy-channel coding theorem (achievable transmission rate). Development led to the creation of error-correcting codes (Hamming, Reed-Solomon, turbo codes, LDPC), compression algorithms (Huffman, arithmetic coding, LZW), and cryptography.

History

Claude Shannon (1916–2001) formulated the mathematical theory in his secret wartime reports of the early 1940s and summarized it in the famous 1948 paper in the Bell System Technical Journal. He introduced the term 'bit' (suggested by J. Tukey) and linked information entropy with Boltzmann's physical entropy. Predecessors included H. Nyquist (studied telegraph transmission speed, 1924) and R. Hartley (proposed a logarithmic measure, 1928). Later, A.N. Kolmogorov developed an algorithmic approach to information (Kolmogorov complexity). In quantum information theory, von Neumann entropy generalizes Shannon entropy for mixed quantum states.

How it works

Information is measured in bits as the logarithm of the number of possible states: I = log₂ N or −log₂ p. Entropy H = −∑ pᵢ log pᵢ reaches its maximum with a uniform distribution. When transmitted over a channel with capacity C (Shannon-Hartley formula: C = B log₂(1+S/N) for Gaussian noise), Shannon's theorem guarantees: if the coding rate R < C, then an arbitrarily low error probability can be achieved using sufficiently long block codes. In practice, the limit is approached using turbo and LDPC codes, which employ iterative decoding and prior probabilities. For lossless compression, the optimal code achieves an average length equal to entropy and is constructed using Huffman's algorithm or arithmetic coding. Information theory is also applied in machine learning (minimum description length principle, Bayesian methods) and bioinformatics.

H = -\sum_{i} p_i \log_2 p_i
H — entropy (bit/symbol), p_i — probability of the i-th outcome, Σ — sum over all possible outcomes
C = B \log_2 \left( 1 + \frac{S}{N} \right)
C — capacity (bit/s), B — channel bandwidth (Hz), S/N — signal-to-noise power ratio (dimensionless)

Open problems & fun facts

Computability of Kolmogorov complexity (non-computability in the general case); Integration of semantics: how to measure the meaningfulness of information, not just its volume; Quantum information theory: overcoming classical channel capacity limitations through entanglement; Efficient coding methods for short packets in ultra-reliable systems (5G/6G)
