microorganism

3

Tiny living creatures, invisible to the eye, that are everywhere: in the air, water, on the skin. They were the first to colonize the Earth and control the cycle of substances.

In practice: Bacteria and fungi produce antibiotics, bread, yogurt, and also decompose garbage; without them, the planet would drown in waste.
Microorganisms are the dominant form of life on Earth in terms of biomass and diversity. They include prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) without a nucleus and eukaryotic microbes (fungi, algae, protozoa). Metagenomics has shown that 99% of microbial species cannot be cultured in the laboratory. The human microbiome contains about 100 trillion bacteria — 10 times more than our own cells, and encodes 300 times as many genes as our genome.

History

The discoverer of microbes is considered to be Antonie van Leeuwenhoek — in the 17th century, he saw 'animalcules' in a drop of water through a homemade microscope. Later, Louis Pasteur proved that they cause fermentation and disease, and Robert Koch isolated the causative agents of tuberculosis and cholera.

How it works

Microbes live in communities — biofilms on teeth or stones. They feed by absorbing dissolved substances through their membrane, and reproduce by dividing in half. Some can form spores — tough capsules that withstand heat, cold, and even space.

💡 The microbe Deinococcus radiodurans withstands a radiation dose 3000 times higher than the lethal dose for humans; it is used to clean up radioactive waste.
Microorganisms are the smallest organisms that can be seen only with a microscope. Among them are bacteria, unicellular fungi (yeasts), archaea (ancient single-celled organisms similar to bacteria but with a different structure) and protozoa like amoebas. Viruses are also often classified as microbes, but they lack cells and are not alive in the usual sense — they only reproduce inside other cells. Most microbes are harmless, and many are beneficial, participating in digestion or producing oxygen.

Microorganisms are microscopic organisms representing all three domains of life: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. They exhibit colossal metabolic diversity: photoautotrophs (cyanobacteria), chemolithotrophs (oxidizing sulfur or iron), and organotrophs. Bacteria reproduce by binary fission but exchange genetic material through conjugation, transformation, and transduction, which accelerates adaptation. Quorum sensing allows populations to coordinate gene expression, for example, for biofilm formation or bioluminescence. Archaea often inhabit extreme conditions: thermophiles at 120°C, acidophiles at pH 0, halophiles in salt lakes. Microorganisms close the biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus, and their total biomass is estimated at hundreds of gigatons.

History

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1676) first described 'animalcules' using lenses of his own making. In the 19th century, Christian Ehrenberg introduced the term 'bacteria', and Pasteur and Koch laid the foundations of medical microbiology. Sergei Winogradsky discovered chemosynthesis, and Martinus Beijerinck isolated nitrogen-fixing bacteria and discovered viruses. In the 20th century, electron microscopy and molecular biology (16S rRNA sequencing) radically changed the systematics, revealing the scale of unexplored diversity.

How it works

The structural organization of prokaryotes lacks membrane-bound organelles; the genetic apparatus is represented by circular DNA in the nucleoid. Population growth in a closed environment is described by a curve: lag phase, exponential phase, stationary phase, and death phase. Growth kinetics follow the Monod equation: μ = μ_max * S/(K_s + S), where μ is the specific growth rate, μ_max is the maximum rate, S is the substrate concentration, and K_s is the half-saturation constant. Many species form dormant forms — endospores (Bacillus, Clostridium), resistant to radiation, desiccation, and boiling temperature. Horizontal gene transfer and high mutation rate make microorganisms an ideal model for studying evolution in real time.
