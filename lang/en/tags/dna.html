DNA

DNA is a molecular instruction for living organisms, written with four chemical letters on a twisted spiral staircase.

In practice: By reading DNA, we treat hereditary diseases, create GMOs, and solve crimes.
DNA is a long chain molecule (polymer) consisting of two strands of nucleotides (building blocks) twisted into a double helix. Each nucleotide contains one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), cytosine (C). The strands are held by weak chemical bonds (hydrogen) between pairs A–T and G–C, which complement each other like a key to a lock (they are complementary). The sequence of bases encodes hereditary information. In organisms with a cell nucleus (eukaryotes), DNA is packaged into chromosomes.

History

The double helix structure was discovered in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick, using X-ray crystallography data (a method that reveals the arrangement of atoms by shining X-rays on crystals) obtained by Rosalind Franklin.

How it works

During cell division, the double helix unwinds, and each strand builds a partner from the floating base-letters around. This produces two identical copies — cells inherit the same instruction.

💡 The total length of DNA in all human cells is about 10 billion kilometers — enough to stretch a thread to Pluto and back several times.
DNA resembles a very long rope ladder twisted into a spiral. Its rungs are pairs of chemical compounds (nitrogenous bases) that act like letters: A, T, G, C. The sequence of these letters provides instructions for building an organism, like a page in a book. Each cell in the body contains the same book, but reads only the necessary pages.

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is a biopolymer composed of nucleotides. Each nucleotide includes deoxyribose (a sugar), a phosphate group, and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), or cytosine (C). Two antiparallel polynucleotide chains are twisted into a right-handed helix with a diameter of about 2 nm and a helical pitch of 3.4 nm (10 base pairs per turn). The chains are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases: A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds), G with C (three bonds). This structure was discovered in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick using X-ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. DNA stores genetic information as a code where nucleotide triplets (codons) correspond to amino acids. DNA replication occurs in a semi-conservative manner with the involvement of multiple enzymes (DNA polymerase, helicase, etc.).

History

The idea that hereditary information is transmitted through molecules arose in the early 20th century. In 1944, Oswald Avery demonstrated that DNA is the carrier of genetic information in bacteria. In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick, based on the X-ray diffraction images of Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins, proposed the double helix model. An important contribution to the development of X-ray crystallography methods was made by William Lawrence Bragg, whose work enabled the deciphering of biomolecular structures. Erwin Schrödinger, in his book 'What is Life?' (1944), predicted the existence of an 'aperiodic crystal' as the carrier of heredity.

How it works

DNA works through the central dogma of molecular biology: information is transferred from DNA to RNA (transcription) and then to protein (translation). Transcription is performed by RNA polymerase, which synthesizes messenger RNA (mRNA) based on a DNA template. The resulting mRNA exits the nucleus and binds to a ribosome, where protein synthesis occurs. The nucleotide sequence is read in triplets (codons), each codon specifying one amino acid. The genetic code is universal for all living organisms. Regulation of gene expression is carried out through promoters, enhancers, and epigenetic modifications (e.g., DNA methylation).

Open problems & fun facts

The question of the origin of DNA in the primordial soup remains unsolved; The problem of DNA folding within the volume of the nucleus; The mechanisms of aging related to DNA damage are not fully understood
