PI-RADS classification

2

PI-RADS is a scale from 1 to 5, like stars in a hotel review: 1 is excellent, nothing found; 5 is very bad, almost certainly cancer. It helps doctors describe how suspicious the detected areas on prostate MRI are in a unified language.

In practice: The PI-RADS classification standardizes MRI reports, reducing interpretation variability and guiding decisions on the need for biopsy: for PI-RADS 1-2, biopsy is usually not needed; for 3, at discretion; for 4-5, mandatory.
The PI-RADS (Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System) is a standardized method for assessing the probability of clinically significant prostate cancer based on multiparametric MRI. Each detected lesion in the transition or peripheral zone receives a score on a 5-point scale. The assessment is based on analysis of T2-weighted imaging (dominant sequence for the transition zone), diffusion-weighted imaging (dominant for the peripheral zone), and dynamic contrast enhancement (which plays a supporting role). The rules for assigning the final score depend on the zone and the presence of certain features: border clarity, size, signal intensity. PI-RADS 3 indicates an uncertain result, PI-RADS 4 and 5 indicate the need for biopsy.

History

Before 2012, each doctor described results in their own way, leading to confusion. Then a group of experts from the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) created the first version of PI-RADS. In 2015 it was improved (version 2), and in 2019 the current version 2.1 came out with even clearer guidelines.

How it works

The doctor examines three main images: T2, an ADC map, and a contrast-enhanced image. For each zone they see, they assign a separate score for each type of image. Then, using special rules (algorithm), these scores are combined into a final score from 1 to 5. For example, if T2 shows a clear dark lesion, on ADC it is also dark, and contrast actively enters it — that is a clear 5.

💡 PI-RADS version 2.1 clarified that lesions smaller than 1 cm in the transition zone cannot receive a score higher than 3, which reduced overdiagnosis.
After a doctor looks at a prostate MRI, they need to describe in words how much each finding resembles cancer. PI-RADS is like an agreement among doctors worldwide on which words to use. Lesions are given scores: 1 — definitely normal, 2 — most likely normal, 3 — unclear (questionable), 4 — suspicious, 5 — very suspicious. When assigning a score, they look at size, shape, brightness in different MRI modes — it's like evaluating a watermelon: if it's small, with a smooth rind and a crisp sound when tapped, it's good; if it's large, with a soft spot and a dull sound, it's bad.

The PI-RADS v2.1 classification is a detailed semiotic system stratifying the risk of clinically significant cancer (defined as ISUP ≥ 2, i.e., Gleason ≥ 3+4 or volume ≥ 0.5 cm³) based on mpMRI. The assessment is zone-specific: for the peripheral zone, the leading sequence is DWI/ADC, with the DWI score being primary; for the transition zone, T2. Dynamic contrast enhancement is used to clarify uncertain cases (category 2 or 3 on DWI): a positive DCE upgrades the final category to 4. The system requires high-quality scanning (at least 1.5 T) with adherence to minimal technical specifications. PI-RADS 1 — normal, 2 — clearly benign changes, 3 — intermediate risk, 4 — high probability of cancer, 5 — very high probability. Categories are linked to an action plan: PI-RADS ≥3 may be referred for MRI-guided targeted biopsy. Scientific data show that the negative predictive value of PI-RADS 1-2 for excluding clinically significant cancer is 85–95%.

History

The need for unification arose in the early 2000s with the increasing use of mpMRI. The first PI-RADS version was released by ESUR in 2012 but was imperfect due to convoluted algorithms. In 2015, together with the American College of Radiology (ACR) and the AdMeTech Foundation, version 2 was presented, simplifying the rules and separating the approach for the transition and peripheral zones. In 2019, an interdisciplinary expert group released PI-RADS v2.1, refining criteria for the transition zone (for example, removing the 2+1 score, relaxing requirements for nodule shape), which increased specificity. PI-RADS is recognized as an international standard, and its validation continues in different populations.

How it works

After obtaining mpMRI images, the radiologist evaluates each finding sequentially: 1. Determine the zone (peripheral or transition) using T2 anatomy. 2. Peripheral zone: primary DWI assessment. DWI score: 1 — no changes, 2 — linear/wedge-shaped vague hypointensity, 3 — focal moderate hypointensity (indistinct, small), 4 — distinctly hypointense lesion <1.5 cm, 5 — distinctly hypointense lesion ≥1.5 cm or with extracapsular extension. For DWI score 3 — assess DCE: if there is focal enhancement occurring earlier than or simultaneously with the peak of normal tissue, then the final PI-RADS category is 4; if negative — 3. For DWI score 4 or 5, the category corresponds to the DWI score regardless of DCE. 3. Transition zone: primary T2 assessment. T2 score: 1 — homogeneous transition zone, 2 — nodules with a clear capsule or chondroid nodules, 3 — moderate inhomogeneity with indistinct margins, 4 — lenticular, irregular, indistinct lesion, 5 — same lesion with invasive features or ≥1.5 cm. For T2 score 2 or 3 — consider DWI: if DWI ≥4 (distinct restricted diffusion <1.5 cm), then the category is upgraded to 3 or 4 respectively. DCE does not affect the transition zone. Size is measured by the largest diameter on an axial image. If there are multiple findings, an index lesion (the most suspicious) may be given. The report includes a map indicating the PI-RADS score of each finding.

Open problems & fun facts

Subjectivity in assessing border 'fuzziness' and moderate hypointensity, leading to variability between experts; Insufficient specificity of PI-RADS 3 — a high proportion of false-positive biopsies; Evolution of diagnostic criteria as data accumulates (revision of size thresholds, the role of DCE for the transition zone remains controversial); Applicability of the system on low-field devices and without an endorectal coil requires additional validation; Need for adaptation to new biomarkers (restosis, radiomics) and artificial intelligence methods
