wavelet transform

1

A zoom lens on a camera captures both the entire landscape and the pattern on a butterfly's wing. The wavelet transform similarly changes scale: it decomposes a signal into scaled copies of itself, allowing one to see both the overall shape and the tiniest jumps.

In practice: Digital fingerprints and barcodes are compressed using wavelets. Instruments that search for defects in aircraft parts listen for crack echoes with wavelets.
The secret of wavelets lies in compromise. Fourier analysis provides perfect frequency resolution but loses time information. The windowed Fourier transform fixes this but fixes the window size. Wavelets adjust the window width adaptively: for high frequencies, short windows are used; for low frequencies, long ones. This is like musical notation, where long notes represent bass sounds and short ones represent rapid trills. In practice, wavelets excel at image compression (JPEG 2000 format) and cleaning signals from noise without blurring sharp transitions—for example, in medical tomograms.

History

Wavelets emerged in the 1980s when geophysicist Jean Morlet sought a way to extract weak reflected waves in seismic data. Together with physicist Alex Grossmann, he developed the continuous wavelet transform. Later, mathematician Ingrid Daubechies constructed elegant orthogonal wavelets with compact support, paving the way for fast algorithms.

How it works

Take a short wave template (the wavelet). Compare it to a segment of the signal at the beginning—you get a similarity number. Shift the template slightly forward—another number. After sliding through the whole signal, you obtain a series of numbers for that template size. Then stretch the template (increase the scale) and repeat. A tiny template catches sharp peaks; a large one captures smooth curves. The collection of such series for different stretches is the wavelet spectrum.

💡 Human vision is somewhat similar to wavelet analysis: we can discern both the overall shape of a chair in dim light and a fine inscription on its leg—the brain processes images at multiple scales simultaneously.
Ordinary Fourier analysis shows which frequencies were present in a signal, but not when. It's as if someone told you that a symphony contains the note C, but not at what moment. The wavelet transform solves this problem: instead of endless sinusoids, it uses small waves—wavelets—that decay. The wavelet is applied to the signal and shifted along like a template. Changing the size of the template (scale) lets you see both broad features and fine details. This yields a map: time on the horizontal axis, scale on the vertical, and color indicating the strength of the match.

The wavelet transform is a mathematical tool for time-frequency analysis of signals with adaptive resolution. The continuous wavelet transform (CWT) is defined as the convolution of a signal with scaled and shifted versions of a mother wavelet. The discrete wavelet transform (DWT) uses multiresolution analysis to obtain a hierarchical set of coefficients—approximation and detail. Wavelets provide optimal localization in the time-frequency domain in the sense of the uncertainty principle, but with variable window width, unlike the fixed-window Fourier transform. They form a basis in function spaces and are used for denoising, compression, feature extraction, and solving differential equations.

History

The history of wavelets began with Haar's work (1909)—the simplest step-like system. From the 1930s to the 1980s, separate ideas emerged: the Littlewood–Paley function, quadrature mirror filters by Croisier, Esteban, and Galand. A coherent theory arose in the early 1980s from the work of geophysicist Jean Morlet and mathematician Alex Grossmann, who built the continuous wavelet transform for seismic signal analysis. Then Ingrid Daubechies (1988) created smooth orthogonal wavelets with compact support, enabling a fast transform. Later, Mallat and Meyer developed multiresolution analysis, linking wavelets to signal filtering.

How it works

The scaling function φ(t) and mother wavelet ψ(t) generate a basis such that {ψ_{j,k}(t) = 2^{-j/2} ψ(2^{-j}t - k)}. Any signal can be decomposed: f(t) = Σ_k a_{j0,k} φ_{j0,k}(t) + Σ_{j≥j0} Σ_k d_{j,k} ψ_{j,k}(t). The coefficients d are obtained by the fast Mallat algorithm using cascaded filtering. At each level, the signal passes through a pair of filters—low-pass (averaging) and high-pass (detailing)—followed by decimation. This is dual to a bank of quadrature mirror filters. The orthogonality condition of the wavelet basis requires the filters to satisfy certain relations. The analysis is flexible: the wavelet shape can be tailored to the data type.
