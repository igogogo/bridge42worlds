Post-transcriptional regulation

2

Quality control at the cell's factory: RNA copies of genes are checked, cut, tagged, or destroyed after synthesis to precisely tune protein production.

In practice: Explains how cells adapt to stress without changing DNA; underlies RNA vaccines and small interfering RNA therapy.
Post-transcriptional regulation encompasses all changes and the fate of RNA molecules after transcription (RNA synthesis on a DNA template). These include: splicing (removing non-coding introns and joining exons), alternative splicing (creating different protein variants from one gene), capping, polyadenylation, base editing, transport from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, localization, control of stability and translation rate (protein synthesis). This is critical for dynamic cellular adaptation to environmental conditions and underlies many diseases.

History

For a long time, it was believed that the main control was turning genes on and off at the DNA level. In the 1990s, it was discovered that regulation at the RNA level is no less important. A key discovery—microRNA (small regulatory molecules) in 1993 in the worm C. elegans—showed that cells actively manage RNA after transcription.

How it works

It’s like a postal service: the gene is a letter, RNA is a copy of the letter that is carried to the ribosome for protein synthesis. Regulation can delay the courier, change the text (RNA editing), tear up the letter, or redirect it to another address—this way the cell controls message delivery.

💡 The same gene can yield over 38,000 protein variants through alternative splicing—like assembling different sentences from the same set of words.
Genes are instructions in DNA. To make a protein, the cell first creates an RNA copy (ribonucleic acid) of the gene. Post-transcriptional regulation controls the fate of this RNA after copying: the cell can cut out extra pieces (splicing), attach a protective cap (cap) and tail (poly-A), send it for storage, or quickly destroy it. Thus, the cell decides how much and when a protein will be synthesized.

Post-transcriptional regulation is a set of molecular mechanisms that control the processing, transport, stability, and translation of RNA after its synthesis by RNA polymerase. Key processes: 1) capping of the 5' end with 7-methylguanosine for protection and translation initiation; 2) splicing—removal of introns by the spliceosome or autocatalytically (ribozymes); 3) editing (cytidine to uridine or adenosine to inosine substitution) altering the sequence; 4) polyadenylation of the 3' end, affecting stability and export; 5) transport by mRNP complexes through nuclear pores; 6) regulation of translation involving microRNAs, siRNAs, RNA-binding proteins, and localization in specific compartments (e.g., in neurons). Each step is finely tuned by signaling pathways, ensuring plasticity of gene expression without altering the genome.

History

Until the 1970s, regulation of gene expression was largely associated with transcription (the Jacob and Monod operon model). In 1977, Phillip Sharp and Richard Roberts independently discovered introns and splicing in adenoviruses, and in the 1980s, Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman proved the catalytic activity of RNA (ribozymes). In 1993, Victor Ambros and colleagues described the first microRNA, lin-4 in C. elegans, demonstrating the regulatory function of small RNAs. Later, mechanisms of RNA interference (siRNA) and editing (APOBEC enzymes) were discovered. These works shifted the paradigm from linear information transfer DNA→RNA→protein to a complex network of post-transcriptional control, recognized as key in development, immunity, and pathology.

How it works

Post-transcriptional mechanisms modulate the fate of the transcript through the formation of ribonucleoprotein complexes. The cap-binding complex attracts elongation factors, the spliceosome recognizes splicing sites by consensus sequences, and the poly(A)-binding protein stabilizes mRNA. MicroRNAs within RISC partially pair with the 3' untranslated region of mRNA and repress translation or cause deadenylation and degradation. Localization is achieved by cytoskeleton-dependent transport and anchoring. Disruptions of these processes underlie many diseases: from spinal muscular atrophy (splicing mutation in SMN1) to cancer (global splicing imbalance).
