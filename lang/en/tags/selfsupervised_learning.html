Tag

self-supervised learning

1

A child learns to speak by listening to adults for years, without dictionaries or rules. Self-supervised learning works the same way — it finds patterns in piles of unlabeled examples.

In practice: Speech recognition systems, machine translation, all modern large language models (like GPT), medical diagnostics from images without doctors' annotations.
Self-supervised learning is a paradigm in which the model generates training signals by extracting them from the very structure of unlabeled data. Unlike unsupervised learning, which often focuses on clustering or dimensionality reduction, here surrogate (pretext) tasks are built that do not require human annotations. For example, in natural language processing, some words in an input sentence are masked, and the network learns to recover them from the context. In computer vision, contrastive methods are popular: two randomly distorted versions of the same image are brought closer in the representation space, while being pushed away from other images. This forms a visual concept reminiscent of how infants distinguish objects from the background.

History

The methods emerged in the 2010s as a way to combat the high cost of data labeling. A turning point came in 2018–2020, when models like BERT and SimCLR showed that it is possible to learn from raw text and images, matching and sometimes exceeding supervised learning.

How it works

The algorithm itself creates a “teacher” from the data. For example, it takes the sentence “The cat sits on the...”, removes a word, and learns to predict “mat.” Or it takes a photo, randomly rotates it, and learns to determine the rotation angle. These clever tasks force the neural network to truly delve into the structure of the world, not just memorize images.

💡 After self-supervised training on texts, the GPT-3 model turned out to be able to write poems, code, and scientific essays, although no one specifically taught it to do so.
Usually, to train a computer, thousands of labeled images are needed: “this is a cat”, “this is a dog.” Self-supervised learning dispenses with such manual labor. It invents its own task — for example, guessing which part of the text is missing, or reconstructing a hidden part of a photograph. It’s like solving a crossword puzzle: the questions serve as hints, and the answers are the target labels. This way, the machine understands deep connections: that a face with whiskers and a tail typically occur together. Later, this skill is applied to any other task — from defect detection to movie recommendations.

Self-supervised learning (SSL) constructs auxiliary tasks (pretext tasks) from unlabeled data to obtain informative representations applicable to subsequent downstream tasks. Formally, let D = {x_i} be an unlabeled dataset. A pretext task is defined with automatic generation of pseudo-labels y_i = T(x_i), where T is a transformation without human involvement (e.g., rotation, cropping, token masking). The model f_θ is trained to predict y_i, in the process extracting internal patterns from x. In contrastive learning, the InfoNCE loss serves as the criterion: L = - log( exp(sim(z_i, z_j^+)/τ) / Σ_k exp(sim(z_i, z_k)/τ) ), where z_i is the representation of the anchor image, z_j^+ is a positive pair, z_k are from a negative pool, and τ is a temperature parameter. The success of SSL is evident in NLP (BERT, GPT) and CV (SimCLR, MoCo, MAE), where a linear classifier on frozen representations achieves about 90% accuracy on ImageNet without a single label.

History

Ideas of self-supervision can be traced to works on autoencoders (Hinton, 2006) and representation learning through reconstruction. The turning point was 2018 with the emergence of BERT (Devlin et al.), which uses word masking. For images, in 2020, the SimCLR method (Chen et al.) showed that a simple contrastive scheme with large batch sizes is comparable to supervised learning. Later, Vision Transformers combined with masked autoencoding (MAE) replicated the success of NLP, demonstrating scalability.

How it works

The general SSL scheme includes: 1) a pretext task — automatic generation of a target signal; 2) an encoder that extracts representations; 3) optionally, a projector for metric comparison; 4) a loss function that solves the specific pretext task. In NLP: masked language modeling (MLM) — the task of recovering hidden tokens. The model receives a sequence with [MASK] tokens and learns to predict the original words via cross-entropy. In CV: contrastive methods learn representations invariant to augmentations, using large batches and a momentum encoder mechanism for stabilization. Recent masked autoencoders (MAE) mask random patches of the image, and the decoder reconstructs the pixels, forcing the model to understand the global structure of objects.

\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_j^+) / \tau)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_k) / \tau)}
zi — anchor representation, zj+ — positive sample (augmentation of the same source object), zk — other samples in the batch (negatives), sim — cosine similarity, τ — temperature, typically 0.1

Open problems & fun facts

Generalization of pretext tasks to new domains without manual design; Potential collapse of representations in unsuccessful contrastive learning; Effective combination of SSL and supervised learning with limited labeled data; Theoretical explanation for the success of simple pretext tasks
