Aharonov–Bohm effect

Electrons bypass an invisible barrier, and even empty space forces them to take a detour.

In practice: Based on this effect, ultra-precise magnetic field sensors and new types of transistors are created.
An electron beam, split in two, bypasses a microscopic coil and, upon recombination, produces fringes — like ripples on water. The shift of these fringes proves: the magnetic force here is zero, yet the coil still affects the electrons. Quantum mechanics describes particles as waves, and when moving in electromagnetic potentials, their wave phase changes. Even if the field (B) itself is zero, but the vector potential (A) is nonzero, the phase still accumulates. The experiment with a solenoid, whose field is confined inside and outside B=0, shows interference fringes shifted in proportion to the magnetic flux through the solenoid. This confirms that potentials are not just a mathematical abstraction but a physical reality.

History

In 1959, Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm theoretically showed that in the quantum world, it is not the fields themselves but their invisible 'prescribers' (electromagnetic potentials) that determine motion. A year later, R. G. Chambers in England confirmed this by observing electron interference around a tiny magnet.

How it works

An electron flies along two different paths around a concealed magnet and, when meeting itself, combines sometimes in step, sometimes out of step — the phase shift depends on the strength of the hidden field. Thus, the invisible prescriber leaves a mark on the particle's wave.

💡 Oscillations of electrical resistance in thin metal rings in a magnetic field are a direct manifestation of the Aharonov–Bohm effect, used in magnetometers.
A ship's mast hidden by fog. You don't see it, but the path around it still bends. In the same way, an electron senses a magnetic field even when it is completely isolated and does not touch it. An invisible 'shadow' of the field (the electromagnetic potential — a quantity that prescribes the future motion of a charge) guides the particle, even though at that point there are no electric or magnetic forces.

The diffraction pattern of electrons bypassing a magnetic solenoid shifts by fractions of a micron, although the electrons themselves never enter the field region. The Aharonov–Bohm effect is a quantum mechanical phenomenon in which a charged particle is influenced by electromagnetic potentials (φ, A) in regions where the fields (E, B) are zero. In classical physics, potentials serve merely as convenient tools, while force is determined by fields. In quantum mechanics, however, the wave function acquires a phase difference Δφ = (e/ħ) ∮ A·dl when traversing a closed loop, even if the particle does not pass through the field. This difference equals the magnetic flux Φ through the area enclosed by the loop and leads to a shift in interference fringes. The effect demonstrates the nonlocality of quantum mechanics and the fundamentality of gauge fields.

History

The idea dates back to discussions on the role of potentials in quantum mechanics. In 1959, Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm published a paper describing a hypothetical experiment. The first experimental confirmation was performed by R. G. Chambers in 1960 using an interferometer with a magnetic whisker. Later, Akira Tonomura and colleagues conducted demonstrative experiments with electron holography that left no doubts. The effect is closely related to the Berry phase and has led to the development of interferometric devices.

How it works

In the Schrödinger equation for a charged particle, the vector potential A appears. When traversing a closed loop C, the wave function acquires an additional phase Δφ = (e/ħ) ∮_C A·dl. By Stokes' theorem, this integral equals the flux of the magnetic field B through any surface bounded by C: Φ = ∫ B·dS. If the field B is concentrated inside a solenoid and does not penetrate the region of motion, the flux Φ is nonetheless nonzero, causing a shift in interference fringes. Gauge invariance ensures that only the flux modulo 2π is observable.

\Delta\varphi = \frac{e}{\hbar} \oint_C \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}
Δφ — phase shift, e — elementary charge (≈ 1.602176634×10^{-19} C), ℏ — reduced Planck constant (≈ 1.0545718×10^{-34} J·s), ∮_C A·dl — circulation of the vector potential A along the closed loop C, equal to the magnetic flux Φ
