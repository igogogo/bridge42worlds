Tag

computer vision

3

A baby, opening its eyes for the first time, learns to link patches of light to shapes and movement to objects. Computer vision does the same: it learns to see meaning in sets of pixels.

In practice: A car recognizes road signs and pedestrians in real time to brake or alert the driver in time.
Computer vision relies on convolutional neural networks (CNNs). Their architecture mirrors the visual cortex: simple cells respond to lines at specific angles, complex cells to their combinations. A convolutional layer scans the image with a small window, multiplying it by a filter and producing feature maps. Several such layers sequentially build increasingly abstract representations: from edges and textures to object parts and entire faces. Modern systems also use attention mechanisms and transformers, treating the image as a sequence of patches.

History

It all began in the 1960s when scientists tried to teach a computer to distinguish simple geometric shapes. The first steps were difficult: Larry Roberts' program in 1963 was able to 'outline' the edges of a brick in a photo. Then for decades, edge detectors and segmentation were developed. The real breakthrough came in 2012: the neural network AlexNet won the ImageNet image recognition competition by a large margin, showing that deep neural networks could handle this task much better than previous methods.

How it works

Training resembles a soccer goalkeeper's practice: the more balls he deflects, the better he predicts the trajectory. Similarly, the neural network processes millions of labeled images, each time guessing whether it's a cat or a dog. The error is calculated and sent back to adjust the weights—the sensitivity of each virtual 'neuron'—in tiny steps. Gradually, the network becomes an expert.

💡 The Google Translate system can translate text on a menu photo in real time: you point the camera at foreign text, and it appears on screen in your language—all thanks to image recognition and understanding.
Computer vision is the ability of programs to 'see': to receive an image from a camera and extract information from it. Imagine you look at a photo and describe it: 'a cat is sleeping on a rug.' The program, instead of eyes, uses a matrix of numbers—the brightness of each pixel (dot). It processes these numbers, finding patterns: where are the boundaries, what is the shape, what color. For this, neural networks are created—mathematical constructions resembling interconnected knots that learn from examples.

Computer vision is an interdisciplinary field combining signal processing theory, machine learning, robotics, and cognitive science, aimed at automatic extraction of meaningful information from images and video. It is defined by four classic tasks: classification (assigning a label), detection (localization and labeling), segmentation (pixel-wise labeling), and 3D structure recovery. Methods are divided into 'pre-deep learning' (SIFT, HOG, SVM) and 'post-deep learning' (CNN, Vision Transformers, diffusion models). Key benchmarks: ImageNet, COCO, Cityscapes.

History

The work of Hubel and Wiesel (1959) on the visual cortex is considered the starting point, inspiring hierarchical models. In 1963, Larry Roberts implemented the 'Blocks World' program, interpreting photos of cubes. In the 1970s, geometric methods developed rapidly. In the 1990s, the first effective edge detectors appeared. Convolutional networks were proposed by Yann LeCun in 1989 for digit recognition. The real revolution came in 2012 when Alex Krizhevsky and colleagues released AlexNet, using GPUs—accuracy on ImageNet jumped from ~74% to ~84%. In subsequent years, architectures deepened: VGG, ResNet (2015) solving the vanishing gradient problem through skip connections. Since 2020, Vision Transformers (Dosovitskiy et al.) brought self-attention to images, and models like DALL·E 2 and Stable Diffusion learned to generate new images from textual descriptions.

How it works

The main building block is convolution: a kernel of size k×k is multiplied element-wise with a patch of the image, and the result is summed. A pooling layer reduces resolution, emphasizing the most important. Fully connected layers take the final feature vector and output a prediction. Training is done via gradient descent with backpropagation. In modern systems, a large model is often pre-trained on large datasets (ImageNet-21k) and then fine-tuned for a target task (transfer learning). For image generation, autoencoders (VAE), generative adversarial networks (GAN), and recently diffusion probabilistic models are used, gradually removing noise from an initial random field until a clear picture emerges.

Open problems & fun facts

Robustness in non-standard conditions (night, fog, unusual camera angles).; Interpretability: why did the model make that decision?; Dependence on labeled data and domain adaptation.; Ensuring privacy in face recognition.; Temporal consistency in video: absence of flickering artifacts from frame to frame.; Energy efficiency of inference on edge devices.
