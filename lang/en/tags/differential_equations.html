A speedometer shows speed at every moment. An odometer shows the distance traveled. Between them is an invisible thread connecting the moment with the total. This is the language of change.

In practice: Differential equations describe how coffee cools, populations grow, and signals propagate in neurons.
A differential equation is a compass for a moving world. It tells us not where we are, but where we are going and how fast, depending on our surroundings. Solving it means reconstructing the entire route from instantaneous clues. Such equations can be ordinary (with one independent variable, such as time) or partial (with many variables, as in describing a vibrating string). They underlie everything: from a ball's trajectory to the dynamics of galaxies.

History

In the 17th century, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz created calculus—a tool for working with continuous change. Thus was born the language in which the laws of motion, heat transfer, and electromagnetism are formulated.

How it works

Imagine a pool. Water drains through a hole. The flow rate depends on the water level, and the level changes due to the flow—a feedback loop. A differential equation describes this relationship and lets you calculate when the pool will be empty. It takes the instantaneous rate of change and reconstructs the whole story.

A differential equation is a mathematical relationship linking an unknown function with its derivatives. It sets a rule whereby the rate of change of a quantity at each point in space or time depends on the quantity itself. The order of the equation is determined by the highest derivative, and its degree of linearity depends on the presence of nonlinear combinations of the function and its derivatives. Equations can be ordinary (ODEs, one variable) or partial (PDEs, several variables). Solving a differential equation reconstructs the integral picture from the local law of change and requires setting initial or boundary conditions. Most fundamental laws of physics are formulated as differential equations. That is why the speedometer and odometer are not just instruments, but visible images of a profound mathematical connection.

History

The origins lie in the 17th century, when Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz independently created differential and integral calculus. Newton formulated the second law of motion as a differential equation. In the 18th century, Leonhard Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, and Pierre-Simon Laplace developed methods for solving broad classes of equations, especially in celestial mechanics. The 19th century brought the theory of partial differential equations: Jean Baptiste Joseph Fourier—the heat equation, James Clerk Maxwell—electrodynamics. In the 20th century, Henri Poincaré's qualitative theory and functional analysis made it possible to study solvability and stability even without explicit formulas.

How it works

Consider a first-order ODE dy/dx = f(x, y). Geometrically, this equation defines a direction field on the plane: at each point (x, y), the slope of a small segment equals f(x, y). An integral curve passing through a given initial point must be tangent to this field at every point. The simplest solution method is separation of variables, if f(x, y) factorizes. For linear ODEs with constant coefficients, solutions are sought as exponentials; multiple roots of the characteristic equation lead to resonant terms of the form x e^{λx}. Partial differential equations require more delicate techniques: separation of variables, Green's functions, Fourier transforms. The well-posedness of a problem (existence, uniqueness, stability of the solution) is established by theorems, such as the Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem for analytic PDEs or the Galerkin method for weak solutions. Numerical methods (finite differences, finite elements) allow approximating solutions with controlled accuracy.
