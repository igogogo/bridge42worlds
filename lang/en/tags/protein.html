protein

5

Protein is a molecular machine built from a chain of amino acids that performs almost all work in the cell: from oxygen transport to protection against microbes.

In practice: Proteins are part of food, enzymes in laundry detergents, and their design using AI opens the way to new drugs.
Proteins are large molecules built from one or more chains of amino acids (polypeptide chains). Amino acids are connected by special bonds (peptide bonds). From 20 types of amino acids, a unique sequence is formed that determines how the chain folds into a three-dimensional shape. Proteins perform many tasks: they act as catalysts (enzymes that speed up chemical reactions), transporters, building materials, signaling molecules, and defenders (antibodies).

History

The word 'protein' comes from egg white, which coagulates when heated. In the 19th century, chemists began studying the composition of proteins, and in the 1950s, the first spatial structure of a protein was deciphered.

How it works

A gene in DNA encodes the sequence of amino acids in a protein. The cell assembles the protein on a ribosome (a molecular machine for protein assembly), like on an assembly line. Then the chain folds itself into the required three-dimensional shape, which determines its function.

💡 Spider web is almost pure fibroin protein, which surpasses steel of the same weight in tensile strength.
Protein is like beads, where each bead is an amino acid. There are only 20 types, but they can be arranged into an infinite variety of forms. The folded chain becomes a protein with its function. For example, hemoglobin carries oxygen, and keratin makes hair strong.

Proteins are linear polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. The order of amino acids is determined by the genetic code. The folding of the polypeptide chain into the native three-dimensional conformation occurs spontaneously and is determined by the amino acid sequence (Anfinsen's hypothesis). Structural hierarchy: primary (sequence), secondary (α-helices, β-sheets), tertiary (overall folding), and quaternary (assembly of subunits). Proteins perform catalytic (enzymes), structural (collagen, keratin), transport (hemoglobin), protective (antibodies), signaling (receptors, hormones), and motor (myosin) functions. Their dysfunction underlies many diseases. Structure determination methods include X-ray crystallography, NMR, and cryo-electron microscopy.

History

The concept of proteins as a special class of substances emerged in the 18th century. In 1838, Jöns Jacob Berzelius proposed the term 'protein'. In 1902, Franz Hofmeister discovered that amino acids are linked by peptide bonds. The primary structure of a protein (insulin) was deciphered by Frederick Sanger in 1953. The spatial structure of myoglobin was determined in 1958 by John Kendrew using X-ray crystallography, whose methods were developed by William Lawrence Bragg. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AlphaFold) has revolutionized protein structure prediction.

How it works

Proteins are synthesized on ribosomes during translation. Genetic information from mRNA is read by codons; tRNA delivers the corresponding amino acid. The growing polypeptide chain often undergoes post-translational modifications (phosphorylation, glycosylation, etc.), which affect protein activity and localization. Protein folding often occurs with the help of molecular chaperones. Misfolding leads to aggregation and diseases (e.g., Alzheimer's disease). Proteins can interact with other molecules with high specificity, forming complex networks of signaling pathways.

Open problems & fun facts

Complete understanding of the protein folding mechanism; Prediction of protein functions de novo; Creation of artificial proteins with desired properties
