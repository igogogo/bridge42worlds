Casimir effect

Two mirrors in emptiness attract, as if the emptiness itself presses on them from outside.

In practice: The effect causes tiny parts in microchips to stick together without any lubricant.
At a distance of 10 nanometers, two mirror plates are compressed with a pressure of one atmosphere, even though there is an absolute vacuum around them. This is the Casimir effect: quantum fluctuations give rise to a real force in the void. The vacuum constantly boils with pairs of particles and antiparticles that exist for instants. In the gap between the plates, only those fluctuations fit whose wavelength is a multiple of the distance—like standing waves on a string. Outside, however, the spectrum is unrestricted. As a result, the 'population' of particles is larger outside, and it presses on the plates, pushing them together. The force quickly weakens with increasing gap—inversely proportional to the fourth power.

History

The effect was predicted by Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir in 1948. It was first measured by American Steve Lamoreaux in 1997 using precise torsion balances.

How it works

Quantum tremors (fluctuations) of the electromagnetic field are ubiquitous. But in the gap between the plates, only those tremors survive whose wavelength fits an integer number of times. There are fewer of them, and the pressure difference brings the surfaces together.

💡 If the plates were perfectly smooth and infinite, the Casimir force at microscopic distances could reach tens of atmospheres.
In empty space, ghost particles (virtual particles) are constantly born and disappear. Between two close plates, there is less room for them than outside. Therefore, there are more particles outside, and they push the plates toward each other—like ocean waves that hit the outer walls of a ship harder and squeeze it.

When the distance between the plates is less than a micron, quantum vacuum fluctuations become a tangible force. The Casimir effect is the attraction of two uncharged conducting bodies in a vacuum, caused by the change in the zero-point energy of the electromagnetic field due to imposed boundaries. In quantum electrodynamics, each field mode has zero-point energy. In the presence of boundaries, the set of modes is rearranged: between the plates, only modes with a wavelength that is a multiple of the gap are allowed, whereas in the outer space the spectrum is continuous. The integral over the pressure difference outside and inside yields the resultant attractive force. For perfectly conducting plates, the force per unit area is F/A = -π²ħc/(240a⁴). At temperatures above zero, thermal photons contribute and the force increases. In nanomechanics, the effect is one of the main sources of stiction (sticking) of parts.

History

The idea came to Casimir during discussions with Bohr about the properties of colloidal solutions. Together with Dirk Polder, he derived a formula for the force between two perfectly conducting plates. Later, Evgeny Lifshitz generalized the theory to real materials, taking into account finite conductivity and roughness. The first confident measurement was made by Steve Lamoreaux in 1997 using torsion balances; it was followed by experiments with an atomic force microscope that confirmed the dependence on distance and surface properties.

How it works

Quantization of the electromagnetic field in the presence of boundaries leads to a renormalization of the zero-point energy. The mode density between the plates is lower than in the outer space, and the integrated pressure difference yields an attractive force. In the formula, the force is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the distance. For real metals at small distances, retardation starts to play a role, and the force transitions to the van der Waals force. At higher temperatures, thermal fluctuations enhance the effect.

\frac{F}{A} = -\frac{\hbar c \pi^2}{240 a^4}
F/A — force per unit area, minus sign indicates attraction, ℏ — reduced Planck constant (≈ 1.0545718×10^{-34} J·s), c — speed of light in vacuum (≈ 2.99792458×10^8 m/s), a — distance between plates
