Wigner function

A quantum particle has no trajectory, but the Wigner function can be thought of as its probabilistic portrait in phase space. It shows where the particle might be and how fast it might move, seamlessly blending both properties.

In practice: The Wigner function aids in developing quantum computers—it reveals errors in qubits and allows for precise tomographic images of quantum states.
The Wigner function represents a quantum state in phase space (the space of coordinates and momenta). It is a quasiprobability distribution: integrating over momenta yields the probability for coordinates, and vice versa. However, unlike true probability, it can take negative values. Negative regions indicate quantum correlations and non-classicality, serving as a resource in quantum computing. The map of the Wigner function, even with negative peaks, remains the most detailed snapshot of quantum reality.

History

In 1932, Eugene Wigner invented this tool to bridge quantum mechanics with statistical physics and to visually demonstrate the transition to classical behavior.

How it works

Take the wave function (the primary quantum description) and perform specific mathematical operations—the result is the Wigner function. It behaves almost like a probability but can assume negative values. These negative dips are a clear sign of quantum interference, which has no analog in the classical world.

💡 The negativity of the Wigner function is a precise indicator that a quantum state cannot be efficiently simulated on a classical computer (quantum supremacy).
In classical physics, we specify an object's position and velocity. In the quantum world, these two quantities cannot be measured precisely simultaneously (uncertainty principle). The Wigner function is a special graph that distributes 'quantum uncertainty' across two axes: position and momentum (mass times velocity). It resembles a topographic map: hills indicate high probability of finding a particle with given parameters, valleys indicate low probability. Negative values are dips on the map, impossible for ordinary probabilities. Their depth is comparable to units of Planck's constant—a number with 34 zeros after the decimal point, finer than splitting a hair into trillions of parts. Thus, the map of the Wigner function, with its hills and ravines, becomes a guide to the elusive quantum world.

The Wigner function is the Weyl transform of the density matrix into phase space. It is real but not positive definite, hence called a quasiprobability distribution. The marginals (integrals over momentum or position) yield true probability distributions. For pure states, negativity arises from interference terms. For Gaussian states, the Wigner function is always nonnegative. In quantum optics, it enables reconstruction of complete field state information.

History

Proposed by Hungarian-American physicist Eugene Wigner in 1932 as a bridge between quantum and classical statistical mechanics. Later, it became a key tool in quantum optics and quantum information. Experimental measurement of the Wigner function became possible with the development of homodyne tomography in the 1990s.

How it works

The Wigner function for a pure state with wave function \(\psi(x)\) is defined as \\(W(x,p) = \frac{1}{\pi \hbar} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^*(x+y) \psi(x-y) e^{2i p y / \hbar} dy\\). For mixed states, it is analogous with the density matrix. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle manifests itself in constraints on the possible shapes of the distribution. Negativity is a direct consequence of the superposition principle and cannot be eliminated by any classical mixing.

W(x,p) = \frac{1}{\pi \hbar} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^*(x+y) \psi(x-y) e^{2i p y / \hbar} dy
x — position, p — momentum, \hbar — reduced Planck constant (≈ 1.054 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s), \psi — wave function, i — imaginary unit
