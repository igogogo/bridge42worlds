antennas

The gramophone horn collected the faint sounds of the needle and directed them into the room. An antenna similarly captures radio waves and focuses them into an electric current.

In practice: The antenna in your phone maintains a connection with the tower, the GPS receiver picks up satellite signals from 20,000 kilometers away, and the satellite TV dish receives broadcasts from space.
An antenna is a transducer between electromagnetic waves and currents in the transmission line. The key characteristic is the radiation pattern: the directions in which the antenna radiates (or receives) most strongly. A simple dipole (two straight conductors each a quarter-wavelength long) radiates uniformly in the plane perpendicular to itself, but not along its axis. Parabolic reflector antennas (satellite dishes) concentrate energy into a narrow beam, like a searchlight. The size of any antenna is dictated by the wavelength: for transoceanic flights, vast fields of masts are built, while for cellular communication, antennas fit in the palm of your hand.

History

The first antennas were built by Heinrich Hertz in 1888 for his experiments with electric sparks. In 1895, Alexander Popov used a raised wire to detect lightning discharges, and later Guglielmo Marconi transmitted a signal across the Atlantic using an antenna.

How it works

Electricity runs back and forth in the antenna, producing an alternating current. Around such a current, according to the laws of physics, oscillating electric and magnetic fields are born, which escape into space at the speed of light. During reception, it's the reverse: the incoming wave agitates electrons in the antenna, creating a weak current that the receiver amplifies.

💡 Your credit card can be paid contactlessly thanks to a tiny antenna hidden inside the plastic—a loop of conductor that powers the chip with radio wave energy over a distance of a few centimeters.
An antenna is a device that transmits or receives radio waves. In a transmitter, it works like a loudspeaker, converting an electric signal into waves, and in a receiver, like a microphone, catching waves and turning them back into current. The simplest antenna is a piece of wire cut in the middle: a generator or receiver is connected to the ends. The wire length must match the wavelength so that oscillations enter resonance.

An antenna is a device that provides radiation and reception of electromagnetic waves, matching the wave impedance of free space (377 Ω) to the impedance of the feeder line. Fundamental parameters: input impedance, bandwidth, directivity, gain (including losses), polarization. Types of antennas: wire (dipoles, monopoles, loops), aperture (horn, reflector), microstrip, phased array antennas. In transmit mode, the high-frequency current creates an alternating electromagnetic field, which in the far field (R > 2D²/λ) takes the form of a spherical wave; fields decay as 1/R, power decay rate is 1/R². In receive mode, the incident wave induces an EMF in the conductors.

History

The theoretical foundation was laid by James Clerk Maxwell in 1864, predicting electromagnetic waves. Experimental confirmation and the first dipole antennas—Heinrich Hertz, 1887–1888. In the 1890s, Oliver Lodge invented a tuned antenna with an inductive coil. Practical radio communication was established by Popov and Marconi using vertical wires. Sergei Nadenenko derived the formula for the radiation field in 1914. In 1926, Hidetsugu Yagi and Shintaro Uda created a multi-element directive antenna (now known as a Yagi–Uda antenna). The development of microwave technology in the 1940s led to the creation of waveguides and horns. The end of the 20th century was marked by the emergence of microstrip antennas and adaptive arrays with digital beamforming.

How it works

Antenna radiation is described by Maxwell's equations. The accelerated motion of charges in a conductor creates an alternating electromagnetic field that propagates as a wave. In the far field, the electric and magnetic fields are transverse to the direction of propagation and are related as E/H = Z₀ ≈ 377 Ω. For quantitative calculation, the vector potential is used: A(r) = (μ₀/4π) ∫ J(r') e^{-j k |r-r'|} / |r-r'| dV'. From A, the fields are found. In a receiving antenna, the wave induces a current distribution, which through the reciprocity theorem is related to the antenna's characteristics in transmit mode. Phased arrays sum signals from many elements with controlled phase, enabling beamforming and steering, as well as adaptive interference suppression (spatial filtering). Impedance matching is critical: mismatch leads to reflections and a drop in efficiency.
