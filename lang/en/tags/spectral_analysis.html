Spectral Analysis

6

White light passing through a prism turns into a rainbow. Spectral analysis breaks down any signal—sound, radio waves, light from distant stars—into similar colored bands, telling us what it is made of.

In practice: Astronomers use a star’s spectrum to learn what elements it is composed of. In medicine, tissue spectra help distinguish healthy cells from cancerous ones.
Spectral analysis is a universal key. It doesn’t require touching the object; it’s enough to capture its emission. For example, light from a distant galaxy passing through a gas cloud loses very specific colors—they are absorbed by atoms. Such an absorption spectrum resembles a barcode: each dark line corresponds to a particular element. From the shift of these lines, one can even measure how fast the object is moving toward or away from us (the Doppler effect, a change in frequency due to motion). And in the radio range, spectral analysis ‘listens’ to molecular clouds, finding complex organic molecules.

History

In 1814, German optician Joseph von Fraunhofer was the first to systematically describe the dark lines in the Sun’s spectrum. Later, scientists understood that each chemical element leaves its unique mark in the spectrum.

How it works

A gas or heated body does not emit light of all colors, but only specific ones—those that resonate with its atoms. Passing this light through a prism or a diffraction grating (a plate with fine lines), we see individual bright lines against a dark background. Just as a guitar string sounds at its note, each atom glows at its own frequencies. By analyzing the positions of the lines, we learn what element we are looking at.

💡 The spectrum is the only way to know the chemistry of distant objects: no space probe has ever visited the Sun, but we know for certain that it consists mainly of hydrogen and helium, thanks to spectral lines.
A spectrum is like a musical chord: it is made up of many pure tones (simple vibrations). Spectral analysis is like being able to hear each individual note in the chord. Only instead of sound, we analyze light, radio signals, or even the pulsations of a distant star. The output is a graph showing how much energy is at each ‘note’ (frequency). Sharp peaks (‘notes’) reveal the presence of specific substances—like fingerprints.

Spectral analysis is a set of methods for determining the composition and physical parameters of a substance from its spectrum. The spectrum is obtained by spatially separating the radiation by wavelength using dispersive elements (e.g., a diffraction grating). Depending on the nature of the light–matter interaction, emission (radiation), absorption, and Raman (scattering) spectra are distinguished. Each chemical element has a unique set of spectral lines corresponding to quantum transitions of its electrons. The width, intensity, and shape of the lines carry information about the temperature, pressure, velocity, and magnetic fields of the source. The method is fundamental to astrophysics, plasma physics, analytical chemistry, and medical diagnostics.

History

The origins of spectral analysis lie in 17th-century optics (Newton decomposed light with a prism). In 1802, Wollaston noticed dark lines in the solar spectrum. From 1814 to 1823, Joseph von Fraunhofer catalogued them, not knowing the cause. The decisive step was taken by Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen in 1859, who proved that each line is produced by a specific chemical element. Thus, the chemical analysis of radiation was born. Later, Johannes Rydberg (1890) derived a formula for hydrogen lines, and quantum mechanics explained spectra based on electron transitions.

How it works

Atoms absorb or emit photons of precisely defined energies equal to the difference between the energy levels of electrons. A transition from one level to another corresponds to a spectral line at frequency ν = ΔE/h. Spectra are classified into line spectra of atoms, band spectra of molecules (rotational and vibrational transitions), and continuous spectra of hot dense bodies (thermal radiation). For analysis, dispersive instruments (prism, diffraction grating), interferometers (high resolution), and photodetectors are used. Key characteristics: line position (wavelength), intensity, width, and line shape (Doppler and Lorentz broadening). The methods are applied across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, from radio to gamma rays.
