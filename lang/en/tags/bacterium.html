bacterium

3

Bacterium is a microscopic factory: inside it, the work of thousands of molecular machines boils without interruption.

In practice: Bacteria ferment yogurt and kefir, produce insulin for diabetics, and help extract useful minerals from low-grade ores.
Bacteria are prokaryotic (lacking a nucleus) single-celled organisms of microscopic size (0.5–5 μm). Their circular DNA is concentrated in the nucleoid. The cell wall made of peptidoglycan gives shape (cocci, bacilli, spirilla) and protects against osmotic shock. They feed in various ways: photosynthesis (cyanobacteria), fermentation, respiration with or without oxygen. Plasmids – small circular DNA – often carry antibiotic resistance genes and are transferred between bacteria.

History

The first to glimpse bacteria was Robert Hooke in 1665, examining a thin slice of cork under a microscope, and they were described in detail by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek a decade later.

How it works

The bacterium grows faster and faster until it divides in two. In warm milk, it does this every 20 minutes, which is why yogurt thickens overnight. Many bacteria are equipped with a flagellum – a tiny propeller that rotates and pushes the cell forward.

💡 The total mass of bacteria on Earth exceeds the mass of all plants and animals combined.
Bacteria are the smallest living creatures, made of a single cell. Unlike the cells of our body, they have no nucleus: their genetic information (DNA) floats directly in the cytoplasm. Most bacteria are harmless – without them there would be no cheese or sourdough bread.

Bacteria are a domain of prokaryotes characterized by the absence of a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles. Sizes range from 0.2 to 750 μm (Thiomargarita namibiensis). Metabolic diversity is enormous: chemolithoautotrophs oxidize inorganic substances, phototrophs use light, organotrophs use organic matter. The cell envelope of gram-positive bacteria contains a thick layer of peptidoglycan with teichoic acids, while gram-negative have a thin layer and an outer membrane with lipopolysaccharides. The bacterial flagellum is a unique rotational nanomotor powered by a proton or sodium gradient. Bacteria reproduce by binary fission; generation time under optimal conditions for E. coli is 20 minutes. Genetic exchange via conjugation, transformation, and transduction facilitates rapid adaptation. Biofilms – structured communities encased in an extracellular matrix – enhance antibiotic resistance.

History

In 1665, Robert Hooke described the cellular structure of cork and later drew microorganisms, but the true discoverer of bacteria is considered to be Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1676), who created lenses with 300x magnification. Louis Pasteur in the 1860s disproved the theory of spontaneous generation and proved that bacteria cause fermentation. Robert Koch (1876–1882) identified the causative agents of anthrax, tuberculosis, and cholera, and formulated Koch's postulates. Alexander Fleming in 1928 discovered penicillin. Carl Woese in 1977, based on analysis of 16S rRNA, separated archaea, dividing prokaryotes into two domains.

How it works

The exponential growth phase is described by the formula N(t)=N₀·2^(t/g). In stationary phase, stress-resistance genes are turned on. Chemotaxis: a network of signaling proteins (CheA, CheY) controls the direction of flagellar rotation, enabling bacteria to swim along gradients of attractants. Quorum sensing: using autoinducers (e.g., AHL in gram-negatives), bacteria assess their density and coordinate behavior – synchronously switching on bioluminescence or virulence genes. Transcription of operons (e.g., lac operon) is regulated by repressors and inducers.

N(t) = N_0 \cdot 2^{t/g}
N(t) – population size at time t, N₀ – initial population size, g – generation time (doubling time). For Escherichia coli g ≈ 20 minutes in optimal medium at 37°C.
