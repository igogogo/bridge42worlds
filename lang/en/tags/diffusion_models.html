Diffusion Models

1

Snowy 'noise' on a TV screen slowly transforms into a clear face. This is how diffusion models work—they remove randomness step by step until a meaningful image emerges.

In practice: Generating photographs from text descriptions, improving telescope images, designing new materials.
Diffusion models are a generative approach that transforms Gaussian noise into ordered data, such as a photo of a cat. The process resembles restoring a mosaic from memory: you have only a rough sketch, but you remember what similar works looked like, and you gradually recreate the pattern—from fuzzy patches to fine lines. The model contains a 'representation' of the world, learned from millions of examples, and thus can generate plausible new images. The key difference from other generative networks is iterativity: the picture is born not instantly but through a chain of small improvements.

History

The first ideas emerged in 2015 from the physics of non-equilibrium systems. In 2020, scientists from OpenAI and Google proved that this approach could surpass other methods in image quality. Today, diffusion powers DALL-E, Midjourney, and other tools.

How it works

Training is like a reverse game of telephone. First, take a real photo and repeatedly mix in random pixels—the image 'degrades', turning into noise. The model learns to guess what the picture was before distortion. After training, it is given pure noise—and, going backward, it 'develops' a new image that never existed before.

💡 Although diffusion seems slow, modern versions create an image in just half a second on a powerful graphics chip.
Diffusion models create an image from random points. Imagine fogged glass: at first nothing is visible—only gray smudges. The model, like an artist, adds details drop by drop, clarifying the contours. First an oval of a face appears, then eyes, mouth. It all starts with pure noise (a jumble of random numbers) that is processed hundreds of times. Each step slightly reduces the disorder, relying on what the model 'knows' about the structure of objects from analyzing thousands of real photographs.

Diffusion models in machine learning are a class of deep generative models based on the principle of gradual destruction and subsequent restoration of information in a random process. Fundamentally, they use a stochastic forward process that sequentially adds noise to data (e.g., to image pixels) according to a predefined schedule, and a reverse process trained by a neural network for denoising. Formally, the forward diffusion process is defined by a Markov chain that brings the distribution of real data to a standard normal distribution. The reverse process, also Markovian, is parameterized by a neural network that predicts the noise added at each step. Training involves minimizing the distance between the true and predicted noise. After training, new data can be sampled by inputting pure noise and applying the reverse process step by step. This approach does not use adversarial training like GANs, nor does it require variational inference like VAEs, which largely stabilizes training and improves generation quality.

History

The method's roots go back to works on thermodynamics and statistical physics (e.g., the works of Langevin), but the modern embodiment was proposed in 2015 by Jascha Sohl-Dickstein as a stochastic generative model based on non-equilibrium thermodynamics. The breakthrough came after the 2020 publication of the paper 'Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models,' which demonstrated outstanding results on image synthesis tasks. Further improvements (accelerated sampling, conditional guidance) led to the creation of systems like DALL-E 2, Imagen, Stable Diffusion, which revolutionized the field of AI creativity.

How it works

Forward process: to the original image x0, small amounts of Gaussian noise are added over T steps, obtaining a sequence x1, x2, ..., xT, where xT is practically indistinguishable from white noise. The transition at each step: q(xt|xt-1) = N(xt; sqrt(1-βt)·xt-1, βtI), where βt is the variance schedule. Reverse process: a neural network εθ(xt, t) learns to predict the noise added at step t, minimizing E[||ε - εθ(xt, t)||²]. During generation, one starts from xT ~ N(0, I) and iteratively applies xt-1 = (1/√(αt))·(xt - (1-αt)/√(1-ᾱt)·εθ(xt, t)) + σt·z, where z is noise. To speed up sampling, DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) with step skipping is used. Conditional models augment the input with information about text, class, etc., guiding the reverse process.

q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t} x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)
xt — noisy data at step t, βt — noise drift coefficient, I — identity matrix

Open problems & fun facts

High computational cost of sampling without optimization; Difficulties in controlling rare concepts and precise instruction following; Potential generation of undesirable content and the need for filtering; Scaling to video and 3D data

💡 Training Stable Diffusion v2.1 used the LAION-5B dataset of 5.85 billion image-text pairs, and the model itself has around 860 million parameters.
