molecular property prediction

3

How to guess the taste of a dish without tasting it, by the list of ingredients. This is how computers learn to predict the properties of molecules — their toxicity, solubility or drug efficacy — avoiding lengthy laboratory experiments.

In practice: Speeds up the development of new materials and drugs, from biodegradable plastics to targeted anticancer agents.
Molecular property prediction is an interdisciplinary field at the intersection of chemistry, physics, and computer science, where computer models are trained on experimental data to estimate the characteristics of molecules without laboratory synthesis. Models can be based on physical laws (quantum mechanics) or on statistical analysis of big data (machine learning). They predict parameters such as toxicity, biological activity, solubility, melting point, and even the color of a substance.

History

The idea emerged in the mid-20th century along with the development of quantum chemistry (the science that describes the behavior of electrons in molecules). The first mathematical models linking structure and properties were proposed by chemist Corwin Hansch in the 1960s. With the advent of powerful computers and large databases in the 21st century, the method became truly accurate and fast.

How it works

The computer model is fed thousands of examples: for each molecule in the database, its structure and measured property (say, boiling point) are known. The model learns to associate structural features (e.g., number of carbon atoms, presence of certain groups) with the resulting number. After training, it is given a new molecule — and it produces a prediction, like a psychic but based on rigorous patterns.

💡 Modern algorithms can check millions of molecule candidates for a new drug in seconds, whereas synthesizing and testing a single molecule in the lab takes weeks and costs thousands of dollars.
Molecules are tiny combinations of atoms, and each has its own properties: how strong it smells, whether it dissolves in water, whether it can serve as a medicine. Previously, the only way to find out was a laboratory experiment. Now scientists train computer programs (algorithms that find patterns in data) to 'guess' these properties just by looking at the molecule's structure. It's similar to how an experienced chef can tell from a recipe whether the soup will be salty or spicy without tasting every spoonful.

Molecular property prediction encompasses a wide range of computational approaches to estimate the physicochemical and biological characteristics of molecules. Starting from the fundamental principle that the properties of a substance are determined by its electronic structure (described by the Schrödinger equation), modern methods follow two paths. The first is quantum-chemical calculations (e.g., density functional theory), which allow the computation of energy, dipole moment, absorption spectra based solely on nuclear positions and electron numbers, but require significant computational resources. The second is empirical machine learning models (gradient boosting, neural networks, graph convolutions), trained on experimental data from public databases (PubChem, ChEMBL). In these approaches, a molecule is represented as a set of descriptors (topological, geometric, electrostatic) or directly as a graph, where vertices are atoms and edges are bonds. The trained model captures complex nonlinear 'structure-property' relationships and can generalize to molecules not included in the training set. A key challenge is interpretability: the physical mechanism often remains hidden in the 'black box' of the algorithm.

History

The roots go back to the 1960s, when Corwin Hansch formulated quantitative structure-activity relationships (QSAR), proposing linear equations linking substituent hydrophobicity to biological activity. In the 1970s–80s, advances in computing made it possible to include quantum-chemical indices and three-dimensional descriptors. A breakthrough came in the 2010s with the emergence of deep learning and GPU availability: models learned to 'read' molecules directly from graph structures, achieving high accuracy in predicting solubility, toxicity, and excited-state energies. Today, property prediction is an integral part of virtual screening in pharmaceutics and the design of new materials.

How it works

At the core of any prediction is the mapping of a molecule into a space of descriptors or a vector representation. The simplest descriptors are atom counts of a certain type, molecular weight, surface area. More complex ones encode information about rings, hydrogen bond donors/acceptors, partial charges (calculated, for example, by the Gasteiger method). In graph neural networks, each atom receives a feature vector, which is iteratively updated by messages from neighboring atoms; the final molecular vector is aggregated and fed to a fully connected layer that outputs a prediction. Training minimizes a loss function, such as mean squared error for continuous properties or cross-entropy for classification. The most important physical laws underlying quantum methods are the Schrödinger equation (time-independent and time-dependent) and the Pauli principle (the exclusion of two fermions occupying the same quantum state). For statistical models, physics is not explicitly required, but feature selection is often motivated by the principle 'like dissolves like': similar molecules have similar properties. Model quality is checked on a hold-out set using metrics (R², RMSE, AUC).

Open problems & fun facts

Interpretability of black-box models: why does the neural network consider a molecule toxic?; Transfer of predictions to new classes of compounds (generalization ability); Accounting for environmental conditions (temperature, pressure, solvent) in a single model; Prediction of quantum properties with accuracy comparable to ab initio methods but in fractions of a second
