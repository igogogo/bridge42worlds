virus

3

A virus is like a USB drive with malicious code: it inserts into a cell and reprograms it to produce its own copies.

In practice: Viruses cause diseases (influenza, COVID-19) but are also used as vectors for gene delivery in gene therapy.
A virus is a nanoscale infectious agent that parasitizes cells. Its genetic material – DNA or RNA – is packed into a protein capsid; some viruses have a lipid envelope. The virus is incapable of independent metabolism and reproduction – it necessarily uses the resources of the host cell. After penetration, viral genes integrate into the cellular machinery, forcing it to synthesize viral proteins and replicate the viral genome. Assembly of new virions occurs in the cytoplasm or nucleus, after which they exit, destroying the cell or budding off.

History

Viruses were discovered by Russian scientist Dmitry Ivanovsky in 1892 while studying tobacco mosaic disease. He showed that the disease passes through filters that trap bacteria.

How it works

The virus attaches to the cell like a key to a lock, then penetrates inside. There it releases its genes, and the cell begins to execute them, like a worker given false instructions, assembling viral parts instead of its own. Eventually the cell dies, and the viruses spread.

💡 Some viruses can integrate their genetic material into the host DNA and remain there for years without causing symptoms (like the herpes virus).
A virus is a tiny particle that can enter a living cell and reproduce only inside it. It consists of genetic material (instructions in the form of DNA or RNA) in a protective shell (protein capsule). Outside a cell, the virus is inactive, like a seed without soil. When it penetrates a cell, it forces it to produce new viruses, which then exit and infect other cells.

A virus is an obligate intracellular parasite, representing a nucleoprotein structure. Unlike cellular organisms, viruses lack ribosomes and energy metabolism systems. Its life cycle includes adsorption on the cell surface, penetration (endocytosis or membrane fusion), uncoating (release of the genome), gene expression (transcription and translation using the cellular machinery), genome replication, assembly of virions, and release from the cell. Classification of viruses is based on the type of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded), capsid symmetry, and presence of an envelope. Some viruses (retroviruses) use reverse transcription for integration into the host genome. Viruses infect all forms of life, from bacteria (bacteriophages) to humans.

History

The history of virus discovery begins with the work of Dmitry Ivanovsky (1892), who demonstrated that the causative agent of tobacco mosaic passes through bacterial filters. Later, Martinus Beijerinck (1898) confirmed this and introduced the term 'virus' (liquid living poison). In 1935, Wendell Stanley crystallized the tobacco mosaic virus, proving its particulate nature. With the development of electron microscopy and molecular biology, the structure and replication mechanisms of viruses were established.

How it works

Viral parasitism at the molecular level is based on molecular mimicry and the use of cellular receptors. For example, the influenza virus binds via hemagglutinin to sialic acid on the cell surface, enters the endosome, where low pH activates membrane fusion. RNA-genome viruses replicate using RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, which they either encode or carry with them. Retroviruses (HIV) use reverse transcriptase to synthesize DNA from an RNA template, which then integrates into the host chromosome via integrase. Virus release can occur by lysis (cell destruction) or budding, when the virus captures a portion of the cell membrane as an envelope.
