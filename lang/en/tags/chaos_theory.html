chaos theory

A pendulum swings steadily, but push it a little harder and the trajectory becomes unpredictable. Chaos hides in simple systems.

In practice: Weather forecasts are inaccurate beyond two weeks due to the chaotic nature of the atmosphere, and encryption algorithms use chaos to protect data.
Chaos is not synonymous with disorder. It is orderly unpredictability. Look at the smoke rising from a cigarette: at first it flows in a smooth stream, but higher up it suddenly turns into billowing curls. The transition from laminar to turbulent flow is a typical scenario for the birth of chaos. The equations describing such processes are deterministic (containing no randomness), but so sensitive to initial data that the slightest rounding error grows and nullifies any forecast. This property is called exponential divergence of trajectories.

History

Henri Poincaré, studying the motions of three planets, realized that their orbits could be incredibly sensitive to initial conditions. The term 'chaos' was introduced by mathematician James Yorke in 1975, and the famous 'butterfly effect' was formulated by meteorologist Edward Lorenz.

How it works

Fold a sheet of paper in half, then again, and again. After 10 folds you get 1024 layers—that's exponential growth. In chaos, a small uncertainty multiplies just as rapidly, and after a few steps the prediction blurs. Chaos theory is an interdisciplinary field studying the behavior of nonlinear dynamical systems that are extremely sensitive to initial conditions—a phenomenon known as the 'butterfly effect'. Chaotic dynamics are deterministic (described by unambiguous differential or difference equations without random terms), but practically unpredictable over long time intervals due to the exponential divergence of initially close trajectories. A quantitative measure of this unpredictability is provided by Lyapunov exponents. Chaos is often characterized by the presence of strange attractors—complex fractal sets with fractional dimension, on which motion never repeats but remains bounded. So it is with a pendulum: change the initial push by a hair, and instead of regular oscillations we get chaotic rotation.

History

Although the first intuitive insights about the instability of certain motions were expressed by Henri Poincaré as early as the 1890s, modern chaos theory was born in the 1960s–1970s. Edward Lorenz of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology discovered chaos in a simplified model of atmospheric convection in 1963. Mathematician James Yorke and his student Tien-Yien Li introduced the term 'chaos' in 1975. Mitchell Feigenbaum in 1978 discovered universal patterns in the transition to chaos through a period-doubling cascade, linking them to constants named after him.

How it works

The starting point is often the Lorenz system: three ordinary differential equations with two nonlinear terms of the xy and xz type. At certain parameter values (σ=10, ρ=28, β=8/3), the phase trajectories form a chaotic attractor—they endlessly wind around two regions, never intersecting or looping. The evolution of such a system has the property of mixing: any, even tiny, initial cloud of points eventually spreads uniformly over the attractor. Information about the initial state is irretrievably lost, although the equations themselves are reversible. This leads to the practical impossibility of long-term forecasting, despite determinism. Statistical methods, such as constructing return maps (Poincaré sections) and calculating the power spectrum, help distinguish chaos from periodic regimes and noise.
