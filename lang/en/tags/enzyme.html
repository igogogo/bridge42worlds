enzyme

Enzymes are tiny molecular machines inside cells that speed up chemical reactions by millions of times. Without them, life would stop: digestion, breathing, DNA copying—all depend on enzymes.

In practice: Enzymes are used in laundry detergents to remove stains at low temperatures, in cheese and bread production, and in medicines to dissolve blood clots.
Enzymes are biological catalysts, mostly of protein nature, that accelerate biochemical reactions by lowering the activation energy (the energy barrier that must be overcome to start a reaction). They have high substrate specificity and work under mild conditions (temperature, pressure, pH) typical of living systems. Enzyme activity is regulated by inhibitors and activators.

History

The first enzyme—diastase (amylase)—was isolated from malt by French chemist Anselme Payen in 1833. The term 'enzyme' was proposed by Wilhelm Kühne in 1878.

How it works

An enzyme acts like a skilled assembler: it captures a molecule, weakens a specific bond in it, and that molecule breaks apart or joins with another, while the enzyme itself remains unchanged and ready to help again.

💡 The enzyme catalase, found in our liver, breaks down hydrogen peroxide at a rate that, if working at full power, could fill more than half a million liters of water with bubbles in one second.
An enzyme is a protein (special molecule) that works like a key in a lock. Each enzyme fits only its specific substance (substrate) and helps it quickly turn into something else. For example, the enzyme in saliva (amylase) begins breaking down starch into sugars right in the mouth.

Enzymes are globular proteins (except for some RNA catalysts—ribozymes) that function as biocatalysts with exceptional efficiency and selectivity. Their catalytic activity is due to the presence of an active site—a unique three-dimensional structure complementary to the transition state of the substrate. Many enzymes require cofactors (metal ions) or coenzymes (vitamin derivatives). The kinetics of enzymatic reactions is described by the Michaelis-Menten model.

History

The history of enzyme study began with work on fermentation: Louis Pasteur in the 1850s believed enzymes were inseparable from living cells, while Eduard Buchner in 1897 proved the possibility of cell-free fermentation (Nobel Prize 1907). James Sumner in 1926 first crystallized an enzyme (urease) and proved its protein nature. In the 1960s, the three-dimensional structures of the first enzymes were determined using X-ray crystallography.

How it works

The enzyme binds the substrate in the active site, forming an enzyme-substrate complex, stabilizes the transition state, lowering the activation energy. After the reaction, the product is released, and the enzyme returns to its original state. The Michaelis-Menten kinetic equation describes the dependence of velocity on substrate concentration.

v = \frac{V_{\text{max}} [S]}{K_m + [S]}
where v is the initial reaction rate, V_max is the maximum rate, [S] is the substrate concentration, K_m is the Michaelis constant (equal to the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of V_max).

Open problems & fun facts

Prediction of catalytic activity and substrate specificity from amino acid sequence de novo.; Design of enzymes with desired properties (protein engineering) for industry and medicine.; Full understanding of the mechanisms of enzyme regulation in the cellular context (allostery, post-translational modifications).; Creation of efficient artificial enzymes (synzymes) based on non-protein scaffolds.
