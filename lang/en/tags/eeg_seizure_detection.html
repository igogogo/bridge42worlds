EEG seizure detection

Just as a lightning detector catches flashes in the sky, algorithms search for epileptic bursts in the brain's electrical signals.

In practice: Helps doctors detect epilepsy faster by analyzing long EEG recordings; used in wearable devices for seizure prediction.
Electroencephalogram (EEG) records the total electrical activity of brain neurons using electrodes on the scalp. An epileptic seizure manifests as abnormal synchronous discharges, which on EEG look like sharp bursts (spikes) and slow waves. Detection algorithms analyze the multichannel signal, extracting characteristic patterns whose frequency and shape differ from background activity.

History

The first automatic detectors appeared in the 1970s; over time they became smarter thanks to machine learning (the computer learns from examples).

How it works

The program looks at the EEG recording, measures wave height and frequency. If it sees sharp jumps, it marks them as a possible seizure. Modern methods also consider hidden patterns that a human might miss.

💡 Some algorithms can predict a seizure minutes before it starts by analyzing gradual changes in EEG that are invisible to the naked eye.
EEG (electroencephalogram) is a recording of brain electrical activity, looking like many wavy lines. During an epileptic seizure, these lines change sharply, becoming 'sharp' like mountain peaks. Seizure detection is a computer program that, like a trained eye, finds such peaks among ordinary waves.

EEG seizure detection is automated recognition in a multichannel time series of electrographic patterns characteristic of epileptic activity: spikes, sharp waves, spike-wave complexes. Modern approaches include classical machine learning methods based on extracted features (power in frequency bands, entropy, wavelet coefficients), as well as deep neural networks (convolutional, recurrent) trained directly on raw data. The task is complicated by high signal variability between patients and artifacts (movements, muscle activity).

History

The first automatic seizure detectors appeared in the 1970s and were based on simple threshold rules for amplitude and duration. In the 1990s, frequency analysis methods and neural networks began to be used. With the growth of computing power and the availability of large labeled databases (e.g., the Temple University Hospital project) in the 2010s, there was a leap to deep learning, allowing performance on par with expert clinicians.

How it works

The system receives a multichannel EEG signal (usually 16-64 leads), digitized at 200-500 Hz. Preprocessing algorithms remove artifacts (filtering, independent component analysis). Then either predefined features are extracted (e.g., power spectral density in delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma bands, fractal dimension, synchronization), or the signal is directly fed into a neural network. The classifier outputs the probability of a seizure for a sliding window (typically 2-10 seconds). At the postprocessing stage, smoothing and thresholding are applied to eliminate false positives. Some systems use transfer learning to adapt to a specific patient.

Open problems & fun facts

High inter-patient variability of seizure patterns; Lack of labeled data due to the labor-intensive expert annotation; Variety of artifacts masking true activity; Need for real-time operation for warning devices
