fractal

The coastline of an island becomes infinitely long if measured with tiny rulers. This is not a trick — many natural forms are structured this way: mountains, clouds, blood vessels.

In practice: Fractal antennas in mobile phones receive signals over a wide frequency range, and computer graphics create realistic landscapes.
A fractal is a set with a fractional dimension and self-similarity at all scales. For example, the Koch snowflake is constructed by replacing each line segment with a broken line, and after infinite steps its length is infinite but its area is finite. The fractal dimension quantitatively describes how densely an object fills space.

History

The term was coined by Benoit Mandelbrot in 1975. He noticed that the length of a country's borders depends on the map's precision — the more precise, the longer.

How it works

When you zoom into a fractal, it remains equally detailed. In nature, this arises from simple repetition rules: for instance, a river channel branches, and each branch resembles the entire river network.

💡 Human lungs pack about 100 m² of surface area into a volume of a few liters thanks to the fractal structure of the bronchial tree.
A fractal is a self-similar structure: each part, when scaled down, repeats the whole. Much like a fern frond, where each tiny branch looks like the entire leaf. Instead of ordinary length, fractals are assigned a fractional dimension (a number like 1.5 — more than a line, less than a plane).

A fractal is a geometric figure with nontrivial structure at all scales and usually has a fractional Hausdorff–Besicovitch dimension. The rigorous definition includes the property of self-similarity (exact, approximate, or statistical). Many natural objects — coastlines, clouds, trees, blood vessel networks — exhibit fractal properties over a limited range of scales. Mathematically, fractals are often generated by iterating simple mappings; for example, the Mandelbrot set is defined by the recurrence relation z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c.

History

The term 'fractal' (from Latin fractus, meaning 'broken') was coined by Benoit Mandelbrot in 1975, but its origins lie in the work of Pierre Fatou and Gaston Julia (early 20th century), who studied iterations of complex mappings. Lewis Fry Richardson noticed in 1961 that the length of country borders increases as the measurement standard becomes smaller. Mandelbrot generalized these ideas, describing the fractal geometry of nature in his 1982 book.

How it works

The fractal structure arises from the recursive application of a simple rule. For example, the Koch curve: each straight segment is replaced by four segments forming a protrusion. After an infinite number of steps, the length of the curve grows without bound, and its dimension is log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.26. The fractal dimension d is defined by the power law N(ε) ∝ ε^{−d}, where N(ε) is the minimum number of balls of radius ε needed to cover the object (box-counting dimension).

d = \frac{\log N}{\log s}
d — fractal dimension; N — number of identical parts into which the object is divided when the linear size is reduced by a factor of s (s — scaling coefficient)
z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c
z_n — complex number at step n (starting with z_0 = 0); c — complex parameter corresponding to a point on the complex plane
