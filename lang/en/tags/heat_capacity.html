heat capacity

A cast iron skillet heats slowly on the flame and cools down for a long time. An aluminum one flares up with heat almost instantly. Heat capacity is a measure of how stubbornly a substance resists heating.

In practice: The high heat capacity of water moderates the climate: oceans accumulate daytime heat and release it at night. In engineering, the low heat capacity of copper allows radiators to quickly dissipate heat.
Heat capacity C defines the relationship between the heat absorbed ΔQ and the temperature change ΔT: C = ΔQ/ΔT. Specific heat capacity c is normalized per kilogram of substance. Gases, liquids, and solids differ by orders of magnitude. Monatomic gas (argon) has a molar heat capacity of about 12.5 J/(mol·K), diatomic oxygen — 20.8 J/(mol·K), and crystals — approximately 25 J/(mol·K) (Dulong–Petit law). Water breaks records: 4.2 kJ/(kg·K), twice as high as one might expect — colossal energy goes into overcoming hydrogen bonds between molecules. That is precisely why tea cools in a quarter of an hour, while milk — in five minutes.

History

In the 18th century, Scottish chemist Joseph Black first separated the concepts of 'heat' and 'temperature'. Observing how differently iron and water heat up, he introduced the term 'heat capacity'. Later, James Joule and William Thomson proved: heat is the motion of microscopic particles.

How it works

Heating makes atoms tremble, rotate, or rush around faster. The more ways microscopic particles have to move (degrees of freedom), the more heat is required to set this ‘dance’ in motion, and the higher the heat capacity. Water is complex: its molecules not only move but also break the tenacious hydrogen bonds — hence its record appetite for heat.

💡 If the oceans had the heat capacity of sand, at night the planet would freeze down to minus one hundred degrees, and during the day it would heat up to plus one hundred.
Heat capacity is the amount of heat that must be transferred to a body to raise its temperature by one degree. A sponge with fine pores soaks up a lot of water, while a dense stone — only a drop. Heat capacity is the same sponge, but for heat. Water is 'spongy': it takes a huge amount of energy to shift its degree. Iron is 'stony': it heats up quickly. A cast iron skillet full of soup stores heat for tens of minutes, but at night it still warms the cooling house for a long time. That is why the climate of coastal countries is mild — water, like a giant battery, smooths out temperature jumps.

Heat capacity is a thermodynamic response function characterizing the change in internal energy U (or enthalpy H) with temperature: C_V = (∂U/∂T)_V, C_p = (∂H/∂T)_p. For an ideal gas, C_p - C_V = R. Quantum theory of solids (Debye model) predicts C_V ∼ T³ for T ≪ θ_D and approaching the classical limit of 3R at high T. In real crystals, anharmonicities must be taken into account; in metals, the electronic contribution C_el = γT. During second-order phase transitions, heat capacity experiences a jump or logarithmic divergence. At the liquid–gas critical point, an anomalous increase in C_V is observed. Experimental methods: differential scanning calorimetry, relaxation method, adiabatic calorimetry.

History

Joseph Black in the 1760s, studying the heat of fusion of ice, noticed that different bodies require different amounts of 'fire' for the same heating, and called this 'capacity for heat'. Dulong and Petit in 1819 formulated the empirical law of constant atomic heat capacity (~6 cal/(mol·K)). Ludwig Boltzmann in 1871 gave the first statistical explanation via equipartition of energy among degrees of freedom. Albert Einstein in 1907 applied the quantum hypothesis to atomic vibrations in a crystal, and Peter Debye in 1912 refined the theory by accounting for the full spectrum of phonon modes. Today, heat capacity measurements are a standard tool for investigating phase transitions, superconductivity, and topological materials.

How it works

Statistical mechanics connects heat capacity with fluctuations of internal energy: C_V = (⟨U²⟩ - ⟨U⟩²)/(k_B T²). In a classical ideal gas, C_V = (f/2)R, where f is the number of quadratic degrees of freedom. Debye model: C_V = 9R (T/θ_D)³ ∫_0^{θ_D/T} (x⁴ e^x)/(e^x-1)² dx. For metals, the contribution of the degenerate electron gas is significant: C_el = (π²/2)(k_B²/g(ε_F)) T, which at low temperatures becomes C_V = γT + βT³ — a formula used to determine the density of states at the Fermi level and the Debye temperature. Magnetic materials exhibit heat capacity peaks at magnetic transitions (λ-anomalies). In glasses and amorphous solids, an excess low-temperature heat capacity is observed due to tunneling two-level systems. The heat capacity of a black hole, computed by Bekenstein and Hawking, is negative (in the sense of stability).

C — heat capacity (J/K), ΔQ — amount of heat received by the body (J), ΔT — temperature change (K)
C_V = \frac{f}{2} R
C_V — molar heat capacity (J/(mol·K)), f — number of degrees of freedom of the molecule, R — universal gas constant (≈ 8.31 J/(mol·K))
