quasiparticle

In a football stadium, a wave of standing fans circles the bowl. No one runs a lap — only the pattern moves. Similarly, in a solid, collective atomic vibrations give rise to particle-like energy clumps.

In practice: Quasiparticles explain semiconductor conductivity and superconductivity: without the concept of holes — empty places that behave like positive particles — no transistor would work.
A quasiparticle is an elementary excitation of a condensed medium (solid, liquid) that possesses particle-like properties: energy, momentum, angular momentum, charge. Examples: phonons (sound quanta), magnons (spin waves), excitons (bound electron-hole pair). Quasiparticles do not exist outside the medium — they are a collective mode. Landau proposed the concept of a Fermi liquid, where fermions turn into weakly interacting quasiparticles with renormalized mass.

History

The idea was introduced by Soviet physicist Lev Landau in 1941 while describing liquid helium. He realized that complex collective motions can be simplified to particle-like excitations.

How it works

In a solid, particles continuously interact. Instead of tracking each one, physicists replace the system with a set of almost independent quasiparticles that carry energy, momentum, and even charge. This is similar to describing a crowd by density and flow velocity, rather than the movements of individual people.

💡 Without considering hole quasiparticles, it's impossible to understand how solar cells work: it's the movement of holes that creates the current.
A quasiparticle is not an independent particle like an electron, but a coherent excitation of many interacting particles. For example, when a sound wave runs through a crystal, its quantum is called a phonon. A phonon is like a particle of sound. Another example is a hole in a semiconductor: the absence of an electron behaves like a positive charge.

A quasiparticle is a quantum of collective excitation of a many-body system. In the formalism of second quantization, creation and annihilation operators describe the generation and absorption of such excitations. They are classified by statistics: fermions (electronic quasiparticles in metals) obey the Pauli principle; Bose quasiparticles (phonons, plasmons) do not. Properties (effective mass, charge, dispersion) are determined by the medium. The concept is critically important for solid-state theory, superfluidity, and superconductivity — Cooper pairs in BCS theory form bosonic quasiparticles responsible for the superconducting current.

History

Lev Landau introduced the term in 1941 while constructing the theory of superfluidity of helium-II. In 1956, Landau generalized the idea to Fermi liquids. Independently, similar approaches were developed by Eugene Wigner for crystals. Experimental confirmation came through measurements of effective mass in metals by cyclotron resonance and neutron spectroscopy of phonons.

How it works

At low energies, the Hamiltonian of a many-particle system transforms into a sum of independent quasiparticle modes: H = Σ ε_k γ_k^† γ_k, where γ_k are creation/annihilation operators of quasiparticles with momentum k. The interaction of the original particles manifests in the renormalization of the spectrum ε_k and residual weak interaction between quasiparticles. In metals, the lifetime of an electronic quasiparticle grows as 1/T² as temperature decreases, and the energy obeys the Pauli principle: occupancy does not exceed 1.
