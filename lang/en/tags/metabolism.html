metabolism

1

Just as a stove burns wood for heat, your body 'burns' food to live and move.

In practice: Metabolism determines how many calories you burn at rest — this is the foundation of diets and workouts.
Metabolism combines two streams of reactions: catabolism (breakdown of complex substances with energy release) and anabolism (synthesis of compounds necessary for the body with energy expenditure). Basal metabolism — the minimum energy expenditure to sustain life — accounts for about 60–75% of daily calorie consumption. Metabolic rate depends on age, gender, muscle mass, thyroid hormones, and genetics.

History

The concept of 'metabolism' was introduced in the 19th century by the German physiologist Theodor Schwann, but the foundations were laid by alchemists observing fermentation. Later, scientists discovered that enzymes are involved in all reactions.

How it works

After eating, digestion breaks down proteins, fats, and carbohydrates into small molecules. Blood carries them to cells, where enzymes speed up reactions: some break down molecules to release energy, others create new substances for growth and repair.

💡 The hummingbird has one of the fastest metabolisms among vertebrates: its heart beats up to 1,200 times per minute, and it must eat every 10–15 minutes or it will die of exhaustion.
Metabolism is all the chemical transformations in the body that turn food into energy and building blocks. Your cell is a tiny factory: some workers (enzymes — protein catalysts) break down a sandwich into bricks, others assemble muscles from them or send fuel to the 'stoves' (mitochondria — the cell's power plants) so you can run and think.

Metabolism is a set of enzymatic reactions forming branched metabolic pathways. Glucose catabolism starts with glycolysis, continues in the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, where energy is stored as ATP. Anabolism uses intermediate metabolites to synthesize amino acids, lipids, nucleotides. Regulation occurs through transcriptional control of enzyme expression, allosteric modulation, and hormonal signals (insulin, glucagon, adrenaline). The central element is ATP — the universal energy carrier. Metabolic networks exhibit emergent properties: the flow of substances is redirected depending on the cell's needs, and feedback loops maintain homeostasis.

History

The term 'metabolism' comes from the Greek 'metabole' — change. Study began with the work of Jan Baptist van Helmont (17th century), but systematic research emerged in the 19th century: Theodor Schwann formulated cell theory, and Claude Bernard introduced the concept of homeostasis. In the 20th century, Hans Krebs discovered the citric acid cycle, and Fritz Lipmann described the role of ATP. Modern metabolomics studies thousands of metabolites simultaneously.

How it works

Metabolism is carried out through enzymatic reactions, the rates of which are described by Michaelis-Menten kinetics. Key pathways such as glycolysis and the Krebs cycle act as 'hubs' linking catabolism and anabolism. The electron transport chain in mitochondria creates a proton gradient used by ATP synthase to synthesize ATP — this process is called oxidative phosphorylation. Hormones (insulin, glucagon, adrenaline, thyroxine) coordinate metabolic fluxes through signaling cascades. At the organism level, Kleiber's law relates metabolic rate to body mass.

B = B_0 M^{3/4}
B is the animal's metabolic rate (W), B_0 is a constant depending on the taxonomic group (for mammals about 3.4 W/kg^(3/4)), M is body mass (kg).
