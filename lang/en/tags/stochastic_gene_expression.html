stochastic gene expression

1

In each cell, genes are not turned on strictly according to schedule, but with elements of randomness — like raindrops that fall sometimes more often, sometimes less often. This explains why twins with identical DNA are not exact copies.

In practice: Understanding stochastic expression helps explain the resistance of cancer cells to therapy and develop more reliable genetic engineering constructs for biotechnology.
Stochastic gene expression is the variability of mRNA and protein levels in genetically identical cells under the same conditions, caused by random molecular events. The main sources of noise: intrinsic (biochemical fluctuations during transcription and translation) and extrinsic (differences in the number of ribosomes, polymerases, growth factors between cells). This phenomenon underlies cellular heterogeneity and has evolutionary significance, allowing populations to survive in a changing environment.

History

In the 1950s, scientists noticed that seemingly identical bacteria reacted differently to lactose: some produced the enzyme, others did not. But only in the 2000s, with the advent of microscopes that allowed looking at individual cells, it became clear how important this randomness is.

How it works

DNA is a library of recipes, expression is cooking a dish. The activator molecule (the cook) looks for the right recipe. But there are few molecules and they move chaotically (Brownian motion), so the waiting time and the number of proteins produced vary. This results in 'noise' in the cellular kitchen.

💡 In the same pancreatic tissue, different cells can produce insulin with a hundredfold difference, even with identical glucose stimulation.
Stochastic gene expression is a random, unpredictable process of turning genes on and off in the cell. There are thousands of genes in a cell, each turning on by itself, like an independent faucet, sometimes a little more, sometimes less, even if conditions are identical. Because of this, the amounts of proteins (gene products) in different cells of the same organism differ.

Stochastic gene expression is a fundamental property of gene regulation, related to the discrete nature of molecular events and low copy numbers of key components (genes, mRNA, transcription factors). Mathematically described by the chemical master equation or stochastic differential equations. Noise is quantitatively characterized by the coefficient of variation (CV) or the Fano factor. Intrinsic noise (inherent to the genetic circuit itself) and extrinsic noise (due to fluctuations in the cellular environment) are distinguished. The two-state model (gene active/inactive) is often used to describe transcriptional bursts. Stochasticity plays a role in cellular decision-making (lambda phage, B. subtilis competence), differentiation, and therapy resistance.

History

Classical observations of phenotypic heterogeneity in isogenic populations (e.g., fluctuations of β-galactosidase in E. coli, Novick and Weiner, 1957) were long considered an artifact. In the 1990s, with the development of single-cell fluorescence microscopy (e.g., the work of Elowitz and Leibler, 2000), it became possible to quantitatively study noise. Theoretical foundations were laid by Delbrück (1940), and later by Berg (1978), McAdams, Arkin (1997). Modern high-throughput methods (single-cell sequencing) confirm the ubiquity of stochastic expression.

How it works

Transcription is initiated by the binding of RNA polymerase and transcription factors to the promoter, a stochastic process due to the low probability of molecular collisions in the cell volume. After initiation, mRNA synthesis occurs in bursts, and degradation of mRNA and proteins is exponential, creating Poisson noise. Negative and positive feedback loops in regulatory networks can suppress or amplify noise. Evolutionarily selected circuits—for example, bistable switches—use noise for random cell fate decisions.
