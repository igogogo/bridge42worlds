horizontal gene transfer

Genes can travel not only through inheritance but also directly between different organisms, like files via Bluetooth. This is how bacteria instantly exchange antibiotic resistance.

In practice: Explains the rapid spread of antibiotic resistance among bacteria and is used in genetic engineering to create GMOs.
Horizontal gene transfer is a mechanism by which genetic information moves between organisms not through sexual reproduction or inheritance from parents. It is especially characteristic of prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea), but also influences the evolution of eukaryotes. Unlike slow mutations, horizontal transfer allows organisms to acquire ready-made genes, enabling rapid adaptation.

History

This was first noticed in the 1920s when scientists saw that different strains of pneumococci could exchange properties. Later, in the 1940s, Oswald Avery and his colleagues showed that the transfer occurs through DNA.

How it works

The donor organism releases a piece of DNA (the carrier of instructions) into the environment, or passes it through contact, or uses viruses as couriers. The recipient picks up this piece and inserts it into its own DNA, acquiring new abilities.

💡 The human genome contains genes obtained from viruses and bacteria millions of years ago. About 8% of our DNA consists of remnants of ancient viruses that once integrated into the genome of our ancestors.
Usually children get genes from their parents. But some organisms, such as bacteria, have a way to exchange genes directly with each other, even if they are not relatives. This is similar to how people share recipes: one baker learned a new baking method and passed it to a colleague, even though they are not from the same family. This way bacteria can quickly learn new tricks — for example, not to fear antibiotics (substances that kill bacteria).

Lateral (horizontal) gene transfer (LGT or HGT) is the process of transferring genetic material between cells of different taxonomic groups, bypassing reproductive inheritance. The main mechanisms in prokaryotes: transformation (uptake of free DNA from the environment), conjugation (direct contact with transfer of plasmids through pili), and transduction (transfer by bacteriophages). In eukaryotes, it is less studied but has been proven in a number of protists, fungi, plants, and even animals. LGT plays a key role in the spread of virulence factors, metabolic pathways, and resistance to antimicrobial agents, as well as in the evolution of ancient eukaryotes by capturing genes from symbiotic bacteria. Modern phylogenomic methods allow detecting traces of such transfers based on discrepancies between phylogenetic trees of individual genes and species.

History

The concept emerged in the early 20th century. Frederick Griffith in 1928 described the transformation of pneumococci but did not know about DNA. Later, in 1944, the experiment by Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty proved that the transforming agent is DNA. The study of LGT intensified with the development of genomics, when it became clear that many bacteria have mosaic genomes with foreign regions.

How it works

At the molecular level, LGT includes several stages: exit of DNA from the donor (active secretion, cell lysis), existence of extracellular DNA (often in complex with proteins), penetration into the recipient (through membrane channels or receptors), and integration into the genome by homologous recombination or via site-specific recombinases. Many mobile genetic elements, such as transposons and integrons, play an intermediary role. The frequency and success of transfer depend on phylogenetic distance, ecological niche, and the restriction-modification systems of the host cell.

Open problems & fun facts

Prevalence and evolutionary significance of LGT in eukaryotes, especially in multicellular organisms.; Mechanisms of control and regulation of horizontal transfer in natural ecosystems.; Quantitative assessment of the contribution of LGT to adaptation and speciation.; Development of methods to accurately distinguish traces of ancient LGT from artifacts of phylogenetic reconstruction.
