gene expression noise

1

Random fluctuations of protein levels in identical cells — like interference in a radio receiver or uneven sprinkling of salt.

In practice: Explains why bacteria survive antibiotics and why tumors become resistant to therapy; used to create stable synthetic genetic circuits.
Gene expression noise — stochastic (probabilistic) fluctuations in mRNA and protein concentrations, caused by the small number of molecules and the random nature of biochemical reactions. Intrinsic noise — the inherent variability of transcription and translation processes due to the discrete nature of chemical events — and extrinsic noise — fluctuations in the numbers of transcription factors, ribosomes, and metabolites common to many genes in the cell — are distinguished. Intrinsic noise is usually measured as the squared coefficient of variation (σ²/μ²), and it is inversely proportional to the average number of molecules.

History

Random fluctuations in biochemistry were known as early as the 1950s, but the importance of noise for cell fate was realized only in the 2000s, when methods for tracking individual cells emerged. Key experiments: synchronized bacterial cultures with fluorescent proteins showed that noise can predetermine survival under stress.

How it works

Noise arises at every step: first, DNA unstably unwinds, and the enzyme (RNA polymerase) attaches randomly — transcription proceeds in bursts. Then, RNA molecules are short-lived and can degrade before protein synthesis. Protein synthesis (translation) itself is also bursty. The sum of these random events creates noise.

💡 Bacteria use noise as a strategy: at any time, a few cells in a colony randomly become resistant to an antibiotic, even without encountering it — thus the population hedges against extinction.
Even in genetically identical cells in the same environment, the amount of the same protein can vary by orders of magnitude. These random fluctuations are called gene expression noise. The cause: within the tiny volumes of a cell, DNA, RNA, and protein molecules collide and react chaotically, as if you are trying to pick up exactly one grain of sugar with two fingers.

Gene expression noise is a fundamental property of genetic systems, determined by the stochastic kinetics of rare molecular events. Mathematically modeled by the chemical Langevin equation or master equation formalism. Intrinsic noise for an unregulated gene is proportional to 1/(average mRNA number), leading to Poisson statistics of products. However, due to regulatory loops and promoter architecture, distributions can deviate (super-Poissonian or sub-Poissonian). Extrinsic noise is often described by small fluctuation expansion (method of moment equations) or through correlation analysis with reporters (dual histograms). Noise plays a dual role: on one hand, it limits the precision of genetic programs; on the other, it creates phenotypic heterogeneity necessary for evolution and differentiation, for example, in the transition of stem cells to specialized ones.

History

The concept of biochemical noise dates back to the work of Max Delbrück (1940s) on mutagenesis. In 1957, Aaron Novick and Milton Weiner experimentally recorded fluctuations of β-galactosidase in E. coli. However, wide recognition came after 2002 with the publication of Elowitz's paper, where using two fluorescent reporters (CFP and YFP) under identical promoters, intrinsic and extrinsic noise were quantitatively separated. Later, studies by Swain and Thatuv (2005–2010) showed the relationship between noise, gene network architecture, and evolutionary fitness. In the 2010s, super-resolution microscopy allowed visualization of individual transcripts in living cells, confirming the bursty nature of transcription (transcriptional bursts).

How it works

Noise is generated at all stages of expression: (1) transcription initiation — the promoter randomly switches between active and inactive states due to local chromatin rearrangements and dissociation of activators (bursts); (2) mRNA maturation and degradation — lifetime is determined by stochastic interactions with RNases; (3) translation initiation depends on ribosome collision with mRNA, which is a rare event in the cell volume; (4) protein secretion and degradation are also variable. Combinatorially, these processes amplify variation, and feedback loops (e.g., negative autoregulation) can reduce noise. In eukaryotes, noise is modulated by epigenetic factors and compartmentalization.

\eta^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu^2}
\eta^2 — expression noise, dimensionless quantity; \sigma^2 — variance of the number of protein molecules in a cell; \mu — mean number of protein molecules in a cell.

Open problems & fun facts

How do cells maintain precise levels of vital proteins despite high internal noise?; Can we control noise to reprogram cell states (e.g., turn cancer cells into benign ones)?; What is the relationship between expression noise and epigenetic heterogeneity leading to drug resistance?
