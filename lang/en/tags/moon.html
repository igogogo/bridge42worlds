Moon

The nearest celestial body, the eternal satellite of Earth, which with its gravity raises the oceans and illuminates the night sky with reflected sunlight.

In practice: Stabilizes Earth's rotation axis, causing tides that affect navigation, coastal ecosystems and potentially used for tidal energy.
The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and the fifth largest satellite in the Solar System. It formed about 4.5 billion years ago, likely from debris ejected into orbit after a collision between Earth and a Mars-sized body. Its surface is covered with impact craters, and the dark areas — the lunar maria — are solidified basaltic lava plains. The lack of atmosphere leads to extreme temperature variations: from +127°C during the day to -173°C at night.

History

People have observed the Moon since ancient times. In 1609, Galileo Galilei first pointed a telescope at it and saw mountains and craters. Later, scientists realized that the craters were formed by impacts.

How it works

The Moon is kept near Earth by gravity. It completes a full orbit around Earth in about 27 days. It always faces us with the same side because it rotates on its axis at the same rate. The Moon's gravity causes tides in the oceans.

💡 From Earth, only one side of the Moon is visible due to tidal locking (gravitational synchronization of rotation), but that doesn't mean the far side is always dark — day and night alternate there just like on the visible side. The first images of the far side were taken by the Soviet probe Luna 3 in 1959.

The Moon is a rocky planetary body with a radius of 1737 km, orbiting Earth in an elliptical orbit with an average distance of 384,400 km and eccentricity of 0.0549. Its average density is 3.34 g/cm³, significantly lower than Earth's, indicating a low iron content. The Moon is differentiated into a crust, mantle, and possibly a small liquid outer core. The surface is divided into two main types of terrain: light highlands, heavily cratered, and dark maria, filled with basaltic lavas. The composition is similar to Earth's mantle, supporting the giant impact hypothesis. Tidal interaction with Earth slows Earth's rotation and moves the Moon away by about 3.8 cm per year.

History

Ancient civilizations worshipped the Moon and used its phases for calendars. In 1609, Galileo Galilei used a telescope to sketch lunar mountains and craters, disproving the idea of perfect celestial spheres. In 1665, Robert Hooke in 'Micrographia' described lunar craters in detail and suggested their impact origin. The space race began in the 20th century: in 1959, Luna 2 reached the surface, and in 1969, Apollo 11 made the first manned landing. Analysis of returned samples revealed a striking similarity to Earth rocks, strengthening the impact hypothesis. Modern missions like LRO and Chang'e continue to map the Moon and search for water ice in polar craters.

How it works

The motion of the Moon is described by Kepler's laws and the law of universal gravitation. The orbit is perturbed by the Sun and planets, leading to librations that allow us to see slightly more than half the surface. The main feature is tidal locking: due to tidal forces, the Moon always faces Earth with the same side, as its rotation period around its axis equals its orbital period. This results from the dissipation of tidal friction energy. The Moon's gravitational influence on Earth causes tides, which slow Earth's rotation and transfer orbital momentum to the Moon, increasing its distance.
