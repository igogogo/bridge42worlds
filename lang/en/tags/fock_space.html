Fock space

The quantum world does not tolerate a fixed population: particles appear and disappear. Fock space is a universal hotel where a separate floor is prepared for each set of guests.

In practice: Without this concept, one cannot describe how a laser generates a powerful beam of light or how superconductors carry current without loss.
Fock space is an extension of quantum mechanics to systems with a variable number of particles. Unlike ordinary Hilbert space (the arena of quantum states), it is assembled from blocks for 0, 1, 2, and more particles. Like a high-rise building: each floor is a separate universe with a fixed population. Creation and annihilation operators are elevators between floors: they raise or lower the system, changing the number of particles by one. For bosons, the elevators work smoothly; for fermions, they work rigidly (the Pauli exclusion principle forbids two particles from occupying the same seat). In this building of quantum theory, the floors are numbered by particle count, and the operator-elevators work nonstop.

History

The idea was proposed by Soviet physicist Vladimir Fock in 1932 to simplify the description of quantum fields.

How it works

When a particle is born, the system jumps to a higher floor; when one disappears, to a lower floor. This is like an elevator that transports the tower's inhabitants between levels.

💡 In Fock space, the vacuum is not emptiness but a full-fledged state with zero energy, from which one can start and create particles 'out of nothing'.
In conventional physics, the number of particles is constant. In the micro-world, it is different: photons (particles of light) are emitted and absorbed. Fock space is a mathematical tower. Each floor is all possible states of the system with a certain number of particles. The basement is zero particles, the vacuum. Above are floors for one, two, infinitely many particles. Moving between floors is helped by ladder operators: the creation operator adds a particle, the annihilation operator removes one. While for one particle there are more than enough floors, for thousands of particles the tower grows to an unimaginable height — like a skyscraper with a number of floors exceeding the grains of sand in the Sahara. Thus the whole building from basement to infinity describes any possible set of quantum particles.

Fock space is the direct sum of Hilbert spaces with a fixed number of particles. Basis vectors are states with definite occupation numbers (how many particles in each single-particle quantum state). For bosons, occupation numbers are arbitrary; for fermions, only 0 or 1 (Pauli principle). Creation operators \(a^\dagger\) and annihilation operators \(a\) raise or lower occupation numbers, satisfying commutation (bosons) or anticommutation (fermions) relations. In quantum field theory, Fock space is the canonical arena for describing interactions with particle creation and annihilation.

History

Constructed by Vladimir Fock in 1932 to formalize second quantization. The need arose from quantum electrodynamics — it was necessary to describe many-body systems where the number of photons is not conserved. Later generalized to fermions, taking into account the Pauli principle. Paul Dirac made fundamental contributions to the theory of radiation and relativistic quantum mechanics, which is closely related to the Fock space formalism.

How it works

A state with occupation numbers is denoted by \\(|n_1, n_2, \ldots \rangle\\). The creation operator \\(a_i^\dagger\\) increases \\(n_i\\) by 1; the annihilation operator \\(a_i\\) decreases it. For bosons: \\(a_i^\dagger |n_1, n_2, \ldots \rangle = \sqrt{n_i+1} |n_1, \ldots, n_i+1, \ldots \rangle\\); for fermions — with a sign factor and the restriction \\(n_i \le 1\\). The vacuum \\(|0\rangle\\) is annihilated by any \\(a_i\\). Any many-particle state is obtained from the vacuum by repeated application of creation operators. Interactions in quantum field theory are written using these operators. The Pauli principle (for fermions) ensures the antisymmetry of the wave function.

\hat{N} = \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
\hat{a}^\dagger — creation operator, \hat{a} — annihilation operator, \hat{N} — number operator
