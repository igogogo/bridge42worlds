Markov chains

4

A Markov chain is a random path where the next step depends only on the current position, without looking back at the past. Like in a board game where the square determines the next dice roll.

In practice: Text autocomplete on your phone, Google's PageRank search algorithm, and epidemic spread models are all Markov chains.
A Markov chain is a discrete random process with the Markov property: the conditional probability of the future given the present does not depend on the past. It is completely defined by an initial probability vector and a transition probability matrix P(i,j) — the chance of moving from i to j. For example, in the model of a tourist wandering along a pier, each step is random, but the direction depends only on the current spot. Chains can be ergodic (any state can be reached from any other) or absorbing (there are trap states).

History

Andrey Markov, a Russian mathematician, invented these chains in 1906 while studying the alternation of vowels and consonants in Pushkin's poem “Eugene Onegin”. He calculated the transition probabilities from vowel to consonant and back.

How it works

A Markov chain works as a sequence generator following probabilistic rules. Knowing the initial state and the transition table (probability matrix), you can repeatedly 'roll the dice' to determine the next step. After many steps, the chain often 'forgets' the start and settles into a stable distribution — as if a roulette wheel balances itself.

💡 The PageRank algorithm, which made Google the search leader, treats the internet as a giant Markov chain, where pages are states and links are transition probabilities of an imaginary user.
A Markov chain is a sequence of states (points on a diagram), where transitions between them are given by probabilities (numbers on arrows). The key condition: where the system goes next depends only on where it is now — previous steps have no influence. If it's rainy today, sunny tomorrow with a 70% chance — that's a Markov weather model.

A Markov chain is a sequence of random variables {X_n} taking values in a discrete set of states, with the Markov property: P(X_{n+1}=j | X_n=i, X_{n-1}=i_{n-1}, ..., X_0=i_0) = P(X_{n+1}=j | X_n=i) = P_{ij}. If transition probabilities do not depend on n, the chain is homogeneous. Main objects of study: distribution after n steps, stationary distribution π satisfying π = πP, return time, period of a state. Fundamental theorem: for an irreducible, aperiodic, positive recurrent chain, there exists a unique stationary distribution to which distributions from any initial state converge. Just as a frog on lily pads, having forgotten its entire path, after many jumps ends up on each leaf with the same frequency.

History

Markov chains were introduced by A. A. Markov in 1906–1907 as a generalization of the law of large numbers to dependent trials. His motivation arose from a theoretical dispute with P. A. Nekrasov about the applicability of the central limit theorem. Markov analyzed a sequence of 20,000 letters from “Eugene Onegin” and showed that transitions between vowels and consonants are not independent. The theory was later developed by Kolmogorov, Doob, and others.

How it works

The dynamics of a Markov chain are described by powers of the transition matrix P. The distribution after n steps is μ_n = μ_0 P^n. In ergodic chains, as n→∞, μ_n → π regardless of μ_0, where π solves the system πP = π, Σ_i π_i = 1. The convergence rate is determined by the spectral gap (the difference between the unit eigenvalue and the second largest eigenvalue in modulus). For continuous time, continuous-time Markov chains are introduced with an intensity matrix Q. Applications: Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms, hidden Markov models (speech recognition), DNA evolution models.
