genomics

3

The genome is an instruction manual for a living organism, written in each of its cells. Genomics is a way to read this manual from start to finish, like reading an entire book rather than individual sentences.

In practice: Genomics allows predicting hereditary diseases, creating personalized medicines, and tracking the evolution of viruses such as SARS-CoV-2.
Genomics is a field of molecular biology that studies the structure, function, evolution, and mapping of genomes. Unlike genetics, which focuses on individual genes, genomics considers the entire genetic material of an organism as a unified system. Modern high-throughput sequencing methods enable rapid and inexpensive determination of billions of nucleotide sequences.

History

The first complete genome (of a bacteriophage virus) was sequenced in 1977. The human genome was deciphered by 2003 as part of the international Human Genome Project.

How it works

Scientists break DNA into small pieces, read their sequence (sequencing), and assemble them back like a giant puzzle. Then, using computers, they search for 'word-genes' in this text and try to understand their functions.

💡 If you printed the human genome as a book, you would need about 3,000 volumes of 1,000 pages each—and all of that fits into a cell nucleus the size of a thousandth of a millimeter.
Genomics studies the complete set of hereditary information (genome) of a living being. Imagine an organism as a huge library, and genes as individual books. Genomics doesn't just read one book; it reads the entire library at once to understand how they are connected. Terms in parentheses: genome is the complete set of DNA with all instructions; DNA is the molecule that stores the genetic code.

Genomics is an interdisciplinary science at the intersection of biology, computer science, and statistics, dealing with the comprehensive analysis of complete genomes. It includes structural genomics (determining the spatial organization of genes), functional genomics (studying expression and regulation), comparative genomics (comparing genomes of different species), and epigenomics (investigating changes that do not affect the DNA sequence). Key technologies include next-generation sequencing (NGS), microarrays, and mass spectrometry. Genomics has revolutionized medicine, agriculture, and evolutionary biology.

History

The term 'genome' was proposed by Hans Winkler in 1920, but the practical emergence of genomics began in the 1970s with the invention of DNA sequencing methods by Frederick Sanger and Walter Gilbert. In 1990, the Human Genome Project started and was completed in 2003 with the full sequence of the euchromatic portion. Since then, sequencing costs have dropped a millionfold, ushering in the era of personal genomics.

How it works

The process includes sample collection, DNA extraction, fragmentation, sequencing (determining the order of nucleotides), bioinformatic assembly, and annotation (identifying gene locations and regulatory elements). Analysis relies on sequence alignment algorithms, homology searches, and protein function predictions.

Open problems & fun facts

Complete deciphering of the functional significance of non-coding DNA (previously considered 'junk').; Understanding how interactions of thousands of genes determine complex traits (multifactorial nature).; Ethical issues regarding privacy of genetic information and germline editing.; Creating accurate predictive models of phenotype from genotype.
