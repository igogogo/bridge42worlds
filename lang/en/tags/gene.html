Tag

gene

3

A gene is a recipe written in DNA for creating a protein that determines a certain trait of an organism, such as eye color.

In practice: Genetic tests allow predicting predisposition to diseases, and gene therapy is already correcting defective genes to treat hereditary diseases.
A gene is a segment of DNA containing information about the structure of a polypeptide chain (protein) or functional RNA. Each gene has a specific location on a chromosome (a compact packaging of DNA). It consists of exons (coding regions) and introns (non-coding inserts removed during splicing—a cutting process). Gene expression is its transformation into function: first transcription (copying the gene into messenger RNA), then translation (converting the nucleotide sequence into an amino acid chain). Regulatory sequences control when and in which cells a gene is active. Alleles are variants of the same gene that determine, for example, different eye color.

History

The concept of the gene appeared in the late 19th century thanks to Gregor Mendel's experiments with peas, although he did not use the word itself. The term 'gene' was proposed by Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen in 1909. And the deciphering of the DNA structure by Watson and Crick in 1953 showed how genes are physically arranged.

How it works

A gene works like a program. First, a copy is made from it—an RNA molecule (similar to DNA but more mobile). Then RNA goes to ribosomes—the 'assembly machines' of the cell. The ribosome reads the instructions and links amino acids into a chain, creating a protein. This protein then performs its function: it becomes part of a muscle, a hormone, or an enzyme that speeds up chemical reactions.

💡 Genetically, humans and bananas are 60% identical because many basic genes necessary for cell life are highly conserved across different species.
A gene is a section of the DNA molecule (a long chain storing hereditary information). It contains instructions for building a specific protein (the main building and working material of the cell). Proteins make us who we are: they shape facial features, height, hair, and also control all processes in the body. Genes are passed from parents to children—that's why we have common traits with mom and dad. The human body contains about 20–25 thousand genes.

A gene is a section of the DNA molecule that serves as a template for the synthesis of a functional product: a protein or non-coding RNA (tRNA, rRNA, miRNA, etc.). The exact definition of a gene continues to evolve with the development of genomics; it includes not only coding sequences (open reading frames) but also all regulatory elements: promoters, enhancers, silencers, transcription factor binding sites. Eukaryotic genes have a mosaic structure: exons alternate with introns, and alternative splicing allows one gene to encode several different proteins. The human genome contains about 3.1 billion nucleotide pairs, of which only about 1.5% code for proteins, the rest being non-coding DNA performing regulatory functions. Mutations in genes can lead to changes in protein structure or disruption of regulation, which underlies many hereditary diseases.

History

The origin of the concept of the gene is associated with the work of Gregor Mendel (1866), who described discrete hereditary factors. The term 'gene' was introduced by Johannsen in 1909 to denote the unit of heredity. In 1910, Thomas Morgan proved that genes are located on chromosomes. The unraveling of the nature of the gene as a segment of DNA belongs to molecular biology of the 1940s–1950s, culminating in the discovery of the double helix by Watson and Crick in 1953. Erwin Schrödinger in his book 'What is Life?' (1944) predicted that hereditary information should be stored in an 'aperiodic crystal,' which stimulated the search for the structure of DNA.

How it works

The mechanism of gene action involves several stages. Transcription initiation begins with the binding of RNA polymerase to the promoter. With the participation of transcription factors, it unwinds the double helix and synthesizes pre-mRNA complementary to the template DNA strand. Processing includes capping, polyadenylation, and splicing—removal of introns and joining of exons. Mature mRNA is exported to the cytoplasm, where the ribosome, with the help of tRNAs carrying amino acids, carries out translation: the sequence of nucleotide triplets (codons) determines the sequence of amino acids in the protein. Post-translational modifications give the protein its final shape and activity. Regulation of gene expression occurs at all these stages and is critically important for cell differentiation and response to the environment.
