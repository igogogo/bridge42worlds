disjunction effect

2

The disjunction effect is a quirk of our thinking: when a person doesn't know which of two possible events will occur, they often postpone a decision or behave as if both options are unlikely, even if the combined chances are high. It's as if uncertainty paralyzes choice.

In practice: It explains why people don't buy insurance without knowing the exact probability of risks, or why investors wait for clarity even if it means missed profit. It is taken into account in economics, marketing, and political science.
The disjunction effect is a cognitive bias in which people avoid making a definite choice when the outcome depends on one of uncertain, mutually exclusive events. Formally, it is a violation of Leonard Savage's 'sure-thing principle': if in each possible situation the same option is preferred, then it should also be preferred in the absence of information about which situation has occurred. The effect is especially strong under conditions of prospective (future) uncertainty.

History

The effect was discovered by psychologists in the late 20th century while studying decision-making paradoxes. The term 'disjunction effect' was introduced by Amos Tversky and Eldar Shafir in 1992, after they noticed that people violate the 'sure-thing principle' from game theory when the outcome depends on mutually exclusive reasons.

How it works

Normally, a rational person evaluates each event separately. But in a disjunction (an 'either-or' situation), the brain plays it safe: it focuses on the uncertainty and refuses to act, even if any of the specific outcomes would lead to the same decision. It's like a chess game: knowing your opponent's next move, you'll make yours, but if there are two possible moves, you might get confused and do nothing.

💡 In an experiment by Tversky and Shafir, students who knew they had either passed or failed an exam preferred to buy a vacation trip more often than those who had not yet been told the result. Knowledge freed them from choice paralysis.
Imagine you are offered a concert ticket for tomorrow, but you're not sure if it will rain. If you know for sure it will rain, you might decline; if you know for sure it's dry, you'll buy. But if the forecast is uncertain (50/50), you most likely won't buy the ticket, even though there is a chance of good weather. That's how the disjunction effect works: uncertainty between two events makes us act as if both events are worse than each one individually.

The disjunction effect is a phenomenon in behavioral economics and cognitive psychology that describes a systematic violation of the sure-thing principle under uncertainty caused by disjunctive events (mutually exclusive possibilities). If upon the occurrence of event A the agent prefers action X over action Y, and the same upon the occurrence of ¬A, then it is rational to prefer X even without knowledge of the state of A. However, real people often refuse X when state A is unknown, demonstrating 'disjunctive ambivalence'. The effect is related to the Ellsberg paradox and can be explained by ambiguity aversion, emotional factors, or an inability to simulate hypothetical events.

History

The sure-thing principle dates back to Leonard Savage's (1954) work on the foundations of statistics. The disjunction effect was experimentally identified by Amos Tversky and Eldar Shafir in their 1992 paper 'The Disjunction Effect in Choice Under Uncertainty'. They conducted a series of surveys, including the Hawaii problem (vacation after an exam) and the betting problem (gambling after a coin toss). Later, the effect was replicated in neuroeconomic studies, and in the 2000s, quantum-like models of decision-making emerged, where superposition of states explains violations of classical probability laws.

How it works

Standard expected utility theory prescribes acting based on weighted probabilities of outcomes. In a disjunction, the agent faces an event space {A, ¬A}. If under both A and ¬A action X dominates, then in a state of ignorance the choice of X should remain. The observed refusal of X is explained by several mechanisms: the hypothesis of considering unconsidered scenarios (people imagine the worst-case development), emotional anticipation (regret over a decision made when the outcome is unknown), or quantum-like superposition of mental states, where utility evaluation interferes and leads to non-classical choice probabilities.
