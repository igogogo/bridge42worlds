Tag

neutron star merger

Two superdense stellar clumps, each the size of a city, crashed into each other on the galaxy's edge—and ejected clouds of incandescent metal, from which new planets will later form.

In practice: Such mergers are cosmic forges of gold, platinum, and uranium, without which many rare metals would not exist on Earth.
A neutron star merger is a rarest cosmic event where two compact objects lock in a dance, losing orbital energy through gravitational waves, and ultimately merge, forming a black hole or a massive neutron star. The process is accompanied by a short gamma-ray burst and a kilonova—the afterglow from radioactive decay of freshly forged nuclei.

History

The first direct detection of a neutron star merger was in 2017 by the LIGO and Virgo detectors, simultaneously with telescopes that saw its light.

How it works

Imagine two magnets flying toward each other. Their superfluid interiors are torn apart by tidal forces, spilling neutron-rich material that instantly builds nuclei of heavy elements—like gold and platinum.

💡 The 2017 collision produced an amount of gold exceeding the mass of the entire Earth by 10 times—that's where the shine of your wedding ring comes from.
Neutron stars are ultra-dense remnants (mass of the Sun, size of a city) of exploded giant stars. When two such stars, orbiting, lose energy due to the emission of gravitational waves (ripples in spacetime), they spiral together and collide. The collision produces a kilonova—an explosion thousands of times brighter than a typical nova, momentarily outshining entire galaxies.

A neutron star merger is the final stage of a binary system's evolution, whose inspiral is accelerated by gravitational wave emission. Upon contact, the ultra-dense (~10^14 g/cm³) objects deform, matter heats to billions of degrees, and the r-process (rapid neutron capture) forges half of the elements heavier than iron in the universe. The merger's outcome is either a supermassive neutron star that subsequently collapses into a black hole, or a direct collapse forming an accretion disk and an ultra-relativistic jet that pierces the shell of ejected material.

History

The first detection of GW170817 on August 17, 2017, jointly by LIGO (USA) and Virgo (Italy) confirmed the nature of short gamma-ray bursts and launched the era of multi-messenger astronomy. The event was localized in the elliptical galaxy NGC 4993, and subsequent observations in optical, X-ray, and radio revealed details of the kilonova. Theoretical foundations were predicted as early as the 1970s (Clark, Early), but only the development of gravitational-wave observatories made direct detection possible.

How it works

The inspiral is described by the gradual loss of energy through gravitational radiation according to general relativity. Tidal forces disrupt the neutron stars, ejecting neutronized material where rapid neutron capture (r-process) builds heavy-element nuclei. The resulting accretion disk, rotating with enormous angular velocity, collimates a relativistic jet, generating a gamma-ray burst. The thermal energy from radioactive decay in the ejected material powers the kilonova.
