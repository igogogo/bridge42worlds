Lorentz Invariance Violation

On all cosmic highways, the same speed limit applies — 299,792,458 meters per second. Lorentz invariance violation is a check whether this signpost cracks on quantum bumps.

In practice: Telescopes capturing gamma-ray bursts look for a delay between photons of different energies. Detecting such a delay would point the way to a theory of quantum gravity.
Lorentz invariance is the pillar of special relativity. It guarantees that the speed of light in a vacuum does not depend on the motion of source or receiver. Lorentz invariance violation (LIV) is a hypothetical deviation from this symmetry, possible at energies close to the Planck scale (10¹⁹ GeV). Such effects are predicted by some approaches to quantum gravity and string theory. LIV would manifest, for example, as a dependence of the speed of light on its frequency: the higher the frequency, the more noticeable the slowdown.

History

The idea has been tested since the 1990s. Satellites and ground-based detectors, such as the Fermi telescope, monitor gamma-ray bursts across the Universe: if the fastest photons arrive later than slower ones — that's a signal of violation.

How it works

If the signpost wobbles, the speed limit depends on energy: harder (high-energy) photons can lag slightly behind softer ones. By measuring the arrival delay of particles from a distant explosion, physicists check whether the signpost is strict.

💡 Gamma-ray bursts — the most powerful explosions in the Universe — serve as natural laboratories: over billions of years of travel, even a tiny speed difference would accumulate into a noticeable delay.

Lorentz invariance violation (LIV) is a general term for hypothetical effects that break the fundamental symmetry of spacetime underlying special relativity. Lorentz invariance asserts the equivalence of all inertial reference frames and the constancy of the speed of light in vacuum. LIV can manifest as modifications to the dispersion relations for elementary particles, the introduction of a preferred reference frame, spacetime anisotropy, and violation of CPT symmetry. Searches for LIV are conducted in high-energy astrophysics, laser interferometry, and atomic spectroscopy.

History

Lorentz invariance is named after Hendrik Lorentz, whose transformations (1904) preserve Maxwell's equations. In Einstein's special relativity (1905), it was elevated to a postulate. The idea of possible violation arose in the 1980s–90s with the development of quantum gravity: V. Kostelecký, R. Mewes, G. Amelino-Camelia proposed models with modified kinematics. Experimental limits were obtained from Fermi telescope observations of gamma-ray bursts, from neutrino data of supernova SN 1987A, and from precision hydrogen spectroscopy. In the 2000s, systematic ground-based searches began using interferometers.

How it works

In the Standard-Model Extension (SME), LIV is parameterized by adding terms to the Lagrangian that violate Lorentz symmetry. For photons, this leads to a modified dispersion relation: E² = p²c² + ξ(E³/M)c² (or with a higher power), where ξ is a dimensionless violation parameter, M is the new physics scale. From this, the group velocity v ≈ c [1 + (3/2)ξ(E/M)]. For ξ>0, high-energy photons travel more slowly. Experimentally, one searches for a time delay Δt between the arrival of photons of different energies from a single source: Δt = ξ (ΔE/M) (L/c), where L is the distance. The absence of delay for gamma-ray burst GRB 090510 gave a limit |ξ| < 10⁻³. LIV is also tested in vacuum birefringence and synchrotron radiation. The principle of constant light speed and Lorentz transformations are tested with high precision.

E^2 = p^2 c^2 + \xi \frac{E^3}{M} c^2
E — photon energy, p — momentum, c — speed of light in vacuum (c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s), ξ — dimensionless LIV parameter, M — violation scale (often the Planck mass M_P ≈ 1.22×10¹⁹ GeV/c²)
\Delta t = \xi \frac{\Delta E}{M} \frac{L}{c}
Δt — delay between photons with energy difference ΔE, L — distance to the source, c — speed of light, ξ and M — model parameters
