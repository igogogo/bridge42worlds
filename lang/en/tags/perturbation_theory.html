perturbation theory

A 19th-century astronomer predicted Neptune's orbit by calculating Uranus' deviations from a circle — thus perturbation theory was born: any complex problem can be solved by adding small corrections to a simple initial calculation.

In practice: Today, satellite orbits, the behavior of atoms in laser light, and the evolution of galactic clusters are calculated this way.
Real physical systems are rarely solvable exactly. Perturbation theory is a recipe for approximate calculations, where the problem is split into an ideal, exactly solvable 'skeleton' and weak complicating factors (perturbations). The answer is sought as a chain of corrections, each successive one smaller than the previous. This approach dominates quantum mechanics, astrophysics, and field theory—anywhere a small parameter exists. For example, in quantum electrodynamics, the interaction of electrons with light is calculated as a series in the fine-structure constant (a number around 1/137).

History

The method appeared in 18th-century celestial mechanics for predicting planetary motion. Later, it was transferred to quantum physics, where it became the main way to calculate particle collisions.

How it works

If a swing is pushed slightly sideways, it retains its main back-and-forth motion, but a ripple is added. Perturbation theory calculates this ripple without touching the basis. Similarly, in an atom: first, electron orbits are calculated without repulsion between them, and then small corrections account for their 'quarrels' over space.

💡 Using perturbation theory, the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron was calculated, and the result matched experiment to the 12th decimal place — as if you measured the distance from the Earth to the Moon with an error equal to the thickness of a hair.
Many physical problems are too complex to solve directly. So scientists take a similar simple problem, solve it exactly, and then step by step add small additions (perturbations) — much like a builder first erects the shell of a building and then hangs windows and doors. This method is called perturbation theory. It works when all deviations are small and allows the answer to be refined without recalculating everything from scratch.

Perturbation theory is a method for the approximate solution of physical problems, based on separating an exactly solvable part H0 and adding a small perturbation V. The total Hamiltonian H = H0 + λV, where λ is a small formal parameter. Solutions are sought as power series in λ for energy levels and states. In quantum mechanics, the zeroth order gives unperturbed quantities, the first order gives the diagonal matrix elements of the perturbation, and higher orders account for transitions between states. In quantum field theory, perturbation corresponds to Feynman series, where each term is represented by a diagram, and the expansion is in the coupling constant.

History

Henri Poincaré in the 1890s developed methods of series in a small parameter for the three-body problem in celestial mechanics. In the 1920s, Paul Dirac formulated time-dependent perturbation theory for calculating quantum transitions. John Archibald Wheeler applied perturbation theory in general relativity and for analyzing the scattering of gravitational waves.

How it works

In stationary non-relativistic perturbation theory, the first-order energy correction is E_n^(1) = ⟨n^(0)|V|n^(0)⟩, where |n^(0)⟩ are the eigenvectors of H0. In second order, E_n^(2) = Σ_{m≠n} |⟨m^(0)|V|n^(0)⟩|^2/(E_n^(0) - E_m^(0)). The series are often asymptotic, so they converge only for sufficiently small perturbation. In quantum electrodynamics, the expansion in α≈1/137 gives excellent convergence; in quantum chromodynamics at low energies, the coupling constant is large, and non-perturbative methods (lattice calculations) are needed. Thus, like an astronomer refining Uranus's orbit correction by correction, the physicist step by step approaches the truth until small additions no longer change the result.

H = H_0 + \lambda V
H is the full Hamiltonian, H_0 is the unperturbed Hamiltonian, V is the perturbation, λ is a small dimensionless parameter.
E_n^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)} | V | n^{(0)} \rangle
E_n^{(1)} is the first-order correction to the energy of the nth level, |n^{(0)}⟩ is an eigenstate of H_0, ⟨|⟩ is the scalar product.

Open problems & fun facts

Non-convergence of series for large coupling constants (strong interactions).; Inclusion of non-perturbative effects (instantons, confinement).; Higher-order corrections in quantum gravity as an effective field theory.
