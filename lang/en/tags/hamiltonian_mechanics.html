Hamiltonian mechanics

The total energy of a system dictates the only possible path for each of its particles — whether it's a planet around a star or the flutter of a butterfly's wing.

In practice: Calculating satellite orbits, modeling quantum computers, synthesizing new materials with desired properties.
The protagonist of Hamiltonian mechanics is the Hamiltonian function, i.e., the total energy of the system expressed in terms of coordinates and momenta. Unlike the Newtonian approach, where force causes acceleration, here energy reigns. A pair of Hamilton's equations (the first linking velocity to momentum, the second linking change in momentum to force) turns mechanics into the geometry of phase space: each state is a point, and evolution is a line on the energy landscape. This perspective clarifies why energy, momentum, and other quantities are conserved.

History

The method was created by Irish mathematician William Hamilton in 1833. He reworked Newton's laws, replacing the concept of force with the universal language of energy.

How it works

Every system has an 'energy passport' — the Hamiltonian function (denoted H). Two simple equations link changes in position and momentum to this function. Solving them allows tracing the entire destiny of the system — from damped oscillations to the expansion of galaxies.

💡 The formalism turned out to be so elegant that it crossed over into quantum mechanics almost unchanged: the Hamiltonian operator became the main tool for describing particles and fields.
Hamiltonian mechanics describes the motion of everything through a single key quantity — total energy (the sum of kinetic and potential energy). Just as a ball rolling through hilly terrain always finds the easiest path, any object — from a pendulum to a star — follows the 'energy landscape.' Knowing this landscape allows predicting where the object will be at any moment.

Each particle moves across an invisible energy landscape, like a droplet on glass. Hamiltonian mechanics turns this image into a rigorous mathematical apparatus. The state of a system is defined by generalized coordinates and canonically conjugate momenta, and the total energy — the Hamiltonian function H(q, p, t) — generates evolution through the equations: dq/dt = ∂H/∂p, dp/dt = −∂H/∂q. These equations define a flow in phase space that preserves its volume (Liouville's theorem) and carries a symplectic structure. The formalism naturally reveals symmetries: each continuous symmetry gives rise to a conservation law (Noether's theorem). It also serves as a bridge to quantum physics: classical Poisson brackets are replaced by quantum commutators. Thus, the simple principle of energy governs both the smooth dance of the planets and the probabilistic quantum world.

History

The idea grew out of geometric optics. William Rowan Hamilton in 1833 applied the optical-mechanical analogy to rewrite Newton's laws in terms of energy. Later, Henri Poincaré used Hamiltonian methods to analyze the stability of the Solar System, laying the foundation for chaos theory. Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of a Hamiltonian system corresponds to a conserved quantity — a result that cemented the foundation of all modern physics.

How it works

The Hamiltonian function H generates a vector field in phase space. The integral curves of this field are the system's trajectories. If H does not depend explicitly on time, energy is conserved. In the quantum picture, the Hamiltonian becomes an operator that determines the evolution of the wave function via the Schrödinger equation. Canonical transformations here become unitary transformations, and Liouville's theorem becomes the unitarity of quantum evolution.

\frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \quad \frac{dp}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}
q — generalized coordinate, p — conjugate momentum, H — Hamiltonian function (total energy of the system), t — time.
\{f, g\} = \sum_i \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i} \frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i} \frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i} \right)
f, g — arbitrary functions on phase space; the sum is taken over all degrees of freedom i.
