Stochastic modeling

14

A world recreated by the rules of chance, like rolling dice to predict the weather, stock market surges, or the evolution of galaxies.

In practice: Predicting the spread of infections, modeling crowd movement, calculating power plant reliability.
A method for studying complex systems by constructing a mathematical model that includes random variables and probabilistic transitions, and then numerically reproducing it many times (Monte Carlo method). Stochastic modeling is indispensable when the analytical solution of equations is too cumbersome or unknown, but the statistical properties of elementary events are available. It is used in physics, biology, finance, and epidemiology for risk and uncertainty assessment.

History

Early ideas of random processes appeared in the 19th century in gambling, then developed rapidly in the 20th century for the needs of physics (Brownian motion — the chaotic jiggling of pollen in water) and economics.

How it works

A computer model is created, which is run over and over with different random parameters (for example, wind speed). Each run is one scenario of the future. After thousands of such simulations, a cloud of possible outcomes is obtained, from which it is clear what is most likely to happen and what is rare.

💡 Physicists already in the mid-20th century modeled the movement of neutrons in a nuclear reactor using random numbers — this is how the Monte Carlo method appeared, named after the casino.
Stochastic modeling is an imitation of reality that takes randomness into account. Unlike exact calculations, it acknowledges that we don't know everything and adds an element of surprise, like flipping a coin in a game. It is used when a phenomenon is too messy for strict formulas.

Construction and simulation of the dynamics of a system described by probabilistic laws, for example, random processes (Markov chains, Wiener process) or stochastic differential equations (SDEs). The goal is to generate an ensemble of trajectories, from which mean values, variances, and probabilities of rare events are estimated. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods allow sampling from complex posterior distributions in Bayesian inference. Agent-based modeling assigns random behavioral rules to a multitude of individual entities, leading to emergent collective effects.

History

The formal foundations were laid in the early 20th century by the works of A. A. Markov, A. Einstein (Brownian motion), and N. Wiener. The Monte Carlo method was invented by Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann in the 1940s for the Manhattan Project. With the growth of computers, stochastic modeling became a standard tool in all sciences, including climatology (ensemble forecasts) and computational biology.

How it works

A probabilistic model is defined: for example, the SDE dX_t = μ(X_t,t)dt + σ(X_t,t)dW_t, where W_t is a Wiener process (a continuous random process with independent normal increments). A numerical scheme (Euler–Maruyama) generates discrete increments by adding Gaussian white noise. By repeating the simulation N times, we obtain N trajectories, from which histograms, confidence intervals, etc. are constructed. The convergence of the method is on the order of 1/√N.

dX_t = \mu(X_t, t) dt + \sigma(X_t, t) dW_t
dX_t is the infinitesimal increment of process X at time t, μ is the drift coefficient (deterministic tendency), σ is the diffusion coefficient (noise amplitude), dW_t is the increment of a Wiener process, having the properties: E[dW_t]=0, E[(dW_t)^2]=dt

Open problems & fun facts

Computational expensiveness for rare events (huge N required); Choosing an adequate stochastic model when data is incomplete; Model verification and validation when the real system is non-stationary; The curse of dimensionality for multidimensional processes
