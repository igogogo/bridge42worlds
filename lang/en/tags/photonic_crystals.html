Tag

photonic crystals

Light darts among tiny towers, but the only way out is to reflect back. In a photonic crystal, rays enter a labyrinth of alternating layers, through which only waves of certain colors can squeeze through.

In practice: Photonic crystals make LEDs brighter and lasers more efficient. They also hide in opals and butterfly wings, creating iridescent colors.
A photonic crystal is a periodic structure with a contrast in refractive index (the ability to refract light). Alternating regions of high and low index create photonic band gaps — ranges of wavelengths that cannot propagate inside the crystal. An electronic analogy: just as band gaps in semiconductors block electrons, here they block photons. The typical lattice period is hundreds of nanometers, to match the wavelength of visible light.

History

In 1987, Eli Yablonovitch and Sajeev John independently proposed creating materials that forbid light from moving in certain directions. Their idea grew out of work on Bragg gratings, where layers of different transparency reflect light selectively.

How it works

Light is a wave. When a wave encounters an obstacle, it partially reflects. In a crystal, there are many such obstacles, and they are arranged strictly periodically. If the distance between them equals the wavelength, all the reflected waves add up in phase and amplify each other. As a result, light cannot pass — a spectral region (the band gap) appears, where the crystal acts as a mirror.

💡 Natural photonic crystals are found in opals and butterfly wings. Their iridescent shimmering is not a pigment, but a play of light on periodic nanostructures.
A photonic crystal is a tiny sponge for light. It consists of many identical cells, resembling a honeycomb. Each cell either transmits light or reflects it. Together they work like a sieve: one color slips through, another bounces back. The distance between cells (the lattice period) is chosen to control only the desired rays. The thickness of the layers is comparable to the wavelength of light — hundredths of the thickness of a hair.

Photonic crystals are artificial or natural media in which the dielectric permittivity is periodically modulated in space with a period comparable to the wavelength of light. Such modulation leads to the appearance of photonic band gaps — spectral regions where the propagation of electromagnetic waves is suppressed. The phenomenon is based on Bragg diffraction: waves reflected from adjacent layers interfere constructively if the path difference is a multiple of the wavelength. In the band gap, the density of photonic states tends to zero, allowing control of spontaneous emission, light localization, and creation of waveguides with sharp bends. One-dimensional (Bragg mirrors), two-dimensional (hole lattices), and three-dimensional (structures like opals or “wells”) photonic crystals are distinguished. A complete three-dimensional band gap for visible light remains a technological challenge.

History

The concept of periodic structures for controlling waves dates back to Lord Rayleigh (1887), but the birth of photonic crystals is dated to 1987. That year, Eli Yablonovitch (USA) published a work on the suppression of spontaneous emission in three-dimensional dielectric lattices, and Sajeev John (Canada) on the localization of light in disordered media. Their ideas quickly grew into an active field: in 1991, the first three-dimensional photonic crystal for the optical range was demonstrated, and by the late 1990s, photonic crystal fibers appeared. Today, developments are applied in optoelectronics, quantum electrodynamics, and sensors.

How it works

Maxwell's equations for a periodic medium lead to an eigenvalue problem analogous to the Schrödinger equation for electrons in a crystal. The role of the potential is played by the periodic dielectric function. Solutions yield dispersion relations ω(k) — photonic bands. In certain directions and frequency ranges, solutions are absent: these are the band gaps. For a complete band gap, it must overlap for all directions and polarizations. The Bragg condition for a one-dimensional crystal: λ = 2d n_eff / m, where d is the period, n_eff is the effective refractive index, and m is an integer. In real structures, numerical methods (plane wave, FDTD) are used to calculate the band structure. The introduction of defects — i.e., breaking the strict periodicity — creates allowed levels within the band gap, enabling the localization of light in microcavities and the formation of waveguides.

\lambda = \frac{2 d n_{\mathrm{eff}}}{m}
λ — wavelength of light in vacuum, d — lattice period, n_eff — effective refractive index of the structure, m — integer diffraction order
