prostate MRI

2

Prostate MRI is like a detailed satellite image of an area, with different modes highlighting suspicious spots like a thermal camera revealing heat leaks.

In practice: It is used for early detection of prostate cancer, biopsy planning, and monitoring disease progression, replacing random tissue sampling with a targeted approach.
Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) of the prostate combines several sequences: T2-weighted images show anatomy, diffusion-weighted images reveal cell density based on water molecule mobility, and dynamic contrast enhancement shows blood supply. Combined analysis allows distinguishing malignant tumors from benign changes with high probability.

History

The first MRI scanners appeared in the 1970s thanks to the work of Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield. The use of MRI for the prostate began in the 1980s, and by the 2000s it became a precise method with the addition of multiple modes.

How it works

The MRI machine creates a strong magnetic field, causing hydrogen atoms in the body to align like soldiers. Then radio waves briefly disrupt this order, and the atoms return back, emitting a signal. Based on the signal characteristics, the computer constructs an image. Different tissues produce different signals, like different musical instruments sound differently. By adding special pulses, suspicious areas can be 'colored'.

💡 Prostate MRI can detect cancer the size of a pea, which is impossible to feel with a finger, and helps avoid unnecessary biopsies in 30% of patients.
Prostate MRI is a way to look inside the gland without incisions. Just as different filters on a camera (such as black-and-white or thermal) show different aspects of the same object, MRI uses several settings (modes) to see tissue density, water movement within it, and blood flow. Tumors often appear different, like a rotten apple among healthy ones.

Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging of the prostate is a non-invasive imaging method based on differences in tissue magnetic properties. The protocol necessarily includes T2-weighted imaging for anatomical detail, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with an apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map to assess cell density, and dynamic contrast enhancement (DCE) with intravenous gadolinium contrast to assess vascularization. MR spectroscopy may also be used. Interpretation is based on the PI-RADS scale, which standardizes the assessment of clinically significant cancer risk. High-field scanners (3 Tesla) are preferable to 1.5 T, providing better resolution. Patient preparation includes bowel cleansing, suppression of peristalsis with antispasmodics, and pre-voiding of the bladder.

History

The roots of MRI go back to 1938, when Isidor Rabi discovered nuclear magnetic resonance. In 1973, Paul Lauterbur obtained the first MR image, and in 1977, Peter Mansfield developed echo-planar imaging, speeding up scanning. For the prostate, the first studies appeared in the 1980s, but it was not until the mid-2000s that the multiparametric approach took shape. In 2012, the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) introduced the standardized PI-RADS reporting system, revised in 2015 and 2019. Today, mpMRI is a key step in prostate cancer diagnosis, reducing the number of unnecessary biopsies and improving the detection of aggressive forms.

How it works

A high-strength magnetic field (1.5 or 3 T) aligns the nuclei of hydrogen atoms. A radiofrequency pulse excites them, after which they relax, emitting signals that are encoded by gradient fields and converted into an image. The T2-weighted sequence reflects differences in transverse relaxation time, showing anatomy: the normal peripheral zone is bright, cancer is dark. Diffusion-weighted imaging at different b-values is sensitive to Brownian motion of water; by calculating ADC, a map is obtained where areas of restricted diffusion (high cell density, typical of cancer) appear dark. Dynamic contrast enhancement tracks contrast uptake and washout, revealing zones of neoangiogenesis — tumors 'light up' in the early phases. Combined assessment using the PI-RADS scale minimizes subjectivity.

Open problems & fun facts

Variability in interpretation between observers (even with PI-RADS not excluded); Standardization of scanning protocols across different machines; Differentiation of indolent cancer from clinically significant cancer; Artifacts from bowel movement and hip joints; High cost and limited availability of high-field scanners
