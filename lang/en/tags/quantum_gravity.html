Tag

quantum gravity

Gravity and the quantum world are like water and oil. Quantum gravity tries to mix them into a unified picture of the universe at the smallest and most energetic scales.

In practice: A successful theory will explain the first moment of the Big Bang and what is inside black holes, possibly leading to technologies that are currently hard to imagine.
The main difficulty: standard methods of quantum field theory fail when applied to general relativity because gravity is non-renormalizable. Approaches: string theory replaces point particles with vibrating strings, naturally including a massless graviton (the quantum of gravity); loop quantum gravity breaks space down into discrete 'spin networks'.

History

Einstein's general theory of relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime, but it doesn't mesh with the quantum theory that rules the world of atoms. In the mid-20th century, physicists realized that at the Planck scale (an incredibly small length) both theories must be taken into account. John Wheeler introduced 'quantum foam' — a seething structure of space.

How it works

Imagine spacetime as an elastic fabric. Quantum uncertainty prevents us from precisely defining its shape at ultrasmall distances. The fabric bubbles and twists, creating a foam-like geometry. All particles and forces except gravity are described by quantum fields, and quantum gravity aims to make spacetime itself such a field.

💡 The Planck length, where quantum gravity effects become dominant, is about 1.6×10⁻³⁵ meters. If a hydrogen atom were enlarged to the size of the observable universe, the Planck length would be the thickness of a human hair.
In the familiar world, gravity seems smooth, but at the smallest distances, space and time may be grainy and chaotic — like foam on a wave crest. Quantum gravity seeks to describe how the very fabric of spacetime obeys quantum rules.

Constructing a consistent quantum theory of gravity remains the most important unsolved problem in theoretical physics. The main candidates: string theory, where the fundamental objects are one-dimensional strings vibrating in higher-dimensional space, with one vibrational mode corresponding to a massless spin-2 particle (the graviton); loop quantum gravity, which directly quantizes geometry using spin networks and spin foams, yielding a discrete spectrum of areas and volumes; asymptotically safe gravity, causal dynamical triangulations, and ideas of emergent gravity, where spacetime is not fundamental. The theory must predict corrections to general relativity at short distances, quantum properties of black holes, and the early universe.

History

After the creation of general relativity (1916), attempts to quantize it began in the 1930s with the work of Rosenfeld, Pauli, and Fierz. In 1955, Wheeler introduced the concept of quantum foam. In 1967, Bryce DeWitt wrote the Wheeler-DeWitt equation. In the 1980s, string theory took shape (Green, Schwarz), and in the 1990s, loop quantum gravity (Rovelli, Smolin). Despite decades of effort, none of the approaches have yet received direct experimental confirmation.

How it works

At the Planck scale, quantum fluctuations of the metric become significant. Perturbative quantum gravity turns out to be non-renormalizable: an infinite number of counterterms is required. String theory solves this problem through the extended nature of objects, which softens ultraviolet divergences. The graviton emerges as a state of a closed string. In loop quantum gravity, the fundamental variables are holonomies of connections and fluxes, leading to discrete quantum geometry. Both approaches predict modified dispersion relations, possible violation of Lorentz invariance, and new effects in black hole physics.
