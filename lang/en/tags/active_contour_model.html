active contour model

1

An active contour is like an elastic band that automatically wraps around an object in an image.

In practice: Segmentation of cell boundaries in microscopy or outlining organs in ultrasound images.
Active contour models (snakes) are a boundary-based segmentation method. A deformable spline, defined parametrically as v(s) = (x(s), y(s)), evolves under the influence of internal forces (elasticity, bending) and external forces (image gradients, intensity). Minimization of total energy (E = ∫ (E_int + E_ext) ds) leads to convergence at object boundaries. Extensions such as geodesic active contours use level sets to handle topology changes.

History

Proposed by Michael Kass, Andrew Witkin, and Demetri Terzopoulos in 1988. Their paper 'Snakes: Active Contour Models' spawned an entire direction in computer vision. It was initially applied to medical images and object tracking in video.

How it works

The contour is defined by a set of points. The computer calculates an energy (a number indicating how well the contour fits). Internal energy keeps the curve smooth, like a spring that resists bending. External energy pulls the curve towards edges, like gravity. The algorithm moves the points downhill on the energy landscape until they reach a minimum.

💡 Snakes work iteratively: they start from an initial guess and can 'get stuck' if the start is far from the true boundary, much like a blind person feeling their way around an object.
To find the outline of a cell under a microscope, a computer can take a rough curve and gradually pull it towards the edges of the cell. This method, called an active contour or 'snake', works like a rubber band that minimizes energy: it strives to be smooth while lying on brightness transitions. The algorithm (a step-by-step procedure) moves the curve until it locks onto the target.

Active contour models iteratively deform an initial curve to minimize an energy functional. Internal energy ensures smoothness and regularity; external energy attracts to high-gradient regions. The Euler–Lagrange equation gives the force balance. It is implemented via gradient descent or dynamic programming. Variants include geometric active contours (based on level sets)—they naturally handle topology changes—and region-based models (Chan–Vese), which use intensity statistics.

History

Kass, Witkin, and Terzopoulos (1988) formulated the snake model. In 1995, Caselles et al. proposed geodesic active contours, linking curve evolution to energy minimization via level sets. Chan and Vese (2001) created a gradient-free region-based model based on the Mumford–Shah functional. Active contours influenced many segmentation methods before the era of deep learning dominance.

How it works

The curve is defined parametrically: v(s) = (x(s), y(s)). Energy: E = ∫₀¹ (α|v'(s)|² + β|v''(s)|²) + E_ext(v(s)) ds. The first term controls tension, the second stiffness. External energy is typically –|∇I|² for edge attraction. The snake is optimized variationally: ∂v/∂t = αv'' – βv'''' – ∇E_ext. Discretization yields a system of equations solved iteratively. Level set methods: the curve is the zero level of a function φ, evolution: ∂φ/∂t = g|∇φ| div(∇φ/|∇φ|) + ∇g·∇φ.

E = \int_0^1 \left( \alpha |v'(s)|^2 + \beta |v''(s)|^2 \right) + E_{\text{ext}}(v(s)) ds
v(s) — parametric curve (s ∈ [0,1]), α — tension coefficient, β — stiffness coefficient, E_ext — external energy (typically a function of the image gradient)

Open problems & fun facts

Sensitivity to initial guess; Inability of parametric snakes to change topology; Convergence to local minima; Difficulties with blurred or broken boundaries
