neural networks

A child, looking at hundreds of pictures of animals, gradually learns to tell a cat from a dog. After hundreds of such examples, they can already name a new animal without error.

In practice: Face recognition in photos, voice assistants, weather forecasting.
Card by card, lion after lion, zebra after zebra — thousands of examples pass through the network. Each layer of artificial neurons extracts increasingly abstract features: first it catches edges and lines, then assembles them into parts of a face, then into a complete image. The final layer gives an answer — much like a child looks at ears, whiskers, and tail to say 'cat'.

History

The idea was attempted back in the 1940s, when two scientists devised a simple electrical circuit imitating a nerve cell. The real breakthrough came in the 2010s, when powerful computers made it possible to train networks on millions of examples.

How it works

A picture broken down into pixels is fed into the network's input. The first layer of neurons catches simple details — edges, brightness changes. The second layer assembles corners and arcs from them. Further layers combine these pieces into more complex shapes: ears, eyes, paws. The last layer decides what animal is in the photo, based on all the features found.

In the child's hand is a stack of cards with pictures of animals. They try to name each one, and an adult nods or corrects. Neural networks (programs that learn from examples) work in the same way. Card by card, small mistakes turn into the ability to recognize even new animals. Inside the program are layers of artificial neurons (simple computational cells). Each neuron processes a number and passes it on, multiplying by its weight (a number indicating the importance of the signal). Over time, all weights are adjusted so that the network gives the correct answer at the output.

The child clutches a stack of cards in their hand. Over the summer, they've gone through thousands of images and now hardly make mistakes. A neural network is a computational model consisting of layers of artificial neurons (nodes) that transform input data (e.g., image pixels) into an output signal (class or number) through a series of weighted sums and nonlinear activation functions. Learning occurs by adjusting the connection weights based on the prediction error using the backpropagation algorithm. And in the end, clutching a stack of cards, the child looks at a new picture — and confidently says 'dog.' Having gone through millions of examples, the network does the same.

History

The concept of an artificial neuron dates back to the work of Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts (1943), who proposed a mathematical model of the neuron. In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt created the perceptron — the first trainable neural network. However, after criticism by Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert in 1969, interest waned. Revival began in the 1980s with the development of multilayer networks and the backpropagation algorithm (David Rumelhart et al.). In the 2000s, the availability of big data and GPU computing led to deep learning.

How it works

The input signal passes through layers: each neuron computes a weighted sum of inputs, adds a bias, and applies a nonlinear activation function (ReLU, sigmoid, etc.). Training minimizes a loss function via gradient descent; gradients are computed by the backpropagation method — from output to input, using the chain rule of derivatives.

y = \varphi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b\right)
y — neuron output; φ — activation function (e.g., sigmoid); w_i — connection weights; x_i — input signals; b — bias

Open problems & fun facts

Overfitting requiring regularization; Interpretability of decisions — 'black box'; Need for large amounts of labeled data; Catastrophic forgetting during sequential learning; Vulnerability to adversarial attacks

💡 The deepest network as of 2024 is not from architecture but from exploration: in one experiment, a network with 10,000 layers was trained to predict just a single pixel, demonstrating the potential redundancy of depth.
