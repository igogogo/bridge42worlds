spontaneous symmetry breaking

A ball on a hilltop rests in equilibrium, but the slightest breath sends it rolling into one of the valleys. Spontaneous symmetry breaking is when the laws are symmetric, yet the world chooses one of many equally possible states.

In practice: Thanks to this mechanism, particles gain mass, magnets stick to refrigerators, and superconducting magnets work in tomographs.
Spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB) occurs when the ground state (vacuum) of a system is less symmetric than the equations describing it. The classic model is the 'Mexican hat' potential: the energy minimum is not at the center but on a circle of equally possible states. By randomly choosing one, the system generates massless excitations (Goldstone bosons). In gauge theories such as the electroweak theory, these bosons are 'eaten' by fields, becoming their longitudinal components and giving them mass (the Higgs mechanism). In a ferromagnet, rotational symmetry is broken, and the Goldstone bosons are magnons—magnetization waves.

History

The idea was born in the theory of magnetism in the early 20th century, but its real blossoming came in the 1960s when it was applied to quantum field theory. This led to the creation of the Higgs mechanism and the explanation of the masses of W and Z bosons.

How it works

A person balancing on tiptoes—the symmetry is perfect, but a fall inevitably chooses one side. Similarly, atomic magnets in a piece of iron: at high temperature their arrows are disordered and symmetric; upon cooling they suddenly align in a single direction, creating a magnet. The direction is random, but once it appears, it 'freezes' in. In the quantum world, a similar process causes the Higgs field to fill the vacuum and give particles mass.

💡 In the expanding Universe, spontaneous symmetry breaking could have produced cosmic strings—filament-like defects as thin as a proton but millions of light-years long. The search for them continues through light distortions from distant galaxies.
Symmetry in physics means laws remain unchanged under rotation, translation, or particle exchange. But often symmetry disappears in the observable world. A coin on its edge: the situation is symmetric with respect to falling heads or tails, but a slight puff is enough for it to fall and the symmetry to vanish. In physics, such 'spontaneous' disappearance of symmetry is called spontaneous symmetry breaking. It is responsible for magnetism, superconductivity, and even why particles have mass.

Spontaneous symmetry breaking is a phenomenon where the Lagrangian of a system is invariant under the action of a group G, but the vacuum state (lowest-energy state) is invariant only under a subgroup H ⊂ G. According to Goldstone's theorem, each broken generator corresponds to a massless particle (Goldstone boson). The potential of a typical model is given by V(φ) = -μ^2 φ^2 + λ φ^4 with μ^2 > 0; at φ = ±v = ±√(μ^2/λ) the energy is minimal. In quantum field theory, realistic models include complex scalar doublets that break SU(2)×U(1) symmetry down to U(1) of electromagnetism. SSB explains ferromagnetic transition, superfluidity, Bose-Einstein condensation, and mass generation in the Standard Model.

History

The idea of spontaneous symmetry breaking goes back to Werner Heisenberg's theory of ferromagnetism in the 1920s and Lev Landau's theory of phase transitions (1937). In 1961, Yoichiro Nambu and Giovanni Jona-Lasinio built a model of chiral symmetry breaking in hadrons. In 1964, Peter Higgs, as well as François Englert and Robert Brout, showed how to combine SSB with gauge invariance, solving the problem of gauge boson masses. Emmy Noether had earlier in 1918 proved the fundamental connection between symmetries and conservation laws, which formed the basis for understanding broken symmetries.

How it works

Consider the Lagrangian L = (∂_μ Φ)* (∂^μ Φ) - V(Φ) with the potential V(Φ) = -μ^2 |Φ|^2 + λ |Φ|^4. The minima lie on the circle |Φ| = v = √(μ^2/(2λ)). Choosing a specific direction for the vacuum expectation value ⟨Φ⟩ = v breaks the U(1) symmetry. Parameterizing Φ = (v + h) e^{iθ/v} reveals a massive field h (the Higgs particle) and a massless field θ (the Goldstone boson). In the presence of a gauge field A_μ, the covariant derivative D_μ Φ = ∂_μ Φ - i e A_μ Φ leads to the field θ being absorbed, becoming the longitudinal mode of A_μ, which acquires the mass m_A = e v. Thus, like a ball rolling into one of the valleys, the vacuum chooses a specific state, and this choice determines the properties of elementary particles.

V(\phi) = -\mu^2 \phi^2 + \lambda \phi^4
φ — scalar field, μ^2 — positive mass parameter (μ^2 > 0), λ — self-interaction constant (λ > 0). Minima at φ = ±v, where v = √(μ^2/λ).
