Reinforcement Learning

2

A dog gets a treat when it brings back a ball and remembers: doing so is beneficial. Reinforcement learning trains a program exactly the same way — through rewards and misses.

In practice: Game bots (chess, StarCraft), autonomous driving, optimization of factory processes, recommendation systems.
Reinforcement learning (RL) is a branch of machine learning where an agent independently learns optimal behavior through trial and error, interacting with an environment. Unlike supervised learning, there are no ready-made correct answers — only a reward signal, which can be rare and noisy. The agent has to balance between exploring new strategies (maybe they are better?) and exploiting known ones. This exploration–exploitation dilemma is the heart of RL. Modern algorithms, such as Deep Q-Networks and Proximal Policy Optimization, use deep neural networks to process complex visual input and control dozens of actions in parallel.

History

The idea dates back to the mid-20th century when psychologists explained how animals learn through rewards. In the 1980s, algorithms emerged that could play backgammon at a human level, and in the 2010s, programs from Google DeepMind mastered Atari games and beat a Go champion.

How it works

The core scheme: the agent looks at the state, chooses an action, receives a reward. Gradually it builds a table or uses a neural network to remember which moves are more profitable in each situation. For example, in the game 'Snake', turning towards an apple gives points, while colliding with the tail is a penalty. Over time, the snake moves towards the goal almost flawlessly.

💡 During training, AlphaGo Zero beat the version that defeated champion Lee Sedol 100:0 in three days, learning only through self-play, without access to human games.
In reinforcement learning, an agent program lives in its own world (for example, in a crossroads simulator). It sees the surrounding environment — the speed of cars, the traffic light. Its task is to take actions (turn the steering wheel, accelerate) to score as many points as possible. Points are awarded for success — reached the destination; penalties for accidents. The agent tries different moves, like a child learning to walk: fell — it hurts, got up and went on. After thousands of attempts, the strategy becomes ingrained.

Reinforcement learning is formalized as a Markov decision process (MDP), defined by the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ), where S is the state space, A is the action space, P(s'|s, a) is the transition dynamics, R(s, a) is the reward function, and γ ∈ [0,1] is the discount factor for future rewards. The goal is to find a policy π(a|s) that maximizes the expected discounted sum of rewards: J = E[Σ γ^t R_t]. Key concepts: the state value function V(s) = E[Σ γ^t R_t | s0 = s] and the action-value function Q(s, a). Algorithms are divided into value-based (Q-learning, DQN), policy-based (Policy Gradient, REINFORCE), and actor-critic (A2C, PPO) methods that combine both approaches. Deep RL uses neural networks to parameterize V, Q, or π, trained using variations of gradient descent and replay buffers to stabilize learning.

History

The foundations of RL lie in psychological concepts of operant conditioning (Thorndike, Skinner). In AI, the first significant contribution was Minsky's dissertation on neural networks and reinforcement (1954). In the 1980s, Sutton and Barto laid the theoretical groundwork. The public breakthrough came in 2015, when DeepMind demonstrated a system playing 49 Atari games at human level. This was followed by AlphaGo (2016), AlphaZero (2017), and OpenAI Five (2019), proving viability in complex multi-agent environments.

How it works

It is based on Bellman equations. For Q-learning: Q*(s, a) = E[R(s,a) + γ max_a' Q*(s', a')]. The algorithm updates the estimate: Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ max_a' Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)]. In deep Q-learning, the Q-network minimizes the prediction error: L = E[(r + γ max_a' Q(s', a'; θ^-) - Q(s, a; θ))^2] using gradient descent methods, where θ^- are the parameters of a frozen target network. Policy gradient methods directly optimize J(θ) via the gradient: ∇θ J ≈ E[Σ_t ∇θ log π(a_t|s_t) · G_t], where G_t is the total reward. Modern actor-critic PPO limits policy change with a proximal penalty, ensuring stable updates.

Q^*(s, a) = \mathbb{E}\left[ R(s, a) + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s', a') \right]
Q* is the optimal action-value function, s is the state, a is the action, R is the reward, γ is the discount factor (0 ≤ γ ≤ 1)

Open problems & fun facts

The exploration–exploitation dilemma without guarantees of optimal exploration; The problem of generalization to new, unseen states; Efficient learning in environments with very sparse rewards; Safety and predictability when deployed in the real world
