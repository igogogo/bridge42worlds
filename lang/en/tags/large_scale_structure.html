large-scale structure

A soap bubble three hundred million light-years across, its walls glowing with galaxies. The Universe is woven into a colossal foam of filaments and voids, where gravity has been gathering matter for billions of years.

In practice: A map of the cosmic web is a chronicle of the Universe's growth. It tells of dark matter, dark energy, and the first moments after the Big Bang, and also serves as a compass for finding galaxy clusters.
On scales of billions of light-years, the cosmos resembles a cellular network: galaxies, gas, and dark matter are stretched along filaments converging in rich clusters, outlining colossal voids. This 'cosmic web' is the largest known pattern of nature. Its contours are set by the distribution of dark matter, into whose gravitational wells ordinary matter collects. The structure is hierarchical: small halos formed first, then merged into more massive ones. Statistically, it is described by the two-point correlation function and the power spectrum of density fluctuations.

History

Edwin Hubble in the 1920s proved that galaxies are separate islands beyond the Milky Way. In the 1980s, redshift surveys of thousands of galaxies revealed a foamy pattern. Vera Rubin showed that invisible dark matter governs the motion of galaxies and serves as the framework of this network.

How it works

The young Universe can be imagined as an ocean with a gentle swell. Where the density was slightly higher, gravity drew in more matter. Over billions of years, the denser spots turned into galaxies and clusters, while the rarified spots became voids. Today the structure is expanding, but gravity continues to sculpt it according to Einstein's laws (general relativity).

The large-scale structure is the pattern formed by clusters and filaments of galaxies. Inside a void a billion light-years across, you wouldn't see a single galaxy with the naked eye. If you step back billions of light-years, it turns out that galaxies are not scattered randomly but are gathered into a sponge. The dense spots are cosmic cities (clusters), connected by filaments, with voids yawning between them. This foam grew from tiny inhomogeneities in the early Universe.

The large-scale structure (LSS) covers the distribution of matter from tens to thousands of megaparsecs. Characteristic elements include voids, walls, filaments, and galaxy clusters, forming a foamy 'cosmic web'. The statistical measure of LSS is the spatial power spectrum P(k) and the correlation function. The seed perturbations, imprinted in the cosmic microwave background as temperature anisotropies, are amplified by gravitational instability and enter a nonlinear stage. The standard ΛCDM model (cold dark matter + cosmological constant) successfully reproduces the observed structure in numerical simulations. Baryon acoustic oscillations, frozen into the galaxy distribution, serve as a standard ruler for cosmological measurements. The wall of galaxies, the foam of voids—that is the very 'soap film' on the scale of the Universe, a cast of its infant irregularity, stretched by time and dark energy.

History

Hubble in the 1920s established the extragalactic nature of spiral nebulae and the linear law of redshift. The first redshift surveys (CfA, 2dF) in the 1980s–2000s laid bare the foamy morphology. The COBE satellite (1992) detected the primordial fluctuations—the seeds of the future structure. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) covered millions of galaxies, confirming ΛCDM. The contributions of Vera Rubin (rotation curves) and Fritz Zwicky (hidden mass in clusters) pointed to the dominance of dark matter in shaping the LSS.

How it works

Structure growth is governed by gravitational instability in an expanding background. In the linear approximation, the density contrast δ evolves as δ ∝ a in the matter-dominated era. After recombination, baryons fall into the potential wells of dark matter. The power spectrum bears the imprint of baryon acoustic oscillations—frozen sound waves from the primordial plasma. The nonlinear stage is described by the Vlasov-Poisson equations for collisionless dark matter and hydrodynamics for gas, taking into account feedback from star formation and active nuclei. Numerical models reproduce voids, filaments, and hierarchical clustering, creating virtual universes in the computer.

H^2 = H_0^2 \left( \Omega_m a^{-3} + \Omega_\Lambda \right)
H is the Hubble parameter, H_0 is the Hubble constant (~70 km/s/Mpc), a is the scale factor, Ω_m is the relative density of matter, Ω_Λ is the relative density of dark energy
