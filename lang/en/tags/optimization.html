optimization

24

A delivery driver chooses the shortest route among dozens of streets. Optimization is the art of finding the best possible option, be it a route, a schedule, or the shape of a part.

In practice: GPS navigators plot routes, airlines schedule flights, and engineers calculate streamlined shapes — all this is the work of optimization algorithms.
An alpinist sees three ridges ahead, but he needs not the highest, but the lowest pass. Optimization mathematically seeks the minimum (or maximum) of an objective function — a numerical measure of solution quality. Constraints define the feasible region: you can't walk on air or steeper than 45 degrees. Linear programming deals with straight slopes, where the answer lies at a vertex. For smooth hills, gradient descent is used. If the terrain is cut by ravines and cliffs, evolutionary algorithms come into play — they launch a whole crowd of virtual alpinists, and the most successful routes are crossed and mutated. Thus, a path is found in multidimensional spaces where no map can be drawn. When the step becomes smaller than a millimeter, the search stops — the pass is conquered.

History

The first systematic techniques emerged in the 18th century during shipbuilding — how to choose hull lines so that a vessel sails faster under the same wind. Mass application began in the mid-20th century with the rise of computers, when manually sifting through thousands of combinations became impractical.

How it works

A mountain range in the fog. The goal is to find the deepest depression. Standing at one point, you feel the slope and take a step downhill. This is gradient descent — step-by-step movement to where the function value (height) decreases. If a local dip is encountered on the way, the algorithm may get stuck there, never reaching the main valley. For complex landscapes, a 'shake' is added — occasionally jumping up to check if there is a deeper spot nearby. This is how modern global optimization methods work. The fog clears, and the bottom of the main basin is found.

A hanging chain naturally takes the shape that minimizes its potential energy. Similarly, optimization is the search for the state with the smallest value of a given function. Mathematically, it is formulated as a minimization problem of f: Rⁿ → R subject to constraints g_i(x)=0, h_j(x)≤0. Gradient methods use a linear approximation: x_{k+1} = x_k - α_k ∇f(x_k), where α_k is the step size. Convex problems guarantee global convergence. For non-convex problems, stochastic algorithms (simulated annealing, particle swarm) and metaheuristics are used. In unconstrained optimization, quasi-Newton methods (BFGS) approximating the Hessian are popular. Constrained optimization relies on Lagrange multipliers and the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions. In machine learning, stochastic gradient descent with momentum and adaptive step size (Adam) reigns. The chain freezes at the lowest point — the problem is solved.

History

Roots go back to the calculus of variations of the 17th–18th centuries, but the modern era began in the 1940s with the development of linear programming and the simplex method. The advancement of nonlinear optimization in the 1960s brought penalty methods and conjugate gradients. In the 1980s, interior-point algorithms emerged, revolutionizing the solution of large-scale problems. Today, optimization is the foundation of all machine learning technologies.

How it works

Most algorithms construct a sequence of points converging to the minimum. Gradient descent moves against the gradient. In machine learning, the gradient of the loss function is computed on a random subset of data (batch), enabling optimization of billions of parameters. Regularization adds penalties for complexity to avoid overfitting. For constrained problems, methods like the reduced gradient method or sequential quadratic programming are used. Global optimization employs Bayesian methods and search with decision trees.

x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta \nabla f(x_t)
x_t — current point, η — learning rate (step), ∇f(x_t) — gradient of f at x_t (vector of partial derivatives)

Open problems & fun facts

Overcoming local minima in non-convex problems; Computational complexity at large dimensionality (curse of dimensionality); Guaranteed finding of the global optimum within acceptable time; Optimization under uncertainty and noise
