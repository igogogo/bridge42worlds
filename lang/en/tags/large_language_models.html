Large Language Models

10

A stranger thrown into a metropolis without knowing the language eventually learns to understand and respond by listening to conversations around them. Large language models work similarly—they absorb enormous volumes of text and then guess which word should come next.

In practice: Such models write news, compose poems, generate programming code, answer customer questions in chats, and help doctors draft discharge summaries from medical histories.
Modern large language models are neural networks with a 'transformer' architecture, featuring anywhere from hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of parameters. Each parameter is a number that influences how the signal from one word is passed to another. The model doesn't store words but converts them into vectors of numbers—mathematical points in a high-dimensional space of meanings. Pre-training is carried out on an autocomplete task: the model fills in missing words or predicts the next word in a sequence. Afterwards, it can be fine-tuned on specialized examples to hold a dialogue, solve problems, or write code.

History

The first attempts to create computer language models date back to the 1950s, but back then machines lacked memory and data. The real breakthrough came in the 2010s: engineers trained neural networks (programs mimicking the connections of neurons) on the texts of the entire internet. The most famous model, GPT-3 from OpenAI, appeared in 2020 and demonstrated that scale matters a great deal.

How it works

The model works like a game of 'guess the next word.' It receives the beginning of a sentence and proposes dozens of possible continuations, estimating their probabilities. These probabilities are derived from the statistics of the texts it has read. Training is like tuning thousands of radio receivers: each parameter is adjusted to make the prediction more accurate. Inside the model, there are no grammar rules—only numerical weights that have self-organized into something resembling linguistic structures.

💡 GPT-3 can write an essay indistinguishable from a human-written one, as judged by a committee, in 2 seconds—the time it takes a person just to open a text editor.
A large language model (LLM) is a program that learns language by reading billions of web pages, books, and articles. It doesn't understand meaning like a human but finds clever patterns in word combinations. Imagine a foreigner who has memorized a huge volume of phrases and each time, upon hearing the beginning, retrieves the most likely continuation from memory. That's how the model predicts word by word and constructs coherent text.

Large language models (LLMs) are probabilistic autoregressive models that operate on discrete sequences of tokens (subwords or characters). They are based on the transformer architecture, which uses the multi-head attention mechanism for weighted context aggregation. A key feature is scaling: increasing the number of parameters, the dimensionality of hidden representations, and the training corpus size leads to the emergence of qualitatively new abilities (in-context learning, reasoning without fine-tuning). Examples: GPT-4 (OpenAI), Llama (Meta), PaLM (Google).

History

The history began with statistical n-gram models (1950s), then neural network language models (Bengio et al., 2000s). The turning point was the paper Attention Is All You Need (2017), which proposed the transformer architecture. In 2018, OpenAI released GPT-1 with 117 million parameters, and in 2020, GPT-3 with 175 billion parameters demonstrated that a model without explicit training could translate, program, and reason simply by receiving instructions in natural language. Since then, the race has focused on both scaling up models and improving efficiency (Mixture-of-Experts, Chinchilla scaling laws).

How it works

The model receives a context window of n tokens as input. Each token is mapped to an embedding and encoded by self-attention layers, creating contextualized representations. At the output, a linear classifier predicts the probability distribution of the next token. Training minimizes the cross-entropy between the prediction and the true token. During generation, text is produced by sequential sampling of tokens (usually with top-p or top-k nucleus sampling). Key techniques: pre-training on unlabeled text, then instruction-based or RLHF fine-tuning to align with usefulness and safety. The attention mechanism computes similarity weights between queries and keys for each pair of tokens, allowing it to focus on long-range dependencies.

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
Q is the query matrix, K is the key matrix, V is the value matrix, d_k is the dimensionality of the keys, softmax is a function that converts numbers into probabilities. The output is a weighted sum of values, where the weights are determined by the similarity of queries and keys.
