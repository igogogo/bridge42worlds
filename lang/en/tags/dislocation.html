Tag

dislocation

Atomic railway with a switch. The train pushes one car — and the entire line shifts thousands of times easier than dragging the whole train at once.

In practice: Forging metal, rolling steel, and the strength of alloys rely on the skillful management of dislocations — without them, an aluminum fuselage would crack rather than bend.
The crystal lattice is a three-dimensional pattern of atoms. A dislocation disrupts this pattern: it is the edge of an incomplete atomic plane wedged between full layers. Around the dislocation line, the crystal is strained. This stress allows the dislocation to move under load, rearranging atomic rows step by step. Thus the metal flows rather than cracks. In fact, any piece of metal contains billions of dislocations per square centimeter; their tangled movement is plasticity.

History

The idea emerged in 1934 when three scientists — Taylor, Orowan, and Polanyi — independently realized that real crystals bend much more easily than the theory of an ideal lattice predicted. Later, in 1956, Peter Hirsch and colleagues first observed dislocations in an electron microscope.

How it works

Imagine moving a heavy carpet across the floor: dragging the whole thing is difficult, but creating a fold and pushing it along is easy. A dislocation works like that fold. Atoms rearrange bonds not all at once, but locally along the dislocation line. The resistance drops by orders of magnitude.

💡 The yellow color of gold is partly due to dislocations, which affect light absorption. Without them, gold might be silvery, like most metals.
The interior of a crystal resembles perfectly stacked rows of bricks. A dislocation is an extra half-plane of atoms inserted between rows, as if an unfinished row appeared in a wall. The edge of this extra plane — the break line — is the dislocation. Under load, it slides between adjacent rows, switching atomic bonds one by one. The entire crystal deforms gradually, rather than breaking immediately.

A dislocation is a linear defect of the crystal lattice that determines the mechanisms of plastic deformation. There are edge (extra atomic half-plane) and screw (spiral distortion) dislocations. The Burgers vector b characterizes the magnitude and direction of distortion. The stress field around a dislocation extends over several interatomic distances and interacts with other defects. The motion of dislocations (glide and climb) controls yielding, hardening, and fracture of materials. The density of dislocations in annealed metal is ~10⁶–10⁸ cm⁻², increasing to 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻² after severe deformation. The interaction of dislocations with impurities, precipitates, and grain boundaries underlies all methods of alloy strengthening.

History

The concept of dislocations was introduced in 1934 by Geoffrey Taylor, Egon Orowan, and Michael Polanyi to resolve the paradox of the low strength of real crystals compared to theoretical estimates. In the 1940s, Jacques Friedel developed the theory of dislocation networks. Direct observation of dislocations in a transmission electron microscope was achieved by Peter Hirsch and colleagues in 1956. Subsequently, William Lawrence Bragg contributed to the understanding of dislocation structure using X-ray diffraction.

How it works

Under shear stress, a dislocation moves in the slip plane, sequentially breaking and reforming interatomic bonds. The Peierls–Nabarro stress determines the lattice resistance to motion. Schmid's law relates the critical resolved shear stress to the plane orientation. When dislocations meet, they can interact elastically, form Lomer–Cottrell barriers, or annihilate. The Orowan equation relates the plastic strain rate to the density of mobile dislocations, their velocity, and the Burgers vector: dε/dt = ρ_m b v. With increasing temperature, dislocation climb becomes possible through vacancy diffusion, which is essential for creep.

\dot{\epsilon} = \rho_m b v
ε̇ — plastic strain rate; ρ_m — density of mobile dislocations (number of dislocations per unit area); b — magnitude of the Burgers vector (shear amount); v — average dislocation velocity.
