RNA

1

RNA is a working copy of instructions from DNA, a single-stranded molecule that carries information and participates in protein synthesis.

In practice: RNA-based technologies, such as mRNA vaccines, enable rapid development of protection against new viruses.
RNA is a long chain (polymer) of nucleotides (building blocks). It is usually single-stranded, and uses ribose as its sugar (instead of deoxyribose, as in DNA), and contains uracil instead of thymine. RNA types differ by role: messenger (mRNA) — carries instructions from DNA to ribosomes (cellular protein synthesis machines); transfer (tRNA) — delivers amino acids for protein assembly; ribosomal (rRNA) — forms the ribosomes themselves. There are also regulatory RNAs (e.g., microRNAs that fine-tune protein production) and catalytic RNAs (ribozymes capable of accelerating chemical reactions).

History

RNA was discovered in 1961 when scientists realized that a messenger was needed to transfer information from DNA. Later it was found that some viruses store genetic information as RNA.

How it works

When a cell needs a protein, a copy in the form of mRNA is read from the required section of DNA. This mRNA leaves the nucleus (the cell's DNA storage) into the cytoplasm (the cell's interior), where the ribosome (protein factory) assembles the protein by attaching amino acids brought by tRNA.

💡 Some viruses, such as HIV or SARS-CoV-2, use RNA as their genetic material, and their variability is due to the high error rate (mutations) during RNA copying.
RNA is like a tape with written text copied from DNA. It uses the same base letters, but U (uracil) instead of T (thymine). It is more flexible and short-lived than DNA. There are different types of RNA: messenger (mRNA) — carries the instructions; transfer (tRNA) — brings amino acids; ribosomal (rRNA) — builds the protein.

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) is a linear polymer consisting of ribonucleotides linked by phosphodiester bonds. Each nucleotide includes ribose, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base: adenine (A), uracil (U), guanine (G), cytosine (C). Unlike DNA, RNA is usually single-stranded and can adopt complex three-dimensional structures, which determines its catalytic properties. Major types: messenger RNA (mRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), as well as regulatory (microRNAs, small interfering RNAs) and catalytic (ribozymes). RNA synthesis (transcription) is carried out by RNA polymerases. In eukaryotes, mRNA undergoes processing: capping, polyadenylation, and splicing. RNA can serve as the genome in many viruses (retroviruses, coronaviruses). The RNA world theory suggests that RNA was the first molecule capable of storing information and catalyzing chemical reactions.

History

RNA was first isolated in 1868 by Friedrich Miescher, but its role remained unclear for a long time. In 1939, it was shown that RNA participates in protein synthesis. In 1961, mRNA was discovered, and in 1965, the structure of tRNA was deciphered. In the 1980s, the catalytic properties of RNA (ribozymes) were discovered, strengthening the RNA world hypothesis. With the discovery of RNA interference in the 1990s, the development of RNA-based technologies began, including mRNA vaccines.

How it works

RNA is synthesized in the nucleus during transcription: RNA polymerase unwinds a DNA segment and builds a complementary RNA strand. In eukaryotes, the primary pre-mRNA transcript undergoes splicing (removal of introns) and modifications. Mature mRNA is exported to the cytoplasm, where it binds to a ribosome. The ribosome—a complex of rRNA and proteins—moves along the mRNA and, with the help of tRNAs carrying amino acids, synthesizes a polypeptide chain. Each tRNA has an anticodon complementary to an mRNA codon. The process is regulated by various factors, and small regulatory RNAs can suppress gene expression by degrading mRNA or blocking translation.
