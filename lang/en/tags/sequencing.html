sequencing

1

Reading the genetic code letter by letter, like deciphering a cipher written with DNA molecules.

In practice: Medical diagnostics of hereditary diseases, creation of personalized medicine, tracking virus evolution, forensics.
Sequencing is the determination of the nucleotide sequence of DNA or RNA. Modern methods include Sanger sequencing (with dideoxynucleotides and capillary electrophoresis) and high-throughput technologies (NGS), such as Illumina (synthesis with reversible terminators), pyrosequencing, and nanopore sequencing. The principle is based on base complementarity and detection of a signal from the incorporation of each nucleotide into the growing chain.

History

Sequencing methods began to be developed in the 1970s. Frederick Sanger invented a method for which he received the Nobel Prize—he used termination of DNA synthesis at random points, and then reconstructed the sequence from the lengths of the fragments. Later, faster technologies emerged—so-called next-generation sequencing.

How it works

DNA is like a book written in invisible ink. The sequencer 'develops' the text: it copies the fragment many times, and then reads, letter by letter, the signal given by each added letter—for example, a flash of a certain color.

💡 The first complete sequencing of the human genome took 13 years and cost nearly $$3 billion; now it is done in a day and for less than $$1000.
Sequencing is a way to find out the order of 'letters' in a DNA or RNA molecule. There are only four letters: A, T, G, C (adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine). Imagine a very long string of beads of four colors—sequencing allows you to record the sequence of colors. Today, special machines do this, and they can read an entire human genome in a day.

Sequencing is a set of methods for determining the linear sequence of nucleotides in a polymer nucleic acid molecule. It includes fragment library preparation, amplification (PCR or clonal on a solid phase), synthesis of the complementary strand with detection of each step. The Sanger method uses dideoxynucleotide triphosphates that terminate synthesis; separation of fragments by length gives the sequence. NGS platforms (Illumina, Ion Torrent, Pacific Biosciences, Oxford Nanopore) differ in chemistry and type of detected signal—fluorescence, pH, electric current. Bioinformatics processing assembles short reads into contigs and aligns them to a reference genome.

History

In 1977, Sanger proposed an enzymatic method for DNA sequencing using dideoxy analogs; simultaneously, Maxam and Gilbert developed a chemical method. In the 1990s, automation with capillary electrophoresis accelerated the process. The breakthrough was the advent of NGS in the 2000s (454, Solexa/Illumina), which allowed parallel sequencing of millions of fragments. The latest technologies (PacBio, Nanopore) read single molecules in real time.

How it works

General scheme: DNA is fragmented, adapters are ligated to the ends. In Illumina technology, fragments hybridize on a flow cell and are amplified by bridge PCR, forming clusters. Then sequencing-by-synthesis occurs: reversible terminators with fluorophores are added, after each cycle an image is taken, the terminator is removed, and the cycle repeats. Image analysis converts signal intensity into a nucleotide sequence. In nanopore sequencing (Oxford Nanopore), a DNA strand is passed through a protein pore and the change in ionic current, specific for each nucleotide, is measured.

Open problems & fun facts

How to make sequencing cheaper and faster for routine use at the point of care?; How to improve reading accuracy in regions with repeats and modifications?; How to directly sequence RNA and proteins at molecular resolution?
