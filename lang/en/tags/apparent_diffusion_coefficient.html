apparent diffusion coefficient

1

The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) is a measure of how easily water molecules move in tissues. Like trying to walk through a crowd: the denser the crowd, the less your mobility — similarly, water molecules squeezed by cells move less freely.

In practice: In MRI, ADC maps help distinguish cancer (dense cellular structure, reduced diffusion) from healthy tissue or edema, improving diagnostic accuracy without biopsy.
The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) is a quantitative parameter derived from diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI). When DWI is performed with different b-factors (gradient pulse amplitudes), an exponential signal decay curve is obtained. ADC numerically equals the slope of this curve on a semi-log scale and reflects the average diffusion within a voxel (three-dimensional pixel). On ADC maps, areas with restricted diffusion appear hypointense (dark), which correlates with high cellularity of malignant tumors.

History

The method of diffusion-weighted MRI was invented in the 1960s by Edward Stejskal and John Tanner, but it became widely used in medicine in the 1990s thanks to the work of Denis Le Bihan and others. ADC became a standard evaluation parameter.

How it works

The MR scanner takes two images: one without an additional gradient, the second with a strong gradient that 'quenches' the signal from moving molecules. By comparing how much the signal dropped, ADC is calculated. If molecules move freely (as in the bladder), ADC is high. If they are compressed (as in a tumor), the signal persists, and ADC is low.

💡 ADC values help not only to detect cancer but also to predict its aggressiveness: the lower the ADC, the higher the Gleason score on biopsy.
The body contains water, and its molecules are constantly in chaotic motion (called Brownian motion or diffusion). In different tissues, this motion is hindered by various obstacles — cell membranes, proteins. They act like a mesh that traps molecules. Where there are many cells packed tightly (as in a tumor), molecules move more slowly. ADC shows this 'apparent' speed because we measure not the true movement of a single molecule but the averaged effect over the observation time. The lower the ADC, the more restrictions, and the more suspicious the tissue.

ADC — apparent diffusion coefficient, calculated by the formula ADC = -ln(S/S0)/b, where S and S0 are signal intensities with and without the diffusion gradient, b is the weighting factor, proportional to the square of the gradient amplitude and duration. In living tissues, diffusion is non-isotropic and restricted by membranes, so ADC reflects not the true self-diffusion coefficient (D0) but the combined effect of all restrictions on the time scale of the MR experiment (~50 ms). ADC decreases with increased cellular density (tumors), ischemia (cellular edema in stroke), abscesses (viscous pus). It increases in necrosis, cysts, edema. In the prostate, ADC <0.6–0.8×10⁻³ mm²/s is suspicious for cancer. Models that account for non-exponential decay are used: intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) separates true diffusion and perfusion, diffusion kurtosis and tensor imaging describe anisotropy.

History

Diffusion MRI was proposed by Stejskal and Tanner in 1965, using gradient pulses to measure self-diffusion. In 1984, Denis Le Bihan demonstrated its applicability in medicine, obtaining the first diffusion-weighted images of the brain. The term 'apparent diffusion coefficient' emphasized the difference from free diffusion due to tissue barriers. By the 1990s, ADC had become a routine quantitative biomarker in MRI. In the 2000s, the IVIM concept (Le Bihan et al.) allowed separation of the microcirculation contribution. Today, ADC is a key component of multiparametric prostate MRI and brain diagnostics.

How it works

The DWI sequence includes two strong gradient pulses of equal amplitude G, duration δ, separated by interval Δ. The first pulse encodes the phase of spins according to their position, the second refocuses. If a spin has moved, the phase shift is not compensated, and the signal is attenuated. By measuring the signal at a minimum of two b-values (b = γ²G²δ²(Δ-δ/3), where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio), a plot of ln(S) vs b is constructed. The slope of this plot (absolute value) is the ADC. In practice, a monoexponential model is used, but due to complex microstructure, deviations may occur. The ADC map is computed pixel-by-pixel after model fitting.

ADC = \frac{-\ln(S/S_0)}{b}
S — MRI signal intensity with diffusion gradient, S0 — signal intensity without diffusion gradient, b — diffusion weighting factor characterizing sensitivity to diffusion, depends on gradient pulse parameters; ADC units — mm²/s (often expressed as 10⁻³ mm²/s).

Open problems & fun facts

Standardization of b-values and calculation methods across manufacturers and centers; Variability of ADC due to physiological parameters (temperature, hydration), motion artifacts, and eddy currents from gradients; Limitation of the monoexponential decay model; Difficulty in differentiating hypointense benign changes (e.g., stromal hyperplasia of the prostate) from cancer; Need for fast sequences that reduce distortions
