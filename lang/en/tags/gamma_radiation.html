Gamma radiation

2

The most energetic kind of light, invisible to the eye, born in the most explosive and hottest points of the Universe — from supernova explosions to black holes.

In practice: In medicine — sterilization of instruments and cancer treatment; in astronomy — the search for dark matter and the study of extreme processes.
Gamma radiation is electromagnetic radiation with a wavelength less than 0.01 nm and photon energy above ~100 keV. It occupies the highest-energy part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Processes generating gamma rays include nuclear reactions, annihilation (mutual destruction) of particles and antiparticles, and the interaction of high-energy particles with magnetic fields. The Earth's atmosphere is opaque to gamma rays, so they are mainly detected by space observatories.

History

Gamma radiation was discovered in 1900 by French physicist Paul Villard, who noticed that radioactive radium emits rays that are not deflected by a magnet and have enormous penetrating power. Later, Ernest Rutherford named them gamma rays.

How it works

Gamma radiation is a stream of tiny light particles (photons) with a very short wavelength. When an atomic nucleus transitions from an excited state to a calm one, it ejects excess energy in the form of a gamma quantum. In space, powerful gamma-ray bursts occur during the collision of neutron stars or the collapse of massive stars.

💡 Gamma-ray bursts are the most powerful explosions in the Universe: in a few seconds they release as much energy as the Sun will emit in its entire life. One such burst, recorded in 2008, was visible to the naked eye despite a distance of 7.5 billion light-years!
Gamma rays are invisible waves, similar to light but much more energetic and dangerous. Their energy is so great that they can pass through thick layers of metal and damage living cells. They are formed when atoms decay (for example, in nuclear explosions) or when hot gas in space is accelerated to unimaginable speeds.

Gamma radiation (γ-radiation) is shortwave electromagnetic radiation with photon energy typically exceeding 100 keV, corresponding to a wavelength shorter than 0.01 nm. It arises from transitions between energy levels of atomic nuclei (nuclear spectroscopy), annihilation of particle-antiparticle pairs (e.g., the electron-positron line at 511 keV), bremsstrahlung of relativistic particles, and in astrophysical accelerators — supernova remnants, accretion disks of black holes, and gamma-ray bursts. On Earth, gamma radiation is obtained using radioisotope sources (Co-60, Cs-137), nuclear reactors, and accelerators. Detection is carried out with scintillation, semiconductor, and Cherenkov detectors.

History

Gamma rays were discovered in 1900 by Paul Villard during the study of radioactive radiation from radium: he noticed a component with extremely high penetrating power, unaffected by magnetic deflection. In 1903, Ernest Rutherford named this radiation gamma rays, by analogy with alpha and beta particles. In the 1920s, Arthur Compton investigated the scattering of gamma quanta on electrons (the Compton effect), confirming the corpuscular properties of radiation. Since the mid-20th century, astronomical observations began: in the 1960s, the Vela satellites detected gamma-ray bursts, and later telescopes such as Compton, Fermi, and Integral discovered thousands of sources.

How it works

Gamma radiation obeys the Planck–Einstein equation: E = hν, linking photon energy to frequency. It is emitted during the relaxation of excited nuclei: a nuclide in an excited state X* → X + γ. In astrophysics, non-thermal mechanisms are common: synchrotron radiation of relativistic electrons in magnetic fields, inverse Compton scattering, where high-energy electrons transfer energy to photons, and bremsstrahlung. For gamma-ray bursts, models have been developed: the collapsar (hypernova) for long bursts and the merger of compact objects for short ones. Interaction of gamma quanta with matter occurs via the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and electron-positron pair production (the latter dominates at energies >1.022 MeV). These processes also determine detection methods.
