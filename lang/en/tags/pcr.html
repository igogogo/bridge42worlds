PCR

Molecular photocopier: allows billions of exact copies of a desired DNA fragment to be made from a tiny sample.

In practice: Foundation of COVID-19 tests, paternity establishment, and criminal detection from a drop of blood.
Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is an enzymatic method of amplification (multiple copying) of DNA in a test tube (in vitro). It allows selectively synthesizing a huge number of copies of a given DNA fragment, using a thermostable DNA polymerase (an enzyme that withstands high temperatures) and temperature cycling. The method revolutionized molecular biology, medicine, and forensics.

History

The method was invented by American biochemist Kary Mullis in 1983. The idea came to him during a nighttime drive, and for this discovery he received the Nobel Prize.

How it works

A DNA sample, special building blocks for constructing new copies (nucleotides), a builder enzyme (polymerase), and short primers that indicate which exact segment to copy are added to a test tube. Then the mixture is repeatedly heated and cooled: during heating the two DNA strands unwind, during cooling the primers attach to the desired sites, and the polymerase completes the missing halves. Each cycle doubles the number of copies.

💡 The sensitivity of PCR is such that theoretically a single molecule of DNA can be detected in a sample. That is why coronavirus tests could detect infection at the earliest stages.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is a method of exponential amplification of specific DNA sequences, based on repeating cycles of denaturation, primer annealing, and elongation using a thermostable DNA polymerase (usually Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus). Each cycle theoretically doubles the amount of target fragments, allowing 2^n copies after n cycles. In practice, efficiency is below 100%, but a billion-fold increase is still achieved. PCR underlies many applications: from sequencing and genotyping to quantitative analysis (qPCR) and pathogen detection.

History

The concept of DNA amplification via repeated replication cycles was proposed by Kary Mullis in 1983 while working at the biotechnology company Cetus Corporation. The idea came to him during an evening drive along a California highway. The first experiments using the Klenow fragment (unstable at high temperatures) were labor-intensive, as the enzyme had to be added after each denaturation cycle. The key breakthrough was the use of thermostable Taq DNA polymerase, isolated from thermophilic bacteria of Yellowstone, which allowed automation of the process. In 1993, Mullis received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Since then, the method has been continuously improved: real-time PCR (qPCR), digital PCR, and other variants have emerged.

How it works

The process consists of three main stages: (1) Denaturation at ~95°C — breaking hydrogen bonds between complementary DNA strands to form single-stranded templates. (2) Primer annealing at ~50–65°C — specific binding of short oligonucleotide primers to complementary sites on the templates. (3) Elongation at ~72°C — thermostable DNA polymerase synthesizes a new DNA strand starting from the 3' end of the primer, using deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs) as building blocks. The cycles are repeated 25–40 times in a thermal cycler. A key parameter is specificity, ensured by primer design and optimization of the annealing temperature. The quantitative version (qPCR) uses fluorescent probes to monitor product accumulation in real time, allowing determination of the initial template quantity.

N = N_0 \cdot 2^n
N — final number of copies, N0 — initial number of templates (original DNA molecules), n — number of amplification cycles (assuming 100% efficiency).

Open problems & fun facts

Increasing accuracy and reducing amplification errors when working with long fragments; Eliminating contamination by products of previous reactions; Developing faster and cheaper methods for point-of-care diagnostics; Integration with other technologies such as CRISPR diagnostics
