zeta function

The zeta function is a mathematical lens that turns scattered prime numbers into a tidy picture. Through it, we see how often twin primes occur.

In practice: Testing the reliability of cryptographic keys, analyzing random processes, quantum chaos — wherever we need to look into the depths of numerical disorder.
The Riemann zeta function is defined for complex s with real part greater than 1 as ζ(s) = ∑ 1/n^s and analytically continues to the entire complex plane except s=1. Its zeros are divided into trivial ones (at negative even integers) and non-trivial ones. The Riemann Hypothesis states that all non-trivial zeros have real part 1/2. The zeta function factors into the Euler product over primes, linking it to number theory. The distribution of its zeros governs deviations in the prime number theorem. A deep connection with matrices: in the 1970s, Hugh Montgomery discovered that the pair correlation of zeros matches that of eigenvalues of random Hermitian matrices, giving rise to the conjecture of a link with quantum chaos.

History

Leonhard Euler in the 18th century noticed that the sum of reciprocal powers of all natural numbers equals the product over primes. Bernhard Riemann in 1859 extended this idea to complex numbers and discovered that all non-trivial zeros probably lie on the critical line with real part ½. This hypothesis remains unproven to this day.

How it works

Imagine a sieve that sifts out composite numbers. The zeta function is like an X-ray of this sieve: each bright streak on the image (a zero of the function) corresponds to a cluster of primes. If the zeros lie strictly on one line, then the primes are distributed as chaotically as possible — as if scattered by a random wind.

💡 The famous millennium problem — proving the Riemann Hypothesis — is directly related to the security of modern cryptosystems, since a proof could accelerate unveiling the distribution of prime numbers.
The zeta function is not just a formula, but a sensitive instrument. If you plug a complex number (a number with an imaginary part, like a shadow) into it, it yields a result that 'senses' prime factors. The most interesting part is its zeros, the points where the value vanishes. The location of these zeros controls how smoothly the primes are distributed.

The Riemann zeta function ζ(s) is a meromorphic function on the complex plane with a single simple pole at s=1. For Re(s)>1 it is given by the absolutely convergent Dirichlet series or the Euler product over primes: ζ(s) = ∏_{p} (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}. Analytic continuation is achieved via the functional equation linking ζ(s) and ζ(1-s) with involvement of the gamma function. Riemann Hypothesis: all non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line Re(s)=1/2. Verified for the first tens of billions of zeros. The distribution of zeros is described by the Montgomery–Odlyzko formula, and their connection with the spectrum of random matrices (universality) points to a possible explanation via quantum chaos. Generalizations: Dirichlet L-functions, Dedekind zeta functions, elliptic curves.

History

Euler in 1737 derived the product over primes, establishing the fundamental connection. Riemann in 1859 introduced analytic continuation and formulated the hypothesis. In the 20th century, Hardy proved the infinitude of zeros on the critical line, Selberg proved that a positive proportion of zeros lie on it. In 1972, Montgomery identified statistical regularities of zeros, and Odlyzko confirmed them numerically on a huge scale.

How it works

The functional equation ζ(s) = 2^s π^{s-1} sin(πs/2) Γ(1-s) ζ(1-s) is symmetric about the critical line. The explicit Riemann–von Mangoldt formula expresses the sum over prime powers in terms of the sum over non-trivial zeros of ζ(s). This means each prime 'contributes' to the oscillations of ζ(s), and each zero modulates the deviations in the distribution of primes from the main term. The density of zeros at height T grows as (T/2π) log(T/2πe). The Prime Number Theorem is equivalent to the absence of zeros on the line Re(s)=1. Through this lens, we see how primes, like keys, fit the locks of cryptography.

\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^s}
s is a complex variable with real part > 1
\zeta(s) = \prod_{p \text{ prime}} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}}
p runs over all primes, representation as an infinite product
\zeta(s) = 2^s \pi^{s-1} \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) \Gamma(1-s) \zeta(1-s)
Γ is the gamma function, relates the values of the zeta function at s and 1-s

Open problems & fun facts
