horizontal gene transfer

4

Bacteria exchange genes like people exchange text messages: quickly, directly, and without kinship. This is how antibiotic resistance can instantly spread among different species.

In practice: Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) specifically explains the rapid emergence of superbugs in hospitals and enables the creation of genetically modified organisms (GMOs) for agriculture.
Horizontal gene transfer is the process of transferring genetic material between cells of the same generation, bypassing vertical inheritance (from parents to offspring). It is widespread among prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) and less common in eukaryotes. It plays a key role in microbial adaptation, especially in acquiring antibiotic resistance and the evolution of virulence.

History

It was first suspected in 1928 when scientist Frederick Griffith, in an experiment with pneumococci, observed the transformation of harmless bacteria into deadly ones. Later, in 1944, Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty proved that it is DNA that is transferred.

How it works

Bacteria use several methods of 'communication': they can capture free DNA from the environment (transformation), receive genes via a carrier virus (transduction), or connect via a tube for direct exchange (conjugation). All this is like different communication channels.

💡 Genes responsible for toxins in many dangerous bacteria (for example, Vibrio cholerae) were originally brought in by horizontal transfer from harmless ancestors or viruses.

Horizontal (lateral) gene transfer (HGT) is the non-reproductive transfer of genetic material between cells, often phylogenetically distant. Molecular mechanisms: natural transformation (uptake and integration of exogenous DNA), conjugation (transfer of plasmids via type IV pili), transduction (by bacteriophages), and gene transfer agents (GTAs) — virus-like particles. In eukaryotes, cases are known involving endocytosis, viral integration, and intercellular contacts. The scale of HGT is enormous: up to 20% of the genome of a typical bacterium may have foreign origin.

History

Griffith's classic experiment (1928) on pneumococcal transformation first demonstrated the possibility of trait transfer between bacteria. In 1944, Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty identified the transforming agent as DNA. Conjugation was described by Lederberg and Tatum in 1946. Transduction was discovered by Norton Zinder and Joshua Lederberg in 1952. With the development of genomics in the 1990s, it became clear that HGT is not an exception but the norm for the microbial world.

How it works

In natural transformation, bacteria in a state of competence bind double-stranded DNA, cleave it, transport a single-stranded fragment inside, and integrate it into the chromosome by homologous recombination. Conjugation is initiated by contact via pili, cleavage of the plasmid at oriT, and transfer of single-stranded DNA to the recipient. Transduction begins with erroneous packaging of a bacterial DNA fragment into the phage capsid during lysogenic induction. Evolutionary consequences include genome mosaicism, rapid spread of adaptive alleles, and conflict with phylogenetic signals.
