Game theory models the interaction of rational agents striving to maximize their payoff function. Games are classified by the number of players, the possibility of agreements (cooperative or non-cooperative), and the sum of payoffs (zero-sum or non-zero-sum). The central concept is the Nash equilibrium: no player can increase their payoff by unilaterally changing their strategy. In dynamic games, the sequence of moves matters: the threat of punishment can deter defection in repeated interactions — this is how cartels and peace treaties work. Experiments show that real people do not always act as perfect egoists, hence the development of behavioral game theory and evolutionary models where strategies are selected according to Darwinian principles. Today, it is a tool in economics, political science, biology, cybersecurity, and the development of artificial intelligence for auctions and negotiations. History

In the mid‑20th century, mathematicians created an apparatus for analyzing conflict and cooperative situations. Key ideas took shape in works published in 1944 and quickly found application in economics.

How it works

Each player evaluates their payoffs under different combinations of moves. Take the 'prisoner's dilemma': two suspects are interrogated separately. If both remain silent — they each get one year in prison; both confess — three years each; one stays silent, the other betrays — the silent one gets ten years, the betrayer goes free. After calculating the options, each sees: betraying is more advantageous, no matter what the other does. In the end, both betray and get three years, even though they could have gotten away with just one. This is a conflict between personal gain and the common good. Mathematics unpacks such paradoxes for any game rules — from auctions to nuclear deterrence.

Formally, a game is defined by a set of players N, for each i ∈ N a strategy set S_i, and a utility function u_i: S → R, where S = Π S_i is the strategy profile. A Nash equilibrium (in pure strategies) is a profile s* such that for all i: u_i(s*_i, s*_{-i}) ≥ u_i(s_i, s*_{-i}) ∀ s_i ∈ S_i. In games with incomplete information, player types and Bayesian equilibrium are introduced. Repeated games allow equilibria that support cooperation through trigger strategies. Nash's theorem guarantees the existence of a mixed equilibrium for finite games. Evolutionary game theory operates with replicator dynamics and evolutionarily stable strategies. Cooperative game theory analyzes payoffs using the core and Shapley values. The ball is out — the chosen strategy cost a point.

History

Modern game theory emerged in the mid‑20th century. The first mathematical theorem was proved in 1928 for antagonistic games. In 1944, a seminal work systematized methods for analyzing strategic interactions and introduced the concept of expected utility. Later, the equilibrium concept was generalized to non‑cooperative games. Evolutionary game theory arose in the 1970s to explain behavior without assuming rationality. Today, this apparatus is one of the cornerstones of microeconomics and behavioral sciences.

How it works

Analysis begins with constructing a payoff matrix or game tree. The solution is sought by eliminating dominated strategies, computing Nash equilibria, and in dynamic games, through backward induction for finite games with perfect information. Mixed equilibria are found by solving a system of nonlinear equations. For repeated games, the discount factor and observability of actions play a key role. Mechanism design (reverse game theory) constructs rules so that self‑interested agents arrive at the desired outcome.

Open problems & fun facts
