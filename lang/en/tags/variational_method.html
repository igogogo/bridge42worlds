Variational Method

3

You estimate the length of a curved rope by replacing it with a broken line of straight segments. This trick helps to arrive at the exact answer when direct calculation is impossible.

In practice: Quantum chemists calculate molecular shapes this way, and engineers determine the optimal rocket trajectory with minimal fuel consumption.
The variational method is a powerful approximation technique widely used in physics and applied mathematics. Its essence is to minimize or maximize a functional — a rule that assigns a number to a function. In quantum mechanics, the functional is usually the average energy. You set up a trial wave function with adjustable parameters and find those parameter values that minimize the energy. According to the variational principle, the obtained energy is never lower than the exact ground state, so by deepening the trial function (increasing the number of parameters), one can approach the truth arbitrarily closely. A similar approach is used in elasticity theory, hydrodynamics, and machine learning, where variational autoencoders learn to compress and reconstruct data.

History

The foundations of the calculus of variations were laid by Euler and Lagrange in the 18th century. In quantum mechanics, the method was introduced in 1928 by the German physicist Walter Ritz, who proposed a way to calculate atomic energies without complex equations.

How it works

A family of possible solutions is chosen, depending on several numbers (parameters). For each set of numbers, a certain quantity (for example, energy) is calculated. From all the options, the one that gives the smallest (or largest) value is selected, and this is considered the best approximation to the true solution.

The variational method is a search for the best approximation when the exact solution is unknown. Imagine you need to measure the area of a whimsical lawn, but you have no formula. You lay a piece of cloth over the lawn and trim it along the contour — the more precisely you cut, the closer the area of the cloth to the lawn's area. Mathematicians do the same: they try different simple shapes (approximations) until they achieve minimal error. In quantum physics, this is how the distribution of electrons in atoms is found: the parameters of a trial wave function are varied until the computed energy drops to the lower limit.

The variational method relies on the calculus of variations — a branch of mathematics that studies extrema of functionals. The main problem: find a function y(x) that yields an extremum of an integral of the form J[y] = ∫ L(x, y, y') dx, where L is the Lagrangian. The necessary condition for an extremum is given by the Euler-Lagrange equation. In quantum mechanics, the method is used for the approximate solution of the stationary Schrödinger equation. The energy functional E[ψ] = ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ / ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ is minimized over trial functions ψ from a chosen parametric family. By the variational principle (Ritz method), the minimum value is an upper bound for the exact ground-state energy. For excited states, an orthogonality condition is applied. Variational approaches underlie the finite element method, the Hartree-Fock self-consistent field in quantum chemistry, as well as variational Bayesian inference in machine learning, where instead of the exact posterior distribution, one seeks the closest one from a chosen family of distributions (usually with a factorized form).

History

The roots of the method go back to the 17th century — the brachistochrone problem solved by Johann Bernoulli. Systematic development of the calculus of variations was achieved in the works of Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange. Walter Ritz in 1908 published a method of trial functions for solving boundary value problems, and in 1928 applied it to the quantum-mechanical helium atom, obtaining energy values close to experimental ones. Later, the method was generalized into a powerful computational tool with the advent of computers. In statistical physics, the variational approach was developed by Richard Feynman.

How it works

For a quantum system, a trial wave function ψ(α; x) with variational parameters α = (α1, α2, ...) is set up. The functional E(α) = ∫ ψ* H ψ dτ / ∫ |ψ|^2 dτ is calculated, where H is the Hamiltonian. Then E(α) is minimized: ∂E/∂α_i = 0. The solution gives optimal α and an energy estimate E0 ≤ E_exact. Convergence is guaranteed by the completeness theorem: if the trial family is dense in the required functional space, then the limit of E_min coincides with the exact energy. In variational Bayesian inference, the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the approximation and the true posterior distribution is minimized.
