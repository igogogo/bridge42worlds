Tag

synapse

A microscopic gap between nerve cells where an electrical signal turns into a chemical one, like a handshake between two partners.

In practice: Synapses are information processing points in the brain; their plasticity underlies learning and memory, and failures lead to neurodegeneration.
A synapse is a specialized structure that ensures signal transmission between neurons. It consists of a presynaptic terminal (where neurotransmitter is packaged in vesicles), a synaptic cleft (about 20–40 nanometers wide), and a postsynaptic membrane with receptors. The arrival of a nerve impulse triggers calcium ion influx, causing exocytosis of vesicles and release of the mediator, which diffuses to the receptors. The strength of a synapse can change — this is called synaptic plasticity.

History

The term 'synapse' was introduced by Charles Sherrington in 1897, combining the Greek words for 'connect' and 'together'. He realized that neurons are not fused but only touch. Later, the structure of the synapse was observed using an electron microscope.

How it works

An electrical impulse runs along the neuron like a wave, reaches the end and says: 'Time to send the package!'. Vesicles with neurotransmitter burst, spilling their contents into the gap. The neighboring neuron catches the molecules, and a signal is born again.

💡 The human brain has about 100 trillion synapses — more than the number of stars in our galaxy.
A synapse is the point of contact between two neurons where signal transmission occurs. There is a small gap that the signal crosses with the help of chemicals (neurotransmitters). Like on a factory conveyor belt: one workshop passes a part to the next, but there is a gap between them, and the part is thrown over by a courier. Excitatory synapses (speed up the receiving neuron) and inhibitory synapses (slow it down) are distinguished.

A synapse is an intercellular contact consisting of a presynaptic terminal, synaptic cleft, and postsynaptic density. Chemical and electrical synapses are distinguished. In chemical synapses, neurotransmitter vesicles undergo a cycle of exo- and endocytosis; the SNARE protein complex plays a key role. Electrical synapses (gap junctions) provide direct electrical communication through connexons. Postsynaptic receptors are divided into ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channels) and metabotropic (G-protein coupled). Long-term potentiation and depression are the main mechanisms of synaptic plasticity.

History

Charles Sherrington introduced the concept of the synapse in 1897, based on reflex studies. Earlier, Ramón y Cajal showed that neurons are individual cells that contact each other. In the 1950s, electron microscopy confirmed the existence of the synaptic cleft. Pharmacological and electrophysiological research in the second half of the 20th century revealed the diversity of mediators and transmission mechanisms.

How it works

When the action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open. Ca²⁺ ions enter the terminal and trigger a cascade: vesicles merge with the membrane via SNARE proteins, releasing their contents into the cleft (quantal secretion). The mediator diffuses and binds to postsynaptic receptors, which opens ion channels, changing the membrane potential. Mediator remnants are removed by enzymes or reuptake. In electrical synapses, current passes directly through connexons. Modulation of synaptic transmission underlies cognitive processes.
