Natural Language Processing

2

You type 'breakfast with...' — and your phone suggests 'you'. It's not guessing; it reads billions of similar phrases. That's natural language processing.

In practice: Voice assistants, text autocomplete, translators, spam filters — all rely on algorithms that understand human speech.
Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a field at the intersection of linguistics and machine learning, where algorithms learn to extract meaning from text and speech. At its core is the representation of words as vectors (numbers describing their meaning), and neural networks analyze sequences, capturing context. Modern models like GPT don't just cycle through rules — they absorb vast amounts of books, articles, dialogues and build a probabilistic picture of language: which word is most likely to appear after this phrase. This is how coherent text, translation, or an answer to a question is born.

History

The first attempts at machine translation began in the 1950s. In 1966, the famous chatbot Eliza appeared, pretending to be a psychotherapist. The real breakthrough came in the 2010s with the development of neural networks: they learned to memorize long sequences of words.

How it works

Text is turned into numbers. Each word is encoded with a long list of digits — its semantic portrait. Words with similar meanings get similar numbers. Then the neural network reads the sentence word by word, guessing the next one. Given the input 'the cat sat on the...', the network recalls: after 'on' often comes something flat. From this, an answer, translation, or command is born.

💡 The largest language model has 'read' more texts than a person could in a thousand lifetimes — about 500 billion words.
Natural language processing is when a computer learns to understand not commands, but ordinary human speech. Imagine you're explaining directions to a foreigner: you simplify, choosing familiar words. Similarly, a program breaks text into pieces, searching for connections between words and meaning. For example, to understand that 'apple' in one sentence is a fruit, and in another it’s a company. It looks at the neighbors of the word: if next to 'ate' — fruit, if 'stock' — business.

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a branch of artificial intelligence concerned with analyzing, understanding, and generating text and speech in human languages. Tasks include tokenization (splitting into words or subwords), parsing, part-of-speech tagging, named entity recognition, sentiment analysis, machine translation, and dialogue systems. Since the 2010s, neural network approaches have dominated: recurrent networks (LSTM), then the Transformer architecture with attention mechanism, enabling parallel text processing and capturing contextual dependencies between distant words. Models like BERT and GPT are pre-trained on unlabeled text corpora using self-supervised learning (predicting missing words or the next word). They are then fine-tuned for specific tasks. Modern large language models (LLMs) demonstrate emergent abilities: by slightly scaling up, they begin solving tasks for which they were not explicitly trained.

History

The idea of machine translation and text analysis dates back to the work of Alan Turing (1950) and the test named after him. In 1954, the Georgetown experiment — the first public machine translation from Russian to English — took place. In the 1960s-1970s, rule-based systems (grammars, dictionaries) dominated, but they broke down on language ambiguity. In the 1980s-1990s, statistical methods based on text corpora emerged. Neural networks sparked a revolution: in 2013, the word2vec model learned to obtain meaningful vector representations of words; in 2014, seq2seq with attention improved translation; and in 2017, the Transformer architecture became the basis for almost all modern NLP systems. The breakthrough of large language models began with BERT (2018) and GPT-3 (2020), showing that with increasing network size and data, a qualitative leap in language understanding occurs.

How it works

The key mechanism of modern NLP models is multi-head attention from the Transformer architecture. The input text is tokenized and converted into embeddings — fixed-size vectors. Then several layers of self-attention compute for each position a weighted sum of all other positions, allowing a word to gather context from the entire sentence. The attention formula: Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / √d_k)V, where Q, K, V are queries, keys, and values derived from the input embeddings. During pre-training, the model learns to predict missing tokens (as in BERT) or the next token (as in GPT), minimizing cross-entropy. After that, for specific applications, it is fine-tuned on a small labeled dataset. The model's output is a probability distribution over the vocabulary, from which the next token is chosen.

\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
Q — query matrix, K — key matrix, V — value matrix, d_k — dimension of keys; softmax turns similarity scores into probabilities

Open problems & fun facts

Understanding true meaning and pragmatics (models often rely on patterns, not reasoning); Eliminating biases and toxicity embedded in training data; Controlled generation of facts without hallucinations (models can invent names, dates, citations); Efficient training without enormous computational costs; Incorporating common sense and causal reasoning
