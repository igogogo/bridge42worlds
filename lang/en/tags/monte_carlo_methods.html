Monte Carlo methods

3

You throw darts in a dark room to estimate the area of furniture by the number of hits. Randomness turns into precise calculation.

In practice: Engineers use them to verify the reliability of bridges, financiers forecast stock prices, and physicists model nuclear reactions.
Monte Carlo methods are a class of computational algorithms based on repeated random sampling. They allow numerical estimation of high-dimensional integrals, solving differential equations, and optimization problems. The key idea: instead of analytical calculation, one resorts to a statistical experiment. Convergence is slow (error decreases as 1/√N) but independent of dimensionality, making the methods indispensable in multidimensional space. In finance, for example, they estimate the value of complex derivative instruments, in biology they model population evolution, and in computer graphics they create realistic scene lighting (ray tracing).

History

The method was invented in the 1940s by scientists at the Los Alamos Laboratory working on the atomic project. Stanislaw Ulam formulated the idea, and John von Neumann connected it with computations. The name was given in honor of the Monte Carlo casino due to the role of chance.

How it works

You set random input parameters for the problem, run the simulation many times, and see how often the result occurs. By aggregating these repetitions, you get an estimate of probabilities or average values that is difficult to compute with formulas.

💡 The most famous example is computing π: by throwing points into a square with an inscribed circle and counting the ratio of those that fall inside the circle to the total, one gets an approximation 4×(inside/total).
Monte Carlo methods are a way to solve complex problems using random numbers. Imagine you need to find the area of a oddly shaped lake. You enclose it with a rectangle whose area you know, then scatter a handful of sand grains over the rectangle. By counting how many grains fell into the lake and knowing the total number of throws, you can roughly calculate its area. The more throws, the more accurate the result. Similarly, a computer 'throws' millions of random points in complex models—from molecular motion to stock market risks.

Monte Carlo (MC) methods encompass a wide range of stochastic modeling techniques. The foundation is the law of large numbers: the arithmetic mean of random variables drawn from a sampling distribution converges to the expected value. To accelerate convergence, variance reduction methods are used (importance sampling, stratified sampling, control variates). In Bayesian inference, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is especially important, where a sequence of samples forming a Markov chain is generated, whose stationary distribution coincides with the target posterior. The most common are the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and Gibbs sampling. MC methods are also applied in quantum chemistry (diffusion Monte Carlo), particle physics (shower simulation), and reliability analysis.

History

The idea goes back to Buffon's needle experiment in the 18th century, but as a computational tool it took shape in the 1940s. Stanislaw Ulam, recovering from an illness, thought about solitaire games and realized that instead of complex combinatorial calculations, one could simply replay a random game many times and estimate the probability of winning. He discussed this with John von Neumann, and they applied the approach to neutron diffusion calculations. In 1953, the seminal paper by N. Metropolis et al. on the sampling algorithm appeared.

How it works

To estimate the integral I = ∫ f(x) dx, one introduces a probability density p(x) and generates N points xi ~ p(x). The approximation: I ≈ (1/N) Σ f(xi)/p(xi). In MCMC, a Markov chain is constructed with a transition kernel that ensures ergodicity and stationarity on the target distribution. For complex distributions, ensemble sampling and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo are used, which utilizes gradients of the log-density.

\langle I \rangle = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{f(x_i)}{p(x_i)}
I is the true value of the integral, N is the number of samples, xi is a random point from distribution p(x), f(x_i) is the integrand function, p(x_i) is the density of the distribution from which the sample is drawn
