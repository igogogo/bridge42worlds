X-ray radiation

2

X-rays are light so energetic that they pass through soft materials but are blocked by dense ones, like a swarm of bullets piercing paper but getting stuck in stone.

In practice: In medicine, it shines through the body to reveal fractures; at airports, it scans luggage without opening.
X-ray radiation is part of the electromagnetic spectrum with wavelengths from 0.01 to 10 nanometers, corresponding to photon energies from ~100 eV to ~100 keV. It penetrates materials more easily the lower their density and atomic number. In astronomy, X-rays are emitted by hot gas with temperatures of millions of degrees, for example in stellar coronae, accretion disks of black holes, and supernova remnants.

History

Discovered in 1895 by German physicist Wilhelm Röntgen when he noticed that unknown rays exposed a photographic plate even through black paper. For this he received the first Nobel Prize in Physics.

How it works

Inside an X-ray tube, electrons are accelerated by high voltage (voltage is the difference in electric potential) and slam into a metal target. The sudden deceleration produces X-ray photons (radiation particles).

💡 X-ray telescopes on satellites capture radiation from neutron stars; one such object can shine in X-rays as brightly as a million suns.
X-ray beams are a type of invisible radiation, similar to light but with much higher energy. While ordinary light can be stopped by a sheet of paper, X-rays pass through skin and muscle but are absorbed by bones. That's why bones appear white on an image. In hospitals, the beam is directed through the body, leaving a shadow on film or a digital detector.

X-ray radiation consists of electromagnetic waves with energies ranging from tens of electronvolts to several hundred keV. It is generated by the deceleration of charged particles (bremsstrahlung) or by electron transitions in inner atomic shells (characteristic radiation). X-ray photons ionize matter and interact via the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and electron-positron pair production (at energies >1.022 MeV). Their high penetrating power makes them indispensable in condensed matter physics, materials science, and medicine.

History

Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen discovered X-rays on November 8, 1895, while experimenting with cathode rays. He noticed the glow of a barium platinocyanide screen even when the tube was enclosed and called the rays unknown (X). In 1901, Röntgen was awarded the Nobel Prize. In 1912, Max von Laue and colleagues demonstrated the wave nature of X-rays through diffraction on crystals, which led to the development of X-ray crystallography. Arthur Compton in 1923 explained the scattering of X-rays by electrons (Compton effect), confirming the particle properties of radiation. William Lawrence Bragg and his father derived Bragg's law, becoming founders of crystallography.

How it works

When fast electrons collide with the anode in an X-ray tube, the sudden deceleration produces bremsstrahlung with a continuous spectrum, bounded by the minimum wavelength λ_min = hc/(eU), where U is the tube voltage. Electrons also eject inner-shell electrons from the anode atoms; when vacancies are filled, quanta are emitted with energies characteristic of the material, giving a line spectrum. According to Bragg's law, X-ray reflection from crystal planes follows the condition nλ = 2d sin θ, which is used for structure analysis. Compton scattering is described by the wavelength shift Δλ = (h/(m_e c))(1 - cos θ).

\lambda_{\text{min}} = \frac{hc}{eU}
λ_min — minimum wavelength, h ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s — Planck constant, c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s — speed of light, e ≈ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C — electron charge, U — accelerating voltage
n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta
n — order of reflection (integer), λ — X-ray wavelength, d — interplanar spacing in the crystal, θ — glancing angle
\Delta\lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c}(1 - \cos\theta)
Δλ — increase in wavelength of scattered radiation, h — Planck constant, m_e ≈ 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg — electron mass, c — speed of light, θ — photon scattering angle
