modified gravity

When the rules of gravity are rewritten at large distances — as if the force of a fall changed not only with height, but with the scale of the entire cliff.

In practice: Description of galaxy rotation without adding invisible mass; an alternative model for dark matter.
Modified gravity — a class of theories proposing to change the law of gravitation on scales of galaxies and clusters, in order to explain the observed rotation without hypothetical dark matter. In standard cosmology, dark matter adds the missing mass; in alternative approaches, the geometry of spacetime or the potential itself is revised. The best-known model is MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics), introducing a threshold acceleration a₀ ≈ 1.2×10⁻¹⁰ m/s², below which the gravitational force transitions to a different regime.

History

In the 1930s, Fritz Zwicky noticed that galaxies in clusters move strangely. Later, in the 1970s, Vera Rubin confirmed this for spiral galaxies. Then physicists (for example, Mordehai Milgrom) proposed changing the very formula of gravity.

How it works

Imagine that the force of gravity at large distances does not behave as 1/distance², but differently — for example, it weakens more slowly. Then stars on the outskirts are attracted more strongly, and their speed is explained without invisible particles. One popular version is MOND, where at very small accelerations the force becomes larger than usual.

💡 MOND accurately predicted the rotation curves of many galaxies, but it has not yet succeeded in explaining cluster collisions, as in the case of the Bullet Cluster.
Ordinary gravity (Newton's law, later refined by Einstein) says that the farther objects are from each other, the weaker they attract. But on the outskirts of galaxies, stars move too fast, as if something is pushing them. Modified gravity is the idea that at very large distances, the law of gravity changes, becoming stronger, so dark matter (invisible substance) is not needed.

Modified gravity theories generalize Einstein's general theory of relativity (GTR) or Newtonian mechanics on large scales to explain the dynamics of galaxies and cosmological acceleration without invoking dark matter and dark energy. Instead of adding unknown forms of matter-energy within GTR, these theories change the gravitational interaction: for example, replacing the inverse-square potential with a potential having a logarithmic or power-law change. MOND (Milgrom, 1983) postulates that at accelerations below the fundamental constant a₀, Newton's second law takes the form F = m μ(a/a₀) a, where μ(x) ≈ 1 for x ≫ 1 and μ(x) ≈ x for x ≪ 1. Relativistic generalizations like TeVeS (Tensor-Vector-Scalar gravity) by Bekenstein include additional fields.

History

The problem of missing mass was first noticed by Zwicky in 1933 in the Coma Cluster: the velocity dispersion of galaxies indicated a mass many times greater than the visible. In the 1970s, Vera Rubin systematically measured flat rotation curves of spiral galaxies, which were traditionally interpreted as evidence of a massive dark halo. In 1983, Mordehai Milgrom proposed MOND, which reproduced these curves without dark matter, introducing a single constant. Jacob Bekenstein in 2004 developed TeVeS — a relativistic extension of MOND, consistent with lensing and cosmology.

How it works

In the Newtonian limit F = G M m / r², but when a ≪ a₀ (typical value for galaxy outskirts) the effective force in MOND becomes F ≈ m a₀ / √(a/a₀) = m √(a₀ a). For a test body at distance r from central mass M, this yields v ≈ (G M a₀)^(1/4) — constant rotation velocity, explaining flat curves. In relativistic versions, the field equations are modified: instead of standard Einstein equations, additional metrics or scalars are introduced. The equivalence principle is usually preserved in the weak field. On galaxy cluster scales, even modified gravity often requires some amount of ordinary dark matter (e.g., sterile neutrinos).

F = m\sqrt{a_0 a_N}
F — gravitational force, m — mass of the test body, a₀ ≈ 1.2×10⁻¹⁰ m/s² — Milgrom constant, a_N = G M / r² — Newtonian acceleration (G ≈ 6.674×10⁻¹¹ m³/(kg·s²) — gravitational constant, M — mass of the gravitating object, r — distance to it)
v = (G M a_0)^{1/4}
v — orbital speed, G — gravitational constant, M — mass of the galaxy inside the radius, a₀ — Milgrom constant

Open problems & fun facts

Explaining all observed phenomena without dark matter (colliding clusters, CMB anisotropy); Creating a complete relativistic theory consistent with GTR and cosmology; Predicting the existence and properties of additional fields and their detection

💡 The constant a₀ is surprisingly close in order of magnitude to c · H₀ / (2π), hinting at a connection between gravity and the expansion of the Universe.
