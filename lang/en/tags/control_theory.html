control theory

6

Control theory helps maintain balance — like a cyclist subtly adjusting the handlebars to stay upright while moving.

In practice: Aircraft autopilots, household temperature control, camera stabilization systems in smartphones.
Control theory is the mathematics of maintaining order. It explains how to make a system do what you want despite wind, friction, and other whims of the real world. An engineer designs an algorithm (a controller) that continuously reads sensor data and doses commands to actuators to drive the difference between desired and actual values to zero.

History

The desire to tame steam and set flywheels in motion gave rise to a key question: how to restrain such unbridled power? Scottish engineer James Watt devised the centrifugal governor in 1788—spinning balls that spread apart as speed increased, throttling the steam supply. This became the first automatic controller.

How it works

Every control system works like the float in a toilet cistern. The float is a level sensor, the lever is a mechanism that compares the level to a setpoint, and the valve is an actuator. As soon as the water drops below the mark, the float sinks, the valve opens slightly, and the flow replenishes the loss. A thermostat, autopilot, or robotic arm works the same way.

Control theory studies the behavior of dynamical systems and synthesizes controllers to achieve a desired goal. It is based on mathematical descriptions of objects in the form of differential or difference equations. It distinguishes between classical control (PID controllers, frequency-domain methods) and modern control (state-space, optimal, robust, adaptive). Key concepts: observability, controllability, Lyapunov stability, poles and zeros of the transfer function.

History

The roots of the theory go back to Watt's governor (1788). James Clerk Maxwell laid the mathematical foundation in his paper 'On Governors' (1868), where he first analyzed the stability of a feedback system. In 1892, Russian-born Aleksandr Lyapunov introduced a rigorous definition of stability. The mid-twentieth century saw a flourishing of the field with contributions from Harry Nyquist, Hendrik Bode, Rudolf Kalman (for filtering and control problems), and Lev Semyonovich Pontryagin (the maximum principle). Today, control methods permeate aerospace, robotics, economics, and biology.

How it works

The basic feedback loop: a sensor measures the current value y(t), a summing junction computes the error e(t) = r(t) – y(t) (where r(t) is the setpoint), and the controller produces a control input u(t) based on the error. The system's response is described by dynamical equations. For example, in a PID controller, u(t) = K_p e + K_i ∫ e dt + K_d de/dt. The proportional term responds to the current error, the integral term eliminates steady-state error, and the derivative term anticipates future deviations. For complex multivariable systems, the state equation is used: dx/dt = A x + B u, y = C x + D u, and controller synthesis often reduces to solving algebraic Riccati equations.

u(t) — control signal; e(t) — error (difference between setpoint and actual value); K_p — proportional gain; K_i — integral gain; K_d — derivative gain; t — time; τ — integration variable.
\dot{x}(t) = A x(t) + B u(t)
x(t) — state vector of the system (e.g., position and velocity); u(t) — control vector; A — system matrix, defining its internal dynamics; B — input matrix, showing how controls affect the state; the dot over x denotes the time derivative.
