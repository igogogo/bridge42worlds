EHT

A telescope the size of Earth, assembled from antennas on different continents, captured the shadow of a black hole — like a bat's silhouette against the sunset.

In practice: Direct images of the event horizon and testing gravity theories under extreme conditions.
EHT uses the technique of Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI). Eight observatories, from the South Pole to Greenland, are synchronized by atomic clocks, recording signals at a frequency of 230 GHz. This frequency corresponds to a wavelength of 1.3 mm – the submillimeter range where the superheated plasma near the black hole radiates. After years of processing on supercomputers, signal correlation achieved an angular resolution of 20 microarcseconds – equivalent to reading a newspaper from 500 km away.

History

The idea of linking distant radio telescopes emerged back in the 1960s, but data processing techniques only became available by the 2000s. The decisive step was taken by the team led by Sheperd Doeleman, publishing the first image in 2019.

How it works

Each pair of telescopes acts as an interferometer: waves arrive at different times due to Earth's rotation, and from the phase difference, the brightness of each point in the sky is reconstructed. The process resembles reconstructing a stereoscopic image — the brain builds a 3D picture from two flat images, but here thousands of pairwise combinations yield a detail a million times finer than the human eye.

💡 The volume of data collected in one observing campaign exceeds the total daily Internet traffic; it was transported on hard drives by airplanes because network transmission would have taken years.
EHT is not a single instrument but a network of radio dishes across the planet. They simultaneously record signals from the same cosmic target, and then computers combine the data as if the antennas were a single giant mirror 10,000 kilometers in diameter. The image is assembled piece by piece, like a puzzle of a billion fragments.

EHT is a global network of millimeter-wave radio telescopes operating on the principle of Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI). Observations, starting in 2017, involve instruments such as ALMA, APEX, the IRAM 30-meter telescope, JCMT, SMA, SMT, SPT, and the Greenland Telescope. Joint synchronous recording of signals at frequencies around 230 GHz (wavelength 1.3 mm) and subsequent correlation processing of the data array enable image synthesis with an angular resolution determined by the maximum distance between telescopes (baseline) of about 10,000 km, yielding a diffraction limit of approximately 20 microarcseconds. This visual acuity is sufficient to discern the shadow of the black hole at the center of galaxy M87 or Sagittarius A* in the Milky Way. For the first time, it was possible to directly see the photon ring and event horizon, confirming the predictions of general relativity for the shadow shape under the Kerr metric.

History

The concept of combining radio telescopes for interferometry dates back to the 1960s, when the first VLBI experiments were conducted. By the 1990s, advances in millimeter-wave receivers made it possible to consider observing compact sources such as Sgr A*. The EHT project began to take shape around 2009, and the first full observations were conducted in April 2017. After two years of data processing, the collaboration, involving more than 200 researchers from different countries, presented the image of the black hole shadow in M87 in 2019. In 2022, the image of Sgr A* – the black hole at the center of our Galaxy – was published.

How it works

The telescopes record the amplitude and phase of incoming radio waves. Pairwise correlation of signals (product with delay) yields complex visibilities. Then image reconstruction algorithms, such as CLEAN or regularized maximum likelihood methods, translate the visibilities into a brightness map. The interferometer's resolution is given by the diffraction formula θ ≈ λ / D. With λ = 1.3 mm and D ≈ 10,000 km, we obtain θ ~ 20 μas (microarcseconds). The key challenge is achieving sub-nanosecond accuracy synchronization with hydrogen masers and accounting for atmospheric fluctuations. Due to Earth's rotation, the relative positions of the telescopes change, filling the UV-plane and improving image quality.

\theta \approx \frac{\lambda}{D}
θ — angular resolution (rad), λ — wavelength (1.3 mm), D — maximum distance between telescopes (baseline), D ≈ 10,000 km

Open problems & fun facts
